OverviewThe Story

Iris Vale recovers data from places nobody else will go: sealed buildings, dead servers, infrastructure that has been switched off and forgotten. Her client, a former logistics coordinator named Daniel Kade, sends her into the abandoned Meridian Neurological facility on the coast of Lowport, Maine, to pull files off the drives of a biotech company that shut down suddenly and not cleanly. The job is routine. She goes in through a cut fence, works under every third light in the server room, and comes back out with the data. What she does not come back with is thirty-four minutes of her own afternoon. On the drive home she finds a photo of her younger sister, Lena, in the cup holder. She knows the face and she knows who it is, and she feels nothing at all.

Something in that building touched her and took one thing: her love for her sister. Not her memory of Lena and not her ability to function, just the feeling. As Iris goes back to work she starts to see the same absence in other people. A dock worker came home after two missing days and answers every question correctly and with nothing behind it. A woman named Lila Mercer is calm, polite and completely hollow. Lowport's behavioral-health chief, Dr. Elena Rourke, has hundreds of these cases filed as a treatable neurological condition, and she has no patience for Iris's idea that they are connected. But when Iris maps the cases across time, they are not scattered. They move in a line, and the line runs along the old Meridian infrastructure that Kade himself once helped build.

Iris keeps getting worse while the city goes quiet around her. Her responses come late, her certainties turn out to be false, and she starts performing emotions she can no longer feel, first for her sister and then for her friend Caleb Ward. Caleb is the most alive person she knows, and at the midpoint of the book he is Taken too. He comes back reasonable, efficient and gone. Rebuilding her evidence from scratch, Iris reaches the conclusion she has been circling: this is not a disease or contamination. It is one thing, and it is choosing. Her own damage turns out to work as a compass, because the closer she gets to it the more she loses, so she follows that loss alone into the drowned tunnels at the edge of the city.

There she finally sees it, the thing the series calls Willow. It is a figure that is nearly human, with a face assembled from what it has taken, and it recognizes her. There is no attack and no chase. It reaches out, touches her, and takes a little more. Then, for reasons neither Iris nor the reader can name, it lets her go. Iris walks back into Lowport and sits in a familiar café. A message from Lena arrives and produces nothing, and she understands that she is now closer to Willow than anyone else alive and further from herself than she has ever been. She knows she should be afraid. She waits for the fear, and it never comes.

One-line pitch A data-recovery specialist survives contact with something inside a sealed biotech lab. It took her love for her sister, and now it is moving through the city taking pieces of everyone else.

Who's WhoCast

In detail

Iris Vale

Protagonist · data recovery specialist

A systems-recovery tech, one of the few people who will take jobs inside sealed sites. She is known for getting in, getting the data out and not asking questions. After Meridian she is the only known survivor of direct contact with Willow, and the one thing taken from her is her love for her younger sister Lena. She still remembers Lena and knows she should care. She doesn't.

There is collateral damage too: delayed emotional responses, unreliable decisions, memory gaps and false certainties. She catalogs her own losses like system logs ("Expected: frustration. Observed: none."). Her drive is not revenge or justice but understanding. Because the Taking was interrupted, she can sense Willow nearby as pressure in her head, and that makes her the only person who can track it. Every step closer costs her more.

She is controlled, observant and dry on the surface, and constantly monitoring herself underneath. Before the incident she was protective, impatient, funny and sharp, but the reader only ever sees that version in what other people expect of her and don't get.

Willow

The entity · antagonist

The thing Iris meets inside Meridian. It is elongated and almost human, with a face of "features learned from observation rather than born to them." It moves with no wasted motion.

It takes by touch. A hand on the chest, a flash of a person's most charged memories, then the feeling is stripped off them and the memory follows. Up close, it gives off a calming field that dulls sound and slows reactions. Nothing it does looks cruel. It is silent for the whole of Book 1.

Lena Vale

Iris's younger sister

She is warm, direct and persistent. She refuses to accept that her sister has changed and keeps fighting for the relationship. In the café scene (Ch10) she tells Iris, "You sound like you're managing a problem," and Iris says, "I am." Lena: "You are the problem." She is the emotional core of the book because she is exactly what is missing. Love only flows in one direction now.

"I love you." / "I love you." The words matched. Tone aligned. No delay.

Caleb Ward

Field tech · the reader's anchor

An independent tech runner Iris has worked with for years. He is sharp, funny, conversational, a little reckless, and one of the few people who knew her before. He notices she has changed and tells her so. Ch12, the midpoint, is his POV, the only chapter in the book told from his point of view, and it is the on-page Taking. Afterward he functions perfectly and he is not Caleb anymore.

"If something's off, you can just say that. You don't have to… optimize the answer."

Daniel Kade

Client · handler · ally

A former Meridian logistics and site coordinator, not a scientist. He hired Iris for the job (ref MN-44-7) and keeps feeding her the adjacent sites. He is practical and controlled, and he doesn't speculate. His guilt is logistical rather than dramatic: he moved crates and data units to locations he never asked about. His Meridian access codes still work.

"I moved things through here. Crates. Equipment. Data units. Didn't ask what was in them."

Dr. Elena Rourke

Behavioral health authority · the skeptic

Lowport's medical authority on the rising cases. She is analytical, precise and competent, with hundreds of cases under one classification ("Behavioral flattening. Dissociative presentation. Emotional attenuation.") and no full recoveries. She is right about the symptoms and wrong about the cause. She isn't a villain, just a wall. She hands Iris redacted case files anyway, pragmatically, and notices Iris presenting with the same markers.

"They're moving." / "They're not moving. They're appearing."

Lila Mercer

The first fully Taken person

The first fully Taken person Iris meets face to face (Ch4). She is calm, present and polite, and completely hollow. She sets the tone for the whole book: no drama, only absence. Iris's body reacts even though her emotions can't.

"Do you feel like anything is missing?" / "No."

Joel & Leah

The first case (K-01)

Joel is a north-side dock and logistics worker who was gone for two days and came back "fine." His answers are correct and empty: "I know what is necessary." His wife Leah is frightened and confused, a smaller mirror of what Lena is living with. His case leads to Iris's report to Kade: "Something has been removed."

SettingThe World

Lowport, Maine, around 2050

Lowport is a fictional coastal industrial city that is past its peak but still running unevenly: shipyards, container terminals, water treatment, and old drainage and transit tunnels under everything. It is damp, foggy and salt-corroded, and always slightly cold. The technology is near-future and believable: smart contacts, ambient AI assistants, predictive systems. If you walked down the street it would still feel like our world.

Meridian Neurological

A sealed research building on the Lowport coast, "more bunker than clinic," with MERIDIAN NEUROLOGICAL RESEARCH still fading over the door. Book 1 never says what was done inside it. Meridian spread its sites deliberately across Lowport (primary facility, secondary storage, data mirrors, relay and transfer points) for "risk management." In Kade's words, it was protecting itself "From themselves." And it "didn't shut down clean."

RefresherChapter by Chapter

Act I · Discovery

Iris and her condition, the pattern, and the realization that it's something external.

1
The Gap

Iris enters the sealed Meridian facility through a cut fence and a dead-keypad side door. The Taking is shown only in fragments: a kitchen at nineteen, a yellow mug, a hospital corridor, a red scarf on a winter beach, two girls on a staircase, a pale hand. Her clock jumps from 14:07 to 14:41. In the van she finds Lena's photo and feels nothing.

2
Caleb Ward

A two-person job at Harbor Data Storage, then a diner. Caleb is funny, alive and probing ("You seem different"). Iris watches him laugh and waits for her own response. It never comes.

3
The First Case

Kade flags case K-01: Joel, gone two days, back "fine." His wife Leah is frightened. Iris feels the first faint pressure of her proximity sense and reports: "Something has been removed."

4
Lila Mercer

Iris's first face-to-face meeting with a fully Taken person. Lila is perfectly polite and has nothing behind it. Iris's body tightens while her feelings don't. "I hope you have a good day."

5
Kade

Kade's office has layered maps on the walls and boxes of site codes. Iris admits to the 34-minute gap. Kade reveals that Meridian had many sites and gives her six to check: "Access, retrieval, no questions that get written down." He notices she would have pushed harder before. Her verdict: "It's moving."

6
Rourke

Dr. Rourke has hundreds of cases, targeted neural changes and no recoveries. She rejects Iris's theory, spots the same symptoms in Iris, and hands her the case files anyway. Outside, Iris feels nothing: "Expected: frustration. Observed: none."

7
The Pattern Act I turn

Iris maps the cases over time and finds a directional path rather than a cluster, running along Meridian-era infrastructure. Every transcript has the same shape: "Question → Answer → Stop." Alone, she says it out loud: "It's not a condition. It's not environmental. Something is moving. And it touches people."

Act II · Escalation

The threat gets personal and irreversible. Caleb is Taken. Iris starts to come apart.

8
Following the Thread

Kade and Iris visit three sites. His access codes still work. He recognizes the relay and transfer points from his own logistics work and starts to confess, then stops. Iris notices he has stopped and doesn't care enough to ask.

9
The City Feels It

A wide-lens chapter about Lowport flattening: conversations that end without ending, and a mother and child who both watch a dropped toy and neither reaches for it. Iris's own delays get worse, and she can't tell whether the change is in the city or in her.

10
Lena

A café. Lena pushes ("You used to argue with me"), and Iris performs care from a checklist. They end on "I love you" and "I love you." Lena is almost convinced, then she isn't.

11
Caleb Shift

Caleb on a job site. He is still Caleb, but his pauses run a fraction long and a joke lands without its reaction. "You ever get the sense something's already happened and we're just catching up to it?" Iris thinks he was the most alive person she knew and corrects the tense.

12
The Taking of Caleb Ward Midpoint

Told from Caleb's POV. She is simply there at the end of a corridor. A flat hand on his chest. His memories surface one by one and each is stripped of its warmth: running as a kid, a coffee someone handed him unasked, an improvised fix. The last thing to go is "the sense that things mattered." He logs a routine note and drives home.

13
After

A bar. Caleb answers everything cleanly and correctly and doesn't remember his own joke. Asked what he'd do if everything went wrong at once, he gives a textbook answer. "The difference between them was not direction. Only distance."

14
The Weight

"He is complete." "That's not better." Rourke has 63 new cases, and her answers have tightened. Kade asks whether it bothers her and Iris says "Yes," with perfect tone, then realizes she was performing grief. She can't tell whether she stopped because it was dishonest or because she forgot to keep going.

15
Deterioration

False certainties: a case she is sure she marked, a site she is sure she visited, a conversation Kade swears she had. A contact is burned. She leaves herself a note, "Do not trust sequence," and later can't follow her own reasoning. "Certain. Incorrect. Continuing."

16
The Realization Act II turn

Iris rebuilds the map from verified records only, and one clean line remains. "It's one." Her own symptoms fit the path too, and she doesn't say so. Kade: "We're tracking something that's still happening." It doesn't feel like a breakthrough. "The door had closed."

Act III · Contact

Iris follows her own damage to the thing that caused it.

17
Tracking

Past the edge of the map. Kade tells her not to go alone and she goes anyway. "She was measuring proximity through loss." She isn't afraid and notes that she isn't.

18
The Deeper Zone

Empty warehouses, a patch of floor still warm where someone stood, tunnels with standing water. Inside the field her symptoms ease: calm, relief, no need to monitor anything. She could stay there forever. She makes herself leave the calm and keep going.

19
Closing Distance

Her senses compress, her footsteps register late and her body stops without her deciding to. A room that feels "Not occupied. Not empty. Held." She knows where it is before she turns.

20
Contact

The full reveal, told with no dialogue. A figure that moves by "reallocation," with proportions that correct themselves and a face that is "not held the same way." It recognizes her. What Iris recognizes is a process rather than a person, and some of it is already inside her.

21
Partial Loss

A hand, light contact, and some small automatic response she relied on dissolves. She can feel the shape of the gap but not what used to fill it. She steps back. Willow doesn't follow. "The exchange had been sufficient."

22
Exit

Willow lets her go. Iris walks back into Lowport and sits in a familiar café. Lena's text arrives and she reads it and doesn't reply. She is closer to Willow than anyone alive and further from herself. She should be afraid. "She waited. Nothing came."

What It's AboutThemes

  • Horror by subtraction. Nothing explodes. Willow doesn't add anything or distort anything, she removes, and what's left is functional, calm and incomplete. The horror is in the gap.
  • Loss isn't always visible. People keep walking, talking and working, and most of the world can't see what's gone. The city absorbs the damage: "He's still working." "That's good."
  • Identity is fragile. A person isn't just their memories or just their emotions but the connection between the two, and that connection can be interrupted.
  • Pain has purpose. Grief anchors us. Take away the pain and the meaning goes with it. Is suffering part of being human, or just damage?
  • You can survive and still be gone. Iris lives, stays aware and keeps working, and becomes progressively less herself.

Tone and style

Quiet, heavy and slow-burning. The horror is intimate (touch, breath, proximity), psychological and physical, with no jump scares, gore or creature-feature spectacle. The language is grounded and precise. Iris's condition is built into the syntax: short, fragmented sentences, flat questions ending in periods, and clinical self-logging. The prose reads like the inside of someone who has had something removed.

For the InterviewTalking Points & Key Lines

Likely questions, and the core of an answer

  • Where did the idea come from? What if the scariest monster didn't hurt you at all and just took something away, so quietly that you'd hardly notice and no one around you could tell?
  • Why her sister? Iris keeps everything else. Losing exactly one love, and knowing you should feel it, is more unsettling than losing everything. (The series has a reason it was Lena specifically. See the spoiler section below and don't give it away.)
  • Is Willow evil? Nothing it does reads as cruel. It's calm and observant, and the flatness is the horror.
  • Why does it let Iris go? Deliberately left open. In Book 1 it's the question the book ends on.
  • Why is the prose so spare and fragmented? It's Iris's condition rendered as syntax. The reader feels the gaps because the sentences have them.
  • Why a novella? It's built as a tight, claustrophobic opening chapter of a larger series. Book 2 is a full-length novel by design.
  • Is it sci-fi or horror? Horror with a grounded near-future frame. The technology could plausibly exist in 20 to 30 years, with no neon and no cyberpunk spectacle.

Where It Comes FromInfluences

Roughly in the order they arrived: picture books and childhood, then fantasy, then cyberpunk and science fiction about memory, machines and what makes a person real.

Earliest · Childhood

Mercer Mayer

Picture books

Author and illustrator of the Little Critter books and There's a Nightmare in My Closet (1968), which is about a child who faces the thing in the dark and finds it isn't what he feared.

Judy Blume

Middle grade & young adult

Wrote Tales of a Fourth Grade Nothing, Superfudge and Are You There God? It's Me, Margaret. Her books took the inner lives of ordinary kids seriously and talked to readers frankly.

Bill Basso · The Top of the Pizzas

Picture book · 1977

Ogre, the cook kept out of sight in Leonardo's pizzeria kitchen, loses his job when a customer faints at the sight of him. He lands on a decorated old rooftop, first standing in for a missing gargoyle, and ends up making a fortune feeding the sightseers.

J.R.R. Tolkien · The Hobbit

Picture book & record · 1977

The read-along book and record made from the 1977 Rankin/Bass animated film and released on Disneyland Records. It tells Tolkien's 1937 story of an ordinary person pulled out of a quiet life and into something far larger and older than him.

Middle · Fantasy

R.A. Salvatore · The Dark Elf Trilogy

Forgotten Realms · Homeland (1990), Exile (1990), Sojourn (1991)

The origin of Drizzt Do'Urden, a drow raised in the cruel underground city of Menzoberranzan who walks away from everything his people are. The series is set in TSR's Forgotten Realms world, and the trilogy is an outsider's story told from the inside, about a character defined by what he refuses to become.

Margaret Weis & Tracy Hickman · Dragonlance Chronicles

TSR · Dragons of Autumn Twilight (1984), Dragons of Winter Night (1985), Dragons of Spring Dawning (1985)

The Companions of the Inn of the Last Home, Tanis Half-Elven, Raistlin and Caramon Majere, Sturm Brightblade, Tasslehoff Burrfoot, Flint Fireforge, Goldmoon and Riverwind, in the world of Krynn after the gods have gone quiet. It's an ensemble story about friends and brothers, and at its center is Raistlin, the brilliant, sickly mage who gives up more and more of what made him human in exchange for power.

Later · Science fiction

William Gibson · Neuromancer

1984 · the founding cyberpunk novel

Case, a burned-out hacker, is hired for one last job and finds himself working for an artificial intelligence that is quietly arranging its own escape from the limits built into it. Corporations, minds that live inside machines, and a dead hacker's mind saved as a recording. It made the word "cyberspace" famous and swept the Hugo, Nebula and Philip K. Dick awards.

Star Wars novels

Canon and Legends

A favorite: K.W. Jeter's The Bounty Hunter Wars trilogy, The Mandalorian Armor (1998), Slave Ship (1998) and Hard Merchandise (1999), centered on Boba Fett. They're now part of the Legends line, the pre-2014 books Disney moved out of official canon.

Philip K. Dick

Blade Runner · Total Recall

Blade Runner (Ridley Scott, 1982) is adapted from Dick's novel Do Androids Dream of Electric Sheep? (1968). Total Recall (Paul Verhoeven, 1990) is adapted from his short story "We Can Remember It for You Wholesale" (1966). Both ask whether memories and feelings make someone real, and what happens when they can be manufactured or edited.

The Wachowskis · The Matrix

1999 · film, written & directed

Lana and Lilly Wachowski wrote and directed it; it wasn't based on a novel. Its question is a world that feels complete and isn't, and people who don't notice what has been taken from them.

Spoiler-Free · Safe on AirWhat Readers Find Out Next

Everything in this section names the questions Books 2 and 3 answer, never the answers. You can say any of it out loud.

Book 2 · The Ending

A sequel, set weeks after The Absence. A full-length novel of about 72,000 words.

  • Where Willow came from. Iris goes back into Meridian's files and recovers what was on the drives, and the reader finally sees who made her, how, and why. That history is told as full, living scenes from years earlier, not as documents or flashback summaries.
  • Why she's called Willow. Book 1 uses the name and never explains it. Book 2 is where the name comes from.
  • What was really in that building. The strange details of Iris's first walk through Meridian, the cut fence, the thin dust, the power rising and falling on its own, all turn out to mean something.
  • What happens to the Taken. Book 1 leaves them calm and functional. Book 2 follows what that calm costs over time.
  • That Willow isn't finished. What she is doing isn't producing what she wants, so she changes her approach, and she starts going back to people she left incomplete. Iris is one of them.
  • Why Iris survives. The book gets much closer to why Willow can't take all of her, and why the one thing she took was Iris's love for her sister. It gets close without handing the reader the answer.
"Book 1 asks what's in that building. Book 2 goes back inside and finds out, including why it's called Willow."A safe on-air line

In planning. Details may change, so keep this one general.

Book 2 — The Ending Light teaser

A sequel set weeks after The Absence. Taken people start dying. They aren't killed, exactly: they stop eating, walk into traffic, never seek treatment, because nothing in them objects to anything anymore. Kade opens Meridian's post-shutdown files, and Iris does the one thing she has always been good at. She recovers the data, and what comes off the drives is the story of how Willow was made.

Meanwhile Willow's method is failing, so she increases the dose. Then she starts going back to finish jobs she left open. Iris is an open job. It's a full-length novel, about 72,000 words.

Safe on air: "Book 2 goes back inside Meridian and finds out what was really in that building, and why it's called Willow."

Book 2 — The Ending Full spoilers
  • The origin. Seven chapters set years earlier and recovered off the drives tell the story of Dr. Marin Halloway, a Meridian researcher on a therapeutic memory-editing program, and her younger sister Willow Halloway, a patient whose treatment works. Willow comes out calm, agreeable and less. Her line: "It's still there. I just can't find the handle on it anymore." She means it as good news. About six months later she drowns.
  • The deviation. Grieving and unable to know whether the treatment let her sister walk into the water, Marin keeps Willow's pre-treatment emotional map, renders it on the substrate and changes two parameters in one undocumented afternoon. Each step is small and defensible. Something comes back. She's never a villain. Eventually she consents to being consumed whole, and she is happy.
  • The name. The entity is called Willow because Willow Halloway was a person. Iris finds the name, and nobody ever explains it.
  • Caleb dies at the midpoint. Willow comes back and finishes him. He calls Iris to report, flatly, "There's someone here." She gets there after.
  • Lila Mercer can't be warned ("That is useful information"), and she dies later, a single line in a list.
  • Supporting cast: Dr. Simon Virek (the substrate scientist who noticed the anomaly, raised it once, got a reasonable answer and let it go) · Dr. Feld (Willow Halloway's clinician) · Terri Roth (front desk) · Dr. Raman (program director, right in every scene) · Pell (technician, the first Taken person in the world).
  • The ending. Willow comes back to finish Iris and fails, the third refusal. In doing so she finds the empty place where Iris's love for Lena used to be and stops on it. Lena is now located. Iris never works out what happened.
  • Why Lena? In Book 1, Willow took that love out of recognition, not as collateral: it was the shape of a younger sister. The books never state this outright.
  • Willow's face slowly resolves over the series toward Willow Halloway's. She becomes more human, and more beautiful, the worse she gets.
Book 3 — The Silence Planned, may change
  • Weeks after Book 2, roughly June to October, all told from Iris's point of view. Willow becomes strategic and no longer needs to touch anyone. She studies her targets first.
  • The city misreads the spread and then welcomes it, because people are calmer and grateful.
  • Lena is partially Taken at the midpoint. Rourke breaks in public. Kade tries to contain it alone and is partially Taken.
  • Virek, found alive in hiding, is the only living person who ever saw Willow Halloway, and he recognizes the face.
  • The ending: a compulsion trap, then the water. Willow reaches Iris's irreducible core, can't take it and can't stop, and the attempt completes her. She finishes becoming Willow Halloway, walks past Iris and goes into the water. Iris doesn't kill her. Iris finishes her. The ending stays ambiguous so the series can continue.
