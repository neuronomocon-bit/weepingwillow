Chapter 2 — Recovery

The hatch was where the site notes said it would be. That was the last thing about the job that matched the notes.

Iris backed the van up to the service apron and left it facing the road. The utility block sat behind a fence somebody had cut and folded open years ago with better tools than intentions: two stories of yellow brick, no glass left above the ground floor, a transformer yard on the north side still humming behind its cage. Half the block had power. None of it had staff.

The notes said dry access to sub-level two.

She got the hatch up, put her light down the shaft, and watched the beam come back off water about fourteen feet below. Flat, brown, absolutely still.

"Great," she said.

The ladder was steel and had been wet for years. She went down slowly with the pack on her back, testing each rung with a boot before she trusted it. At the bottom she stopped on the second rung from the floor and looked at the room for a while before she put a foot in it.

Eight inches, give or take. The water was clear at the edges, clear enough to show floor tile, then went opaque further in where six years of whatever had been on that floor had gotten into it. Cable trays ran the length of the ceiling. Three fluorescent fittings still burned down at the far end. Past them the dark was thorough enough that her headlamp wasn't much help.

The racks were where they were supposed to be. That was something.

She stepped down. Water came over her boots to the ankle and found the gap at the top of the left one inside four seconds, because it always found the left one. Six years ago she had bought a pair of boots on sale. She had been paying for it ever since.

***

Four racks in two rows. The client wanted three.

Iris waded down the aisle with her light on the faceplates. Most of the labels had gone illegible, so she read serials instead and matched them against the manifest on her slate: *UB-2211. On-site recovery. Municipal utility, sensor and maintenance archive, 2038 forward. Client: Marrow Ridge Casualty.*

An insurer. Somebody suing somebody about a burst main. It was funnier than it needed to be.

The top two shelves of each rack were fine. Standby power was reaching them. She could see amber through the vent slots on the first controller, steady, patient, waiting six years for somebody to ask it a question.

The bottom shelf of the second rack was underwater.

She crouched and put the light on it without touching anything. Four drive units in a horizontal sled, submerged to about two-thirds. There was a tidemark on the sled housing where the water had sat at a lower level for a long time before something upstream failed and brought it up further.

"Of course that's the one they want," she said.

She straightened up, pulled her comms out of the chest pocket, and stood there in the water composing a sentence that would not lose her the contract.

***

Gil answered on the second tone. There was a printer going somewhere behind him.

"You're in?"

"I'm in. Three racks, like the notes said. Top shelves are live and I can pull those today."

"Good. How long?"

"Six hours if the power bridge behaves." She moved the light along the rack. "The bottom shelf of rack two is sitting in the water."

The printer stopped.

"How deep?"

"Two-thirds up the sled."

"Is it running?"

"No."

"So bring it up and run it."

Iris put her free hand flat against the rack upright and gave herself a second, because the first answer available to her was not the useful one.

"No," she said. "If I power a wet unit it arcs. Then I lose the platters, and nobody sues anybody about anything, because there's no record left to argue over."

"You're a recovery tech. Recover it."

"That's what I'm doing." She kept her voice level, which took more than she wanted it to. "Here's the part people don't know. If I pull those drives out and let them dry on the drive back, the contamination in that water dries onto the platter surface and bonds to it. A clean room can't lift it off after that without taking the oxide with it. The data goes with the oxide."

"So what, they stay down there?"

"They stay wet. I bag them sealed, in the water they've been sitting in, and they go to a bench that way. It's standard. Call any recovery house in the state and they'll tell you the same thing in the same order."

"That sounds like the opposite of what a person would do."

"It sounds like it. It's still right."

She watched the amber blink on the live controller. Somewhere over her head, in a building with heating in it, Gil was doing arithmetic about a deadline.

"How long, if they go to a lab?"

"Four to six weeks."

"We need it inside three."

"Then you needed a different basement," Iris said, and regretted the delivery about half a second after it left her. Not the content. The content was fine.

Gil laughed, which was lucky.

"Do the top shelves," he said. "Bag the rest, and send me the chain of custody tonight so I've got something to wave at people. I'll take it up the chain."

"That I can do."

"Iris." A pause with the printer in it. "If the wet ones come back empty, is that you or is that the water?"

"That's the water. It was the water six years ago. I'm just the one who showed up."

***

The work steadied her. It usually did.

She left the pack on the second rung where it would stay dry and brought the kit down piece by piece: portable terminal, fiber tap, power bridge, the local storage array in its case. Then she spent twenty minutes and a certain amount of language building a bench out of a plank and two upturned crates. Everything she was about to do needed a dry flat surface, and the room did not have one.

After that it was procedure, and procedure was restful.

Connect. Wake. Handshake. Authenticate against credentials belonging to a department that had been folded into another one twice since the last time anybody logged in, which meant three passes before the machine accepted that nobody living was coming back for it.

The first node came up slow and came up clean. The municipal archive tree resolved on her terminal with its labels nobody had updated in a decade, sensor telemetry nested under a heading for an office that no longer existed. She loaded the recovery profile, set the filter, and started the scan.

The progress bar found its pace.

This was the part she liked. She had never said so out loud to anybody, because saying it would invite a conversation about why, and there was no answer that did not sound strange. A dead system waking up and answering. Six years of nobody asking, and then somebody, and the machine had no opinion about the gap.

She started the first transfer. The array at her elbow ticked softly as it wrote.

The transfer took its time. There was nothing to do while it ran except watch a bar move, so she did what she always did and had a look at what she was pulling.

Sensor telemetry, mostly. Pressure readings off the main at four-minute intervals going back years, which nobody read until it mattered and then eleven people read at once. Valve states. Temperature at six points along the line.

Underneath it, in a folder somebody had labeled by hand, were the maintenance notes.

A man named R. Oyelaran had worked this section for two and a half years. He had filed a note about the pressure differential at the Ives junction in March. He filed it again in June. He filed it in September with a line at the bottom that read *third submission, no action logged.* The notes stopped four months after that, which meant either the main went or he did.

Iris read three more of them. He had been thorough, and he had been ignored, and he had gone on being thorough for another eight months anyway.

"Sorry, man," she said.

The bar had moved. She got back to it.

***

The bridge tripped at the ninety-minute mark and took the second node down with it.

She said something that would have embarrassed her mother, got down off the crate into the water, and went to look. The connection was fine. The bridge was fine. The fault sat upstream in a panel she had no access to and would not have opened if she had, because the one thing worse than a stalled job was a stalled job plus an electrical incident report with her name on it.

It cost her fifty minutes. Thirty went to rerouting off a separate circuit. The other twenty she spent standing in the dark waiting for the node to finish its own internal argument about whether it had been shut down cleanly. It had not been. Nothing down here had been shut down cleanly.

By the time the bar started moving again she was cold through both feet. She checked the time, did the arithmetic, and understood that she was going to be in this basement until nine.

She put her hands flat on the plank and breathed out through her nose.

Then she went back to it. The alternative was to hurry the write, and hurrying the write is how you get a corrupted block at hour four and a full restart at hour five. She had done that once, at twenty-four, when she had been better at everything except patience.

The transfers ran. She verified packet integrity on each one as it closed, because a transfer that reports complete and a transfer that is complete are two different claims, and only one of them is checkable.

***

The wet drives took longer than the extraction, in their way, because they took care.

She worked the sled loose without lifting it clear of the water. Each unit came up only as far as the surface and no further, then went straight into a bag with enough water in it to keep the drive covered. Four bags, double-sealed. She wrote each serial on the tape with a marker, photographed the label against the slate, and logged the time.

Nobody was going to check any of this. She did it anyway. The one time somebody checked would be the time she had not.

The last bag went into the crate. The crate went up the ladder ahead of her on a line, and she came up after it into air that smelled like rain coming.

***

The rain had not committed yet, but the wind off the water had teeth in it, and the transformer yard hummed behind its cage in the dark. She loaded out and latched the doors. Then she sat in the driver's seat with the heater running and her boots off, and her socks were precisely as bad as she had expected.

Nine forty. There was a receipt to file and a report to write. Neither was happening tonight.

She drove back toward the harbor with the wipers going.

***

The diner on Halstead was open, because it was always open, and it had the specific virtue of not caring what you looked like when you came in.

Iris took the booth at the far end and ordered. She had washed her hands twice at the sink in back and they still carried the basement's damp smell. Across the room two men off a night shift were arguing about a football team with the relaxed viciousness of people who had been having the identical argument for eleven years and had no intention of resolving it.

The food came fast, because it was already made. It was exactly as good as it needed to be, which was the whole point of the place. The woman who brought it took one look at the state of Iris's hands, put the coffee down slightly out of reach of them, and said nothing about it, which Iris appreciated more than she would have appreciated sympathy.

She ate the first half without stopping.

Her device went while she had a fork in her hand. It was Lena, so she answered it.

"Hey."

"Are you eating?"

"I am, yes."

"I can hear it. Keep going, it's fine."

"So Mom called me again about the twenty-second."

"What about the twenty-second."

"The thing I have now told you about twice."

"Then you've told me and I've lost it, so tell me a third time and I'll write it on my hand."

"She wants everyone there." A breath on the line. "And she is not going to ask you herself. She's going to ask me to ask you, and then when you don't come, she gets to be sad about it at me for a month. So I'm asking. Are you coming."

Iris put the fork down.

She was tired. Her feet were wet. She had spent eight hours in a basement getting an insurance company its paperwork, and the part of her still awake enough to form a sentence was not the part she would have chosen.

"You could just tell her to call me," she said. "You don't have to run the whole family through you. You like being the one everything goes through."

It landed. She heard it land, in the quarter second of nothing before Lena said, evenly, "Okay."

And Iris knew. Not later, in the van. Not on the drive home, not at three in the morning. Right then, with the phone against her ear and her food going cold, she knew exactly what she had said, and she knew why it would sit in Lena's chest for two days. She had built that sentence out of parts Lena had handed her herself, years ago, in confidence.

"Lena."

"It's fine. I'll tell her you're working."

"That's not what I meant."

"I know what you meant." Lena's voice had gone light, which was worse. "Go eat. You sound disgusting."

The call ended.

***

The first few minutes she spent being annoyed at Lena, which was easier.

Lena had called her at ten o'clock at night, at the end of a day Lena knew nothing about, to hand over a piece of family administration that was not hers to begin with. She had done it in the voice. There was a voice. Lena had a specific voice for this and had had it since she was nineteen.

What was underneath it, and what Iris got to somewhere around minute five with her thumb working the chipped edge of the table, was that Lena had told her about the middleman thing herself. Years ago, in a car, upset, about their father. Iris had listened to all of it and agreed with all of it. Tonight she had picked it up and swung it, because she was tired and it was the nearest object to hand.

Across the room the two men reached a part of the argument they had plainly reached a thousand times, and one of them laughed at the other one, and the other one took it.

Iris turned her phone face down on the table.

She ate a bit more. She paid, left too much, and went out to sit in the van with the engine off. The harbor lights did their thing on the water. At eleven minutes past ten she picked the phone back up and called.

Lena picked up on the fourth tone, which was on purpose.

"What."

"What time on the twenty-second."

There was a pause. Iris let it run.

"Two," Lena said. "But she'll say two and mean one thirty."

"Then I'll be there at one thirty."

"You're going to be there."

"I said I'd be there." Iris pulled the seatbelt across and got it home one-handed. "Do I have to bring anything, or is it one of the ones where she says don't bring anything and is then quietly devastated that nobody brought anything."

"It's that one."

"Right." She started the engine. "I'll bring the bread from the place on Ives. You can be the one who turned up empty-handed for once."

Lena laughed. It was a real one. Neither of them said anything about the rest of it.

"Drive safe," Lena said. "Are your feet wet? Your voice does a thing when your feet are wet."

"My feet are extremely wet."

"Good. That's what you get."

Iris drove home through a city closing itself up for the night. The heater beat the cold out of the footwell somewhere around the container terminal, and by the time she reached her own street she had stopped thinking about any of it.
