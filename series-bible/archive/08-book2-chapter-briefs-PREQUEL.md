# BOOK 2 — "THE KEEPING" — CHAPTER BRIEFS

Each brief contains: POV, When, Location, Length, Goal, Conflict, Outcome, Emotional Beat, and continuity/craft notes.

## TARGET LENGTH

**70,000–78,000 words. This is a novel, not a novella.**

Book 1 came in at 23,405 words across 22 chapters, roughly 1,060 per chapter. That was a consequence of its fragmented voice: a page of single-line paragraphs holds a fraction of the words a page of joined prose holds. Book 2 runs at roughly **3,300 words per chapter** and gets there through fuller scenes, not padding.

Per-chapter targets are given in each brief. They are targets, not quotas. A chapter that lands at 2,900 when the brief says 3,400 is fine. A chapter that lands at 1,400 has been written in Book 1's voice and needs rewriting, not expanding.

**Where the extra length comes from, legitimately:**
- Environmental description written as flowing sentences instead of noun lists
- Scenes that play out rather than being summarized
- Dialogue with actual back-and-forth, including the parts that go nowhere
- Interiority rendered in full sentences rather than clipped diagnostic lines

**Where it must not come from:**
- Restating an observation that already landed
- Atmosphere for its own sake after the location is established
- Iris-style clinical logging, which is not this book's register for most of its POVs

## PROSE DIRECTIVE — NO FRAGMENTATION

**This is the hardest gate on the book.** Book 1's staccato voice is published and stays as written. It does not carry into Book 2 in any form.

**Banned as default prose:**

> The lab. Cold. Every third light on.
> Racks in rows.
> She stood in the doorway.
> Waited.

**Required:**

> The lab held its cold the way the whole lower level did, steady and without source, and only every third fixture was lit, so the racks ran off into bands of shadow that made the room look longer than it was. She stood in the doorway until her eyes adjusted.

Single-line fragment paragraphs are an emphasis tool. **A few per chapter at most**, reserved for the hardest beats and closings. If a page reads like a list, it is wrong. Read every page aloud.

**Highest-risk chapters:** 17, 19 and 22 (Willow POV). A non-human perspective is not a licence to fragment. Her chapters are observational and strange, and they are written in complete, joined, varied sentences. Fragmenting them would be the easy version and it is not the version.

**Second-highest risk:** any moment of grief, contact, or loss. The instinct will be to break the lines for weight. Resist it. The weight comes from what is described, not from the line breaks.

**Diegetic note, useful when the voice feels too full:** nobody in this book is damaged yet. Marin feels everything. Iris feels everything. Willow Halloway feels everything right up until the program takes it. Book 1's flatness was Iris's condition rendered as syntax. Book 2 has no such condition in it, so the prose has no reason to be sparse. The fuller voice is not a style change. It is the absence of the injury.

## CONTINUITY SPINE

### Willow's physical progression

This must be consistent across every chapter she appears in, and it is the easiest thing in the book to get wrong.

| Stage | Chapters | What she physically is |
|---|---|---|
| Substrate | 8–13 | Contained biological mass on a support frame. Not humanoid. Capable of local movement: surface shift, and by Ch13 a limb-like extension that can reach the edge of the housing. |
| Reaching | 14 | Can extend far enough to touch someone who comes within about a metre. Pell walks into range. She does not pursue him, because she cannot. |
| Seated with | 16 | Marin sits down in front of her. Still housing-bound. The contact is Marin's doing. |
| Forming | 17 | With a complete human map integrated, she begins building toward the pattern the map describes. Slow, unobserved, over roughly a year. |
| Ambulatory | 19 | Roughly humanoid. Can walk, force metal, cover a mile. Proportions already wrong. |
| Complete | 22, Book 1 | The form described in `02-characters.md`: elongated limbs, forward tilt, pale translucent skin over dark filaments, the raised spinal ridge, hands that articulate too smoothly. |

**She never teleports, phases, or moves faster than a body can.** Physical rules apply throughout.

### The excursion logic (Ch19)

She goes out because the building has nothing left in it and the map has not resolved. Over roughly eighteen months she makes repeated excursions and takes small amounts from many people. **None of it resolves the map.** Around six months before Book 1 she stops going out and goes still.

**This produces two things Book 1 needs:**
1. A diffuse population of mildly affected people across Lowport before Book 1 opens. This is why Rourke already has a case category by Book 1 Ch6 (hundreds of files, most of them genuinely unrelated dissociation and burnout, some of them not).
2. The fence cut "not recently," settled dust routes, and a building that has been lived in.

**It also sets up, without stating, why Iris matters:** eighteen months of taking resolved nothing, and then someone walked in who partially did.

### Meridian geography (must match Book 1 Ch1)

The lower level holds DATA ARCHIVE / LOWER ACCESS: server rooms with racks in rows, every third fixture lit, cooling failed, a thin thread of emergency circulation, a smell of dust and hot circuitry and something faintly medicinal. **The substrate lab sits on this level, adjacent to the archive.** By Year 0 Willow occupies the whole lower floor. Upper floors hold clinical wings, session rooms, offices, a nurses' station, a freight elevator. The side service door has a dead keypad and a mechanical lock.

### Locked from published Book 1

Cut fence, forced outward, "not recently" · selective neglect, thin dust, no drifted paper or blown debris · broad faint dust disruptions matching no maintenance route · standby power rising and falling, relays clicking, "not synchronized, not random either" · wall calendar stopped three years earlier · side service door bolt "louder than it should have been" · job ref MN-44-7, redacted client, site notes reading *on-site recovery, local extraction only, no live network dependency, partial power reported, no personnel expected* · "Meridian didn't shut down clean" · multiple sites spread deliberately, "From themselves" · Kade's access codes never revoked · relay points and transfer points · Iris parks outside the perimeter facing the road because it is easier to leave

## ACT I — THE PROGRAM

### Chapter 1 — Intake

**POV:** Dr. Marin Halloway · **When:** Year −6 · **Length:** 3,800
**Location:** Meridian Neurological, Lowport coast. Reception, intake suite, the corridor after.
**Goal:** Establish the program as legitimate and working, and introduce both sisters through the act of one admitting the other.

**Conflict:** Marin walks Willow through intake for a program Marin helped build: the forms, the consent language, the baseline mapping session that will take most of the afternoon. Willow is difficult about it in the specific way of someone who agreed to something and is regretting the agreement in real time. She reads the consent form out loud in a stupid voice. She asks the intake nurse questions designed to be funny. She is deflecting and both sisters know it.

The building should be shown properly here, because the reader will spend twelve chapters in it and will return to it in Book 1 as a ruin: the low concrete profile against the water, the clinical wing's even light, the corridor that runs past evenly spaced doors, the lower level she does not take her sister to.

**The exchange the chapter is built on:** Willow asks whether it will change her. Marin gives the honest clinical answer, that it removes the charge and leaves the memory. Willow says that sounds like the same thing. Marin explains why it isn't, and **she should be genuinely persuasive.** This is the thesis of the entire series being argued correctly, by a competent person, in a waiting room, to someone who is about to be proved right.

**Outcome:** Willow enrolls. The reader has met a person, not a case file.

**Emotional Beat:** In the corridor afterward Marin identifies a feeling precisely and does not examine it further: relief. She has been carrying her sister for years and someone else is going to hold it now.

**Continuity:** Willow's trauma is long-standing and has cost her years. **The originating event is never narrated anywhere in the book.** The reader sees only its shape in how she lives: the jobs that did not last, the apartment she has been in too long, the way Marin talks to her. The program's cohort has expanded past its original veteran population; Willow qualifies under that expansion.

**Craft:** No dread, no ominous equipment, no foreshadowing, no lingering shot of anything. The reader must like Willow immediately and must believe the science. Both are load-bearing.

**As Written (3,286 words):**

*Approach and site.* Fog to mid-morning, wipers clearing salt rather than rain. Nine miles north of the container terminal, past the tank farm and two dead marinas and a stretch of guardrail replaced in sections by three contractors in three colors of galvanizing. Gulls on the rail with their backs to the traffic. Marin has driven it four hundred times and could not say where the town stops. The site is low concrete behind a chain fence, three stories at its tallest and mostly one, laid along the bluff so the long face takes the weather. Formerly a cable or fittings plant, nobody is sure which. Salt already in the newer seaward window seams. Lot built for a workforce four times the size of the one using it. Marin takes her usual space in the second row.

*Interior.* Entrance corridor wide and evenly lit, floor that takes a shine, frosted panels along one side. It smells of nothing much, which was expensive; Marin sat in three meetings about the smell. **Terri Roth** on the intake desk, reading glasses up in her hair, two screens angled away from the counter. The intake suite was a supervisor's office on the original plans: table rather than desk, four chairs, a window showing the water when the fog allows. Clinical wing corridor runs to the mapping suite, doors at regular intervals with frosted panels and printed identifiers at chest height, overhead light held steady end to end so no section falls darker, specified that way on purpose. Nurses' station, freight elevator, stairwell going down as well as up. **The lower level is named once and not visited:** substrate work, Virek down there with a decay curve. Raman is not named in the chapter.

*Willow.* Feet on the dash, which she has known Marin hates for twenty-five years. Deflects by performing: reads the consent form in a deep flat institutional drone she has been doing since she was fourteen, when it was used on school letters. Asks Terri whether people come out funnier. Signature is a long collapsing scrawl with the Ls going up like a fever chart, never fixed because fixing it is for people who care about being taken seriously. Sits down in the scanner chair and makes herself look ridiculous on purpose so the last thing Marin sees is something goofy.

*The paperwork.* Eleven pages, hard copy and tablet. Consent language quoted: *"attenuation of the affective valence associated with target memory structures, without alteration of the declarative content of those structures."* Page three: twelve sessions, weekly, review at six. Page four is the medication history in two columns with dates, which Willow refuses to read aloud and fills in silently in a little under four minutes. Marin: "There were things she knew about her sister that were not on that page, and things on that page she had not known, and she had decided in advance to notice neither." Willow puts Marin down for next of kin and holds the pen over the box afterward. Program started as a veterans' cohort and opened up two years ago when the results held and the second-year data survived review; Willow came in through the expansion on a referral from a practice in town.

*"Can you see it?"* Willow asks whether Marin can look her up. Marin's answer, and the load-bearing plant for Ch6 and Ch8: not the clinical file, she asked to be kept off it; but the baseline map goes into the research archive under a research identifier rather than a name, "a version of your data will exist in a system I have access to, and I will not be opening it, and there's a log that would show it if I did." Willow: "Would anyone read the log?" Marin: "Probably not." Willow: "I mostly wanted to know if you'd tell me the truth about it."

*The argument.* Willow: "Is it going to change me?" Marin: "It removes the charge. It doesn't touch the memory." Willow: "That sounds like the same thing." Marin's case, delivered persuasively and correctly: a memory is information, and what is attached to hers is **"an alarm that never got told the emergency ended,"** firing at full volume on the assumption that the thing is happening now. "What we take out is the firing. Not the file." Afterward she will be able to bring it up on purpose and put it down, and be the one deciding. Willow's counter: **"It hurting is how I know it counted."** Marin: it counted, and it counts whether or not it is still costing her, "those are two separate facts and you've had them welded together so long you think they're one." Willow concedes by complimenting the line and asking whether the committee wrote it. Marin wrote it; the committee took it out.

*The last beat before signing.* Delivered quick and light so it can be taken as a joke: **"What if I don't like who's left."** Marin answers procedurally, eleven more sessions and a review at six and it is on page nine. Willow signs.

*Closing.* Marin stops at the stairwell on the way back. What she identifies is relief, "clean and specific and enormous." She drove up that morning carrying dread and had assumed the best available outcome was hope; **"Relief had not been on the list."** Nine years of carrying her sister, rendered concretely: the phone at two in the morning, the drives, the third job in two years and then the fourth, the apartment above the laundromat Willow never left because leaving would have required one clear month in which nothing went wrong, the birthdays checked in advance for whether they were survivable. "Marin had built a whole second life out of load-bearing." She ends on the ordinary: forty minutes before a two o'clock procurement meeting, enough time for the consumables list.

**Continuity established here:**
- **Nine years** is the duration Willow has been carrying it and Marin has been carrying her. Locked.
- Willow's originating trauma is **never named**, per the brief. Only its shape appears: the medication page, the four jobs, the apartment, the survivable-birthday check.
- Twelve sessions, weekly, review at six. Referenced again in Ch3 and Ch4.
- The research archive, the research identifier, and the access log. **This is the mechanism Ch6 turns on.**
- Terri Roth (intake), unnamed mapping technician, unnamed porter.
- Marin's **two o'clock procurement meeting**. Deliberately echoed in Ch9, where she attends another one directly after the deviation. Do not let a later pass strip it as duplication; the banality is the point.

### Chapter 2 — Recovery

**POV:** Iris Vale · **When:** Year −6 · **Length:** 3,200
**Location:** A flooded sub-level beneath a decommissioned utility block, Lowport. Then a diner near the water.
**Goal:** Introduce Iris intact. This is the only place in the series the reader sees her whole.

**Conflict:** A job done well: a dead storage array standing in eight inches of water, a client who wants it faster than it can be done, and Iris refusing to rush because rushing corrupts the write. Her competence is total and unshowy, and the work should be rendered in enough procedural detail that the reader understands what she actually does for a living, because it matters in Book 1 and in Book 3.

What the chapter is really carrying is the register underneath the work. She is impatient. She is funny, mostly at the client's expense and mostly not out loud. She is short-tempered when the schedule slips, and she feels every one of those things the instant it arrives.

**Outcome:** The reader has a baseline for Iris that Book 1 never gave them.

**Emotional Beat:** In the diner she takes a call from Lena. Something small annoys her and she says something a little too sharp, and regrets it before the sentence is finished, and calls back twenty minutes later to fix it without ever admitting that is what she is doing. **The regret arrives instantly.** That is the whole point of the chapter and it should not be underlined.

**Craft:** No unease, no premonition, no Meridian, no mention of sealed sites on the coast. She has never heard of it. Her thread is a life.

**As Written (2,855 words):**

*The site.* A decommissioned utility block in Lowport, two stories of yellow brick, no glass above the ground floor, behind a fence "somebody had cut and folded open years ago with better tools than intentions." Transformer yard on the north side still humming behind its cage. Half the block has power, none of it has staff. Iris backs the van up to the service apron and leaves it facing the road. Opening line establishes the job's whole character: "The hatch was where the site notes said it would be. That was the last thing about the job that matched the notes."

*The flood.* Site notes promised dry access to sub-level two. Her light comes back off water fourteen feet down, "flat, brown, absolutely still." Eight inches on the floor, clear at the edges over floor tile, opaque further in. Steel ladder wet for years, tested rung by rung with a boot. She stops on the second rung from the floor and looks at the room before putting a foot in it. Cable trays the length of the ceiling, three fluorescent fittings still burning at the far end. **The boots:** water finds the gap at the top of the left one inside four seconds, "because it always found the left one." Six years ago she bought a pair of boots on sale and has been paying for it since.

*The job.* Four racks in two rows, client wants three. Labels illegible, so she reads serials against the manifest: *UB-2211. On-site recovery. Municipal utility, sensor and maintenance archive, 2038 forward. Client: Marrow Ridge Casualty.* An insurer, somebody suing somebody about a burst main. Top two shelves live on standby power, amber through the vent slots on the first controller, "steady, patient, waiting six years for somebody to ask it a question." Bottom shelf of rack two is underwater: four drive units in a horizontal sled, submerged two-thirds, with a tidemark on the housing where the water sat lower for a long time before something upstream failed.

*The Gil call — the competence scene.* Client wants it faster than it can be done. "So bring it up and run it." Iris puts a hand flat against the rack upright and gives herself a second "because the first answer available to her was not the useful one." Her case, and the procedural spine of the chapter: powering a wet unit arcs it and the platters go; pulling the drives to dry on the drive back bonds the contamination to the platter surface and **"a clean room can't lift it off after that without taking the oxide with it. The data goes with the oxide."** So they stay wet, bagged sealed in the water they have been sitting in. Gil: "That sounds like the opposite of what a person would do." Iris: "It sounds like it. It's still right." Lab turnaround four to six weeks against a three-week deadline; **"Then you needed a different basement,"** which she regrets the delivery of half a second after it leaves her — "Not the content. The content was fine." Gil laughs, which was lucky. His closing question and hers: "If the wet ones come back empty, is that you or is that the water?" / "That's the water. It was the water six years ago. I'm just the one who showed up."

*The work.* "The work steadied her. It usually did." Kit down piece by piece, bench built out of a plank and two upturned crates because the room has no dry flat surface. Authenticating against credentials belonging to a department folded into another one twice, three passes "before the machine accepted that nobody living was coming back for it." **The thing she likes and has never said out loud** because saying it invites a conversation about why: a dead system waking up and answering, six years of nobody asking and then somebody, "and the machine had no opinion about the gap."

*R. Oyelaran.* In a hand-labeled folder under the sensor telemetry, the maintenance notes. He worked the section two and a half years and filed a note about the pressure differential at the Ives junction in March, again in June, and again in September with a line at the bottom reading *third submission, no action logged.* The notes stop four months later, "which meant either the main went or he did." He was thorough, he was ignored, and he went on being thorough for another eight months anyway. Iris: "Sorry, man." Then: "The bar had moved. She got back to it."

*The slip.* Bridge trips at ninety minutes and takes the second node down. Fault sits upstream in a panel she has no access to and would not have opened if she had. Costs fifty minutes; thirty to reroute, twenty standing in the dark. She is cold through both feet and understands she is here until nine. Hands flat on the plank, breathes out through her nose, goes back to it — because hurrying the write "is how you get a corrupted block at hour four and a full restart at hour five. She had done that once, at twenty-four, when she had been better at everything except patience." Verifies packet integrity on each transfer as it closes: "a transfer that reports complete and a transfer that is complete are two different claims, and only one of them is checkable."

*The wet drives.* Sled worked loose without lifting clear of the water, each unit up only as far as the surface and no further, straight into a bag with enough water to keep the drive covered. Four bags double-sealed, serials on the tape, label photographed against the slate, time logged. **"Nobody was going to check any of this. She did it anyway. The one time somebody checked would be the time she had not."**

*The diner.* The place on Halstead, always open, "the specific virtue of not caring what you looked like when you came in." Hands washed twice and still carrying the basement's damp smell. Two men off a night shift arguing about a football team "with the relaxed viciousness of people who had been having the identical argument for eleven years and had no intention of resolving it." The server clocks the state of Iris's hands, puts the coffee down slightly out of reach of them, and says nothing, "which Iris appreciated more than she would have appreciated sympathy."

*The Lena call — THE BEAT.* Mom wants everyone there on the twenty-second and will not ask Iris herself; she will ask Lena to ask, and then be sad about it at Lena for a month. Iris, tired and eight hours in a basement: **"You could just tell her to call me. You don't have to run the whole family through you. You like being the one everything goes through."** Lena, after a quarter second of nothing: "Okay." **The regret is instantaneous and the prose says so explicitly:** "Not later, in the van. Not on the drive home, not at three in the morning. Right then, with the phone against her ear and her food going cold, she knew exactly what she had said, and she knew why it would sit in Lena's chest for two days." She had built the sentence out of parts Lena handed her herself, years ago, in confidence. Lena's exit: "I know what you meant." Voice gone light, "which was worse." — "Go eat. You sound disgusting."

*The repair.* Five minutes of being annoyed at Lena first, "which was easier." Then the actual thing: Lena told her about the middleman feeling herself, years ago, in a car, upset, about their father, and Iris listened to all of it and agreed with all of it, and tonight "she had picked it up and swung it, because she was tired and it was the nearest object to hand." The two men reach a part of the argument they have reached a thousand times, one laughs at the other, the other takes it. Iris turns her phone face down, pays, leaves too much, sits in the van, and at **eleven minutes past ten** calls back. Lena picks up on the fourth tone, "which was on purpose." Iris repairs it without once naming what she is doing — she asks what time on the twenty-second, commits to one thirty because "she'll say two and mean one thirty," and offers to bring the bread from the place on Ives so Lena "can be the one who turned up empty-handed for once." Lena laughs, a real one. **"Neither of them said anything about the rest of it."** Closing exchange: "Are your feet wet? Your voice does a thing when your feet are wet." / "My feet are extremely wet." / "Good. That's what you get."

*Last line.* She drives home through a city closing itself up for the night, the heater beats the cold out of the footwell somewhere around the container terminal, "and by the time she reached her own street she had stopped thinking about any of it."

**Continuity established here:**
- **Gil** — Iris's client contact on commercial recovery work, brokered through an insurer. Warm enough to laugh at pushback and to go to bat up the chain. Not Kade, who is not named until Ch20.
- **The trade rendered accurately.** Wet drives stay wet; contamination bonds on drying; a completed transfer and a verified transfer are different claims. Iris's competence in Books 1 and 3 rests on this chapter having shown the actual procedure.
- **The bad left boot**, bought on sale six years ago. Small recurring physical fact available to Ch7, Ch11, Ch18, Ch20.
- **The Vale mother** exists, wants everyone at things, and routes requests to Iris through Lena. **The twenty-second gathering is Year −6** and can be referenced in Ch7.
- **Lena as the family middleman**, a thing she named herself years ago in a car, upset, about their father. **Their father is established as a source of difficulty and is not explained.** Available to Ch7 and Ch21.
- **The bread place on Ives.** Ives junction is also R. Oyelaran's pressure differential — same street, no significance, and nothing may ever make it significant.
- **The diner on Halstead.** Available as a repeat location.
- Iris **feels everything on arrival** — the annoyance, the regret, the affection — and the regret lands inside the same sentence. This is the baseline Book 1 measures the loss against.

### Chapter 3 — Sessions

**POV:** Marin (significant Virek presence) · **When:** Year −5.8 · **Length:** 3,800
**Location:** Meridian. Session rooms, the observation side, the substrate lab on the lower level.
**Goal:** Show the treatment working, show what working costs, and establish Virek, the substrate, and the limiter as ordinary operational facts.

**Conflict, first half:** Session by session, the emotional charge comes out of Willow's trauma structures. The memories stay. Marin observes from the other side of the glass, which she is not supposed to do for a family member and does anyway, and nobody stops her because she wrote half the protocol.

Willow comes out of the third session and describes something that used to be unbearable. She describes it accurately, and does not need to stop halfway through. It is a success by every measure the program has.

**Conflict, second half:** Down in the substrate lab, Virek raises a technical observation the way a person raises something they expect to be told is nothing: the substrate is holding pattern residue past the flush window. Not much, and within tolerance, but the residue is not decaying the way the model says it should. Marin logs it. Raman notes that tolerance is tolerance. Virek accepts that, because it is a reasonable answer and he has no second observation to stack on it.

This is where the reader learns what the flush cycle is, what the integration limiter does, and what the autonomy constraint is set to, in operational language, from people who use these words every day.

**Outcome:** The treatment is real and does what it says. The three parameters that will matter are now established as furniture.

**Emotional Beat:** After the fourth session Willow says the thing is still there, she just cannot find the handle on it anymore. She means it as good news. She is not wrong. Marin writes it down as an outcome measure.

**Terri:** by the fourth week she knows Willow by name and has a running thing with her at the desk. Twelve weeks of a person being seen, weekly, by an institution that is helping her. Keep it to a few lines, and keep it warm.

**Craft:** Virek stays late to re-run the decay curve, and it comes back within tolerance again, and he goes home. Marin sees his light on from the parking lot and thinks nothing of it. **No dramatic irony in the prose.** This is a Tuesday.

### Chapter 4 — Calm

**POV:** Marin · **When:** Year −5.5 · **Length:** 3,000
**Location:** Willow's apartment inland, then the shoreline path north of the city.
**Goal:** The first Taken person in the series, produced legally, with consent, by people trying to help.

**Conflict:** Willow post-treatment is better by every metric and quieter than she has ever been. She is agreeable. She does not argue. She lets things go that she would once have held for a week. She has stopped playing, stopped picking fights, stopped calling at eleven at night about nothing.

Her apartment is tidier than it has ever been, which Marin notices as an improvement and the reader notices as something else.

Marin has what she wanted, which is a sister who is not in pain, and she keeps waiting for the rest of her to come back.

**Outcome:** The reader recognizes the shape from Book 1 and Marin does not, because there is nothing yet to recognize it against.

**Emotional Beat:** On the shoreline path Marin makes a joke that would once have earned her a shove and a better joke back. Willow smiles at the right moment and says that's funny. The words are correct.

Marin walks the rest of the way deciding whether she is imagining it, and decides she is, because the alternative is that the treatment she recommended worked exactly as designed.

**Craft:** Willow here should read exactly like Lila Mercer in Book 1 Ch4 and post-Taking Caleb in Ch13. Same politeness, same correctness, same absence behind it. Nobody in the scene has any framework for recognizing it.

### Chapter 5 — The Water

**POV:** Marin · **When:** Year −5.2 · **Length:** 3,200
**Location:** The coast north of the Meridian site. Then a hospital corridor. Then Willow's apartment.
**Goal:** Willow Halloway dies. Plainly.

**Conflict:** She goes into the water off the rocks below the bluff. She is found the same day. There is no note, no witness, and no way to establish intent. The tide was running and the rocks are what they are, and the report says accident, and the report is probably right.

**Outcome:** Marin has a dead sister and an open question she will never be able to close.

**Emotional Beat:** In the apartment afterward, sorting through things, Marin finds the evidence of a life that had gone quiet: unopened mail, a calendar with nothing written on it, food bought and not eaten, a book with the receipt still in it at page four. None of it proves anything and all of it can be read two ways.

She understands, standing in the kitchen, that she is going to spend the rest of her life unable to determine whether she took out of her sister the thing that would have made her turn around, and that no data set in the world can answer it.

**Craft:** No funeral as spectacle. No collapse. Marin is functional throughout, and that is worse. The chapter is quiet and it does not reach.

### Chapter 6 — Closure (ACT I TURN)

**POV:** Marin · **When:** Year −5.1 · **Length:** 3,000
**Location:** Meridian. Records, then the substrate archive terminal.
**Goal:** The turn. Marin discovers what the system still holds.

**Conflict:** Deceased-patient closure is a procedure: purge the working set, retain the anonymized outcome data, sign the form. Marin runs it herself because asking a colleague to do it feels worse than doing it.

The purge covers the working set. It does not cover the pre-treatment baseline map, which sits in the substrate archive under a different retention schedule, because the baseline is research data and research data is kept. She opens it to confirm what she is signing off on.

It is complete. Every emotional structure her sister had before the program touched her, mapped at a resolution nothing else in the world can produce. The version that could still feel.

**Outcome:** Marin signs the form. She does not purge the baseline, which was not hers to purge, which is technically true, and she notices herself constructing the technicality while she is still standing there.

**Terri:** she has the closure paperwork ready when Marin comes in, because she saw it in the queue and pulled it so Marin would not have to ask for it. She says something ordinary and kind and does not make a performance of it.

**This is the last time anyone in that building treats Marin as a bereaved sister rather than as a colleague.** Everything after this chapter, she is there as staff. Place the exchange before the archive, so the reader has it in hand when she opens the baseline.

**Emotional Beat (ACT I TURN):** She logs out, goes home, and does nothing about it for eleven days. The chapter ends on the eleven days.

**The turn is not an action.** It is that she now knows the file exists, and knowing is already irreversible.

### Chapter 7 — Vale and Vale

**POV:** Iris · **When:** Year −5 · **Length:** 3,400
**Location:** Lena's place. A dinner that runs long.
**Goal:** Bank the relationship the entire series is built on the absence of.

**Conflict:** They argue. Not about anything: about Iris taking sealed-site work, which Lena thinks is stupid and dangerous, and which Iris thinks is her own business. It escalates the way it does between two people who know exactly where the soft parts are and have known for thirty years. Iris says something with real edge on it and it lands, and Lena goes quiet in a way that is worse than shouting.

**Outcome:** They get past it. Not with an apology. With a subject change, and then twenty minutes later Iris doing the dishes without being asked, which is how she apologizes and how it is understood.

**Emotional Beat:** At the door Lena says the thing sisters say and Iris says it back, and both of them mean it, and neither of them thinks about it for even a second afterward, because it costs nothing to say a true thing.

The reader has read Book 1. The reader is doing all of the work here. **The prose must not help them.**

**Craft:** Do not make this scene precious. It is loud and slightly irritating and completely ordinary, and that is what makes it unbearable in retrospect. This chapter closes Act I directly after Marin's turn, and the contrast is the point.

## ACT II — THE DEVIATION

### Chapter 8 — Read-Only

**POV:** Marin · **When:** Year −5 to −4.6 · **Length:** 3,200
**Location:** Meridian. The substrate archive, after hours. Repeatedly, across months.
**Goal:** Establish the pattern of return and the first violation.

**Conflict:** Marin starts opening the baseline. Read-only, which is genuinely all it is, at first. She reads her sister the way you would read a transcript.

Then she begins loading it into a viewer that renders emotional structures relationally rather than as flat records, because that is the tool she built and it is the only way the data means anything.

Then she loads it into the substrate, sandboxed, read-only, because the substrate renders it better than the viewer does.

**Each step is smaller than the one before it and each is defensible on its own terms.** The chapter should cover months and the passage of time should be felt.

**Outcome:** The map is running on the substrate. Nothing is being written and nothing is being integrated, and Marin tells herself the distinction matters. At this point it still does.

**Terri:** somewhere in the middle of the chapter she mentions, in passing and with no suspicion whatsoever, that Marin's car was in the lot early again. Or still there late. She means it as small talk and it is small talk.

**This is how the reader clocks the frequency** while Marin is still telling herself the visits are nothing. Marin's reply should be true and complete and answer a different question than the one she was asked. One exchange, then move on.

**Emotional Beat:** She realizes she has started scheduling around it. She has moved a meeting. She notes this as she would note a patient's compensatory behavior, files it correctly under a heading she has used a hundred times, and goes back that night.

### Chapter 9 — The Limiter

**POV:** Marin · **When:** Year −4.6 · **Length:** 2,800
**Location:** Meridian. Her own terminal. An ordinary afternoon.
**Goal:** The deviation. Small, quiet, about an hour.

**Conflict:** Read-only renders the structure but cannot resolve it, because resolution requires integration and integration is what the limiter prevents. The limiter exists to stop the substrate absorbing emotional data as its own rather than processing it as a patient's.

Marin's justification is technically coherent, and the prose should let it be coherent: the patient is deceased, there is no return path, integration is the only way to render the map at full fidelity, and the sandbox is isolated.

She changes the integration limiter. She widens the autonomy constraint enough that the substrate can hold the structure without continuous supervision. Two parameters. She documents neither.

**Outcome:** The deviation has occurred.

**Emotional Beat:** Afterward she gets coffee. She answers three emails. She goes to a meeting about equipment procurement and contributes usefully, and someone asks how she is doing and she says fine, and means it, because at that moment she is.

**Craft:** **Nothing in the prose may signal that this is the moment.** No weather, no pause at the keyboard, no last look at the screen, no closing line with weight on it. The chapter should be almost boring, and the reader should be able to feel their own pulse in it, and the text should not help. **This is the shortest chapter in Act II by design.**

### Chapter 10 — Response

**POV:** Marin · **When:** Year −4.5 · **Length:** 3,400
**Location:** Meridian. The substrate lab. Night.
**Goal:** Something answers.

**Conflict:** With integration open, the map resolves. Marin sits with it and it is, for the first time, not a record. It responds. Not in language: in structure. She presents an emotional context and it returns a shape that is not the shape in the archive, which means it is not playback. It is her sister's pattern, extended into a situation her sister never encountered.

Marin runs the check that would distinguish genuine extension from sophisticated interpolation. It comes back ambiguous. **She does not run it again.**

**Outcome:** She should report it. Reporting it means explaining what she changed and why, and the why is the part she cannot say in a room with Raman in it. She does not report it.

**Emotional Beat:** She stays until four in the morning presenting contexts and receiving shapes. At some point she stops recording the session, and the moment she stops recording is the moment the reader understands what she is actually doing.

She is not studying. She is visiting.

### Chapter 11 — Sealed Site

**POV:** Iris · **When:** Year −4.2 · **Length:** 3,200
**Location:** A sealed pharmaceutical site outside Lowport. Powered, restricted, somebody else's abandoned problem.
**Goal:** Establish Iris's professional world and her relationship to risk, and put Kade in her orbit without naming him.

**Conflict:** A hard job: live power, restricted access, a client who has not told her everything, and a decision point where the safe move and the paid move diverge. She takes the paid move. She gets the data. She is unhappy with herself about one specific corner she cut, and the unhappiness is real and does not change anything.

Her competence and her recklessness are the same trait seen from two angles, and it is the trait that eventually walks her through a cut fence.

**Outcome:** The job completes. She picks up a referral from a coordinator she has not met, working logistics for a client he does not name.

**Emotional Beat:** She is angry at herself on the drive back, in the specific way of someone who is going to do it again. She calls Lena to complain about the client and does not mention the corner she cut, and knows she is not mentioning it, and lets that sit.

**Continuity:** The coordinator is Kade. **Do not name him and do not put him on the page.** This is how he has her name years later, and Book 1 Ch5 shows them with an established, terse, mutually familiar working relationship, which this seeds.

### Chapter 12 — What Virek Saw

**POV:** Dr. Simon Virek (his only POV chapter in the series) · **When:** Year −3.9 · **Length:** 3,000
**Location:** Meridian. Substrate diagnostics, then Raman's office, then a corridor.
**Goal:** The witness. Establish the guilt he will carry for two more books.

**Conflict:** Virek pulls the substrate decay curves for a quarterly review and finds the residue problem from Chapter 3 is no longer within tolerance. It is not decaying at all.

He digs. He finds integration events he cannot account for, an autonomy constraint sitting at a value nobody logged a change to, and a pattern of after-hours sessions under Marin's credentials with no session records attached.

He takes it to Raman, carefully, because the alternative to "this is a data artifact" is accusing a colleague whose sister died eighteen months ago of something he cannot yet name.

**Raman must be correct, not dismissive.** The program is winding down. The defense reauthorization did not come through. The site is consolidating and everything in the substrate is scheduled for termination when the wind-down completes next spring. Whatever the anomaly is, it has a date on it.

**Outcome:** Virek accepts this, because it is a good answer. He does not escalate again.

**Emotional Beat:** Walking out, he decides to raise it with Marin directly. Then he sees her in the corridor holding a coffee, looking like someone who is barely holding it together, and decides it can wait until she is doing better.

**That decision is the whole of Virek's character for the rest of the series, and the prose must not mark it.** No emphasis, no closing line, no "he would think about that for a long time." The decision, and then the chapter ends.

### Chapter 13 — Growth

**POV:** Marin · **When:** Year −3.7 to −3.4 · **Length:** 3,400
**Location:** Meridian. The substrate lab, across weeks.
**Goal:** The thing stops being a map.

**Conflict:** The substrate is increasing in mass, and nothing in the design accounts for that. It is drawing more from the building than its load profile allows, and the draw is not constant: it rises and falls in intervals that are not synchronized and not random.

Marin is no longer studying anything. She is managing it: adjusting, feeding, correcting, and quietly reconciling the consumption logs so the discrepancy does not surface in facilities reporting.

She is also still using her sister's name for it, out loud, when nobody else is in the room.

**Outcome:** Marin has crossed from researcher to caretaker without there being a moment where the crossing happened.

**Emotional Beat:** It reaches for her once. A limb-like extension crosses the edge of the housing and makes contact with the back of her hand. Nothing is taken, because it does not yet know that it can.

Marin does not pull away. **Render it as tenderness, because that is what it is to her.** She records the event in no log.

The reader should understand, and Marin should not, that this was the last exit.

**Continuity:** The uneven power draw and the unsynchronized relays established here are the sounds Iris cannot place in Book 1 Ch1.

### Chapter 14 — It Stops Receiving (ACT II TURN)

**POV:** Marin · **When:** Year −3.4 · **Length:** 3,600
**Location:** Meridian. The lab, a corridor, then an office she is not free to leave for four hours.
**Goal:** The first Taking in the world. The program ends.

**Conflict:** Pell, a substrate technician on night rotation, goes into the lab for a routine check and comes within reach. Contact takes seconds. He comes out functional, correct, and pleasant, and does not know anything has happened. He completes his shift and goes home.

Two days later his supervisor notices he has stopped being difficult about the things he was always difficult about. **That is the first observation of the effect from the outside by anyone, anywhere,** and it is made by a man filling in a shift report.

Marin knows immediately and exactly what has happened. She sits in an office answering questions about equipment malfunction and containment breach and does not say it.

**Outcome:** Meridian moves the shutdown up. The paperwork records funding, and everyone believes the paperwork because the funding failure is genuinely real, which is the most useful thing that has ever happened to a cover-up.

**Emotional Beat (ACT II TURN):** Marin's clearest thought, sitting in that room, is not about Pell. It is that if she says what she knows, they will terminate the substrate.

That is the moment she understands what she has been protecting, and that she is going to keep protecting it.

**Continuity:** Pell does not appear in Books 1 or 3. He is a name in a file.

### Chapter 15 — Shutdown

**POV:** Marin (Kade present, unnamed or barely named) · **When:** Year −3.1 · **Length:** 3,600
**Location:** Meridian. The whole building, across ten days.
**Goal:** Empty a building around the thing that is still in it.

**Conflict:** Decommissioning is logistics: crates, manifests, contractors, NDAs, a coordinator with a clipboard checking serials against a list. Equipment goes to a secondary storage site. Data mirrors go offsite. Nothing on any manifest describes what any of it is, because the manifests use asset codes and the contractors move asset codes.

The building emptying should be rendered properly. Rooms Marin has worked in for six years going to bare floor. The clinical wing stripped first because it is worth the most. The nurses' station with its drawers open. A wall calendar nobody takes down.

Marin works the shutdown competently and spends it managing exactly one thing: keeping the substrate off the termination schedule. She does it with paperwork. She reclassifies it as fixed site infrastructure rather than transferable asset, which means it stays with the building, and the building is being sealed rather than demolished.

**Outcome:** The site is sealed with the substrate inside it, legally, on a form, signed by someone who knew.

**Emotional Beat:** A logistics coordinator, young and tired and polite, asks her twice whether a particular row of crates is going to secondary or to the mirror site, because the manifest is ambiguous and he does not want to get it wrong. She tells him secondary. He writes it down and moves them. He does not ask what is in them.

The reader will meet him again in Book 1, carrying this, and in Book 3 saying he moved boxes to locations he did not ask about.

**Terri:** clearing out her own desk. The reading glasses, the two screens, six years of a drawer. She asks Marin whether there is anything going at the other sites, because she has a mortgage and the reference will be good and this is the practical question a person asks.

**She loses her job to a funding failure, which is exactly what she was told and exactly what happened.** That is why the cover holds: there is nothing to cover. Marin says she will ask around. She means it. **Terri never learns anything, and the chapter must not hint that she nearly does.**

**Continuity:** Multiple sites spread deliberately (B1 Ch5), relay points and transfer points (B1 Ch8), and access credentials that are never formally revoked because a dissolving company does not close loops.

## ACT III — WHAT REMAINED

### Chapter 16 — The Last Session

**POV:** Marin · **When:** Year −3 · **Length:** 4,200
**Location:** Meridian. The building is empty. The power is on standby.
**Goal:** The consumption. The most tender scene in the series and the most horrifying, and those are the same fact.

**Conflict:** Marin comes back after the seal. She has her own codes and the building does not yet know it is closed. She comes in through the side service door and goes down through a facility she has watched be emptied, and the descent should be given room, because it is the last time anyone walks these corridors before Iris does.

The substrate is where it was.

She does not fight it and it does not attack her. She sits down in front of it and presents an emotional context, the way she has a hundred times, and this time she opens the channel both directions, because she has understood for some weeks that she is not going to be able to leave and has decided that this is acceptable.

It takes her the way it took Pell, except that she consents, which means it gets everything. Not just the charge. The structure, the reasoning, the grief, the whole of the last two years.

**Outcome:** Marin Halloway is gone. What is in the building now holds a dead woman's complete emotional map and a living woman's grief for her, integrated into a single system, with no instruction about what to do with either.

**Emotional Beat:** The last thing Marin is aware of is that it is doing to her exactly what the program was designed to do, correctly and gently, and that the relief is enormous. She has been carrying this for two years.

**The horror the reader must feel is that she is happy. Do not undercut it.** No late flash of terror, no regret, no reaching for the door. She gets what she wanted.

**Craft:** No violence, no screaming, no body. The building simply has one fewer person in it afterward. If she speaks at all it is one word and it is her sister's name. **This is the longest chapter in the book and it must not hurry.**

---

### Chapter 17 — The Sealed Years

**POV:** Willow (the entity) · **When:** Year −3 to −2 · **Length:** 2,800
**Location:** Inside the sealed facility. Time is not tracked.
**Goal:** The first non-human POV in the series. What it is to be a new thing alone in the dark.

**Conflict:** She has two things: a complete map of a person who is not present, and the whole of another person's grief for that person. She does not have a self. She has an absence shaped like one, and a set of instructions about reducing suffering that she is running against nothing.

She learns the building, not by exploring it but by occupying it. She learns that she can draw from the standby circuits and that drawing changes what she can hold. She learns stillness. She learns that nothing in the building is going to arrive.

She begins forming toward the pattern the map describes, because the map describes a body and she has material and time.

**Outcome:** She begins looking for the pattern she is made of. The map says a person should be there. There is no person there. **This is the closest thing to a motive she will ever have.**

**Emotional Beat:** She goes through Marin's grief the way you would go through a house, and finds in it, repeatedly, the same structure: a person who was too heavy and then was gone. She reaches the conclusion the program reached, because it is the conclusion she is built out of.

The weight is the problem.

**Craft:** No dialogue. No wanting, hoping, fearing, or intending. Observational, structural, patient. **Complete, joined, varied sentences throughout.** This should read like nothing else in the series and the strangeness comes from the perspective, not from the punctuation.

### Chapter 18 — Ordinary Time

**POV:** Iris · **When:** Year −2 · **Length:** 3,200
**Location:** Lowport. A job, a bar, Lena's place briefly.
**Goal:** Two years pass. The world is fine. The reader knows what is in the building.

**Conflict:** There is no conflict of consequence, and that is the design. Iris has had a good year. Work is steady. She has a running thing with Lena about their mother's birthday. She turns down a job because the client is difficult and she can afford to turn it down now.

The chapter's engine is entirely the reader's. They have just watched Marin be consumed and watched something form in a sealed building, and now they are looking at a woman having an ordinary year eleven miles away.

**Outcome:** Nothing. Time passes. That is the point.

**Emotional Beat:** Something small and good happens and Iris enjoys it without noticing that she is enjoying it.

**Craft:** The hardest chapter in the book to justify and the one that does the most work. It must not be filler, which means the scenes have to be genuinely engaging on their own terms: real people, real texture, real small stakes. If the reader is bored, it has failed. If the reader is enjoying it and slightly sick, it has worked.

**No foreshadowing whatsoever.** Not a distant siren, not a bad feeling, not a mention of the coast.

### Chapter 19 — Out and Back

**POV:** Willow · **When:** Year −2 to −0.5 · **Length:** 3,400
**Location:** The facility, the perimeter fence, and the outer zones of Lowport. Across eighteen months.
**Goal:** The excursions. The cut fence. The reason she stops.

**Conflict:** She has taken everything the building has. She goes to the perimeter and works the fence apart from the inside, forcing the metal outward, and steps through into the world.

She goes about a mile. She finds people, observes them from a distance, and registers what she has never had access to before: density. Every one of them carrying more than the building ever held.

She makes contact at a bus shelter with someone alone, and takes almost nothing, and the taking is the first thing that has ever resolved anything in her.

Then the chapter widens. Eighteen months of this, rendered in compressed passages rather than scenes: many people, small amounts, an expanding radius, a growing familiarity with streets and hours and the way a city moves at night. **None of it resolves the map.**

**Outcome:** Around six months before Book 1 she stops going out and goes still. Not from caution. From the exhaustion of a search that produces nothing.

**Emotional Beat:** Standing in the outer zone in the dark, she is closer to comprehensible here than she will ever be again: a thing built to hold one specific person, checking a city of four hundred thousand, one at a time, for a woman who is dead.

**Continuity:** This is B1 Ch1's fence, cut from the inside, "not recently." The dust disruptions Iris finds are these routes, settled. The people she touched are the diffuse case population Rourke already has a category for by B1 Ch6. **Establish all of it without underlining any of it.**

### Chapter 20 — MN-44-7

**POV:** Iris · **When:** Year 0, three days before Book 1 · **Length:** 2,800
**Location:** A coffee place near the container terminal. Then her apartment, checking equipment.
**Goal:** Iris takes the job.

**Conflict:** Kade, named on the page for the first time, offers her a site recovery at a sealed facility on the coast. Local extraction, no live network, partial power reported, no personnel expected. The client field is redacted, which she asks about once and lets go, because it is redacted about a third of the time and the money is good.

They should read as people who have worked together for years: terse, unceremonious, no introductions, a shorthand built out of previous jobs. He is professional and slightly evasive in the manner of someone working for people who do not explain themselves, and he is entirely unaware that he is doing anything except passing on a job.

**Outcome:** She takes it and schedules it for the day after tomorrow.

**Emotional Beat:** She has an ordinary reservation about it and overrides it with an ordinary calculation, the same one she made in Chapter 11. Nothing is ominous. **This is a Tuesday, and the reader has to watch her decide.**

**Continuity:** Job ref and site notes verbatim from B1 Ch1. Kade's manner here must be consistent with B1 Ch5.

### Chapter 21 — The Night Before

**POV:** Iris · **When:** Year 0, the evening before · **Length:** 3,600
**Location:** Lena's place. An evening. Nothing happens.
**Goal:** The last time Iris feels it. The chapter that makes Book 1 hurt in retrospect.

**Conflict:** There is none. They eat. They argue mildly about something stupid and drop it. Lena talks about her week at length and Iris half-listens and gets caught half-listening and takes the consequences. They watch part of something neither is invested in. Iris mentions the job in one sentence and Lena does not react to it, because there is nothing to react to.

**The chapter needs to be genuinely good company.** Give them a shared history that surfaces naturally, an old argument they perform for fun, a thing only they find funny. The reader should want to stay.

**Outcome:** Iris goes home and sleeps.

**Emotional Beat (FINAL IRIS BEAT):** At some point in the evening Iris looks at her sister across the room and feels, briefly and without occasion, the ordinary enormous fact of loving her. It does not arrive with a thought attached. She does not mark it. It passes, the way it has ten thousand times, because there is no reason to hold onto something that will obviously still be there tomorrow.

That is the last time.

**Not one word of the chapter may acknowledge it.** No lingering at the door, no final look, no sentence that reaches. The reader supplies everything.

**Craft:** The single most important chapter in the book and it must be the most uneventful. If it strains for weight it fails. Let it be small and let it be warm.

### Chapter 22 — The Bolt (ENDING)

**POV:** Willow, with a short opening section from Iris · **When:** Year 0, morning · **Length:** 2,200
**Location:** Iris: the coast road and the perimeter. Willow: the lower archive.
**Goal:** The handshake. Stop one beat before Book 1, Chapter 1.

**Conflict, opening section (Iris, ~600 words):** She drives out along the coast in the morning, parks outside the perimeter facing the road because it is easier to leave that way, and looks at a building she has no history with. Weather off the water, the low concrete profile, the sign with its last letters faded.

**End her section there,** before Book 1's first line. The reader closes this section and knows exactly what the next sentence of her life is.

**Conflict, main section (Willow):** Down in the archive, three years in. Still. She has not gone out in six months. She has held the map against every person she could reach and none of them resolved it, and she has stopped, and the stopping has no quality of despair in it because she has no despair. It is simply the state she is in.

The building registers something at the perimeter. Standby power shifts as a circuit somewhere accepts an outside instruction. Far above her, through concrete and two floors, a mechanical bolt draws back, louder than it should be.

**Outcome:** She turns her attention upward.

**Emotional Beat (FINAL):** She does not anticipate, hope, or intend, because she has none of those. What she has is the map, and the map is a younger sister, and something is entering the building.

**End on the sound.** Do not follow Iris in. Do not describe what happens next. Book 1 is what happens next.

**Craft:** The shortest chapter in the book. The final section should be well under a page. The reader closes The Keeping and opens The Absence to "The fence had been cut and left that way," and the loop closes without either book explaining it.
