Weeping Willow: The Absence

ACT I - DISCOVERY
Chapter 1 — The Gap
The fence had been cut and left that way.
Not recently. The metal had curled inward where it had been forced, the edges dulled by salt and weather. Someone had gone through it once. No one had bothered to close it after.
Iris stood outside the opening and looked at the facility beyond.
Meridian Neurological sat low against the coast, all concrete and narrow glass, more bunker than clinic. The building had been designed to keep things in or out. It was hard to tell which. Time had reduced the difference. Rust marked the seams. Salt had crusted in the corners of the windows. A section of exterior paneling near the west side had peeled back and stayed there, exposing insulation gone dark with damp.
The sign over the main entrance still said MERIDIAN NEUROLOGICAL RESEARCH, though the last letters had faded enough that the word looked incomplete unless she stared at it.
Wind moved in off the water in long steady passes. It carried salt, wet metal, old rot from the shore below. The sky had the flat gray look it kept for days at a time in Lowport. Not rain yet. Close.
She checked the job on her slate.
MN-44-7
On-site recovery
Local extraction only
No live network dependency
Partial power reported
No personnel expected
Routine.
The client field was still redacted. That happened often enough. A landlord. An insurer. A company paying to find out what had been left behind after everyone important had already gone home.
She zipped the slate into the chest pocket of her jacket and ducked through the gap in the fence.
Gravel shifted under her boots. The lot beyond had been cleared once, then ignored. Faint parking lines cut through weeds and patches of standing water. A security post near the road had collapsed inward on itself. The glass was gone. A plastic chair lay on its side inside.
Her van sat outside the perimeter, facing the road.
She liked it that way. Easier to leave.
The main entrance doors were sealed and unpowered. One pane had fractured from the inside, the cracks held in place by the laminate. She did not stop there. The site notes had mentioned a side service door with a dead keypad and a mechanical lock. Better entry. Less exposure.
She walked the perimeter with the building to her left and the ocean below the bluff to her right.
The sound of the water came and went in uneven ways. Once it seemed too near. Then it dropped off completely for three or four steps, as if something had shut a door on it.
She stopped.
The ocean remained where it should. Gray water. White edge where it struck the rock below. The sound returned, ordinary again.
She kept walking.
The side entrance was recessed into the concrete, half sheltered by a loading overhang. The door was painted institutional green. Most of the paint had blistered off. A dead access panel sat in the wall beside it.
Iris set her pack down and took out the bypass unit.
The work was familiar enough that she barely thought about it. Panel off. Leads in. Manual current. Wait for the lock to accept outside instruction. She watched the small screen on the unit scroll through its handshakes.
The bolt clicked back.
It was louder than it should have been.
She paused with one hand on the bar, listening.
Nothing from inside.
No footsteps. No machinery beyond the faint electrical life of a building not entirely dead.
She opened the door and stepped through.
The air changed immediately.
Cooler. Not sharply. A degree or two at most. The building held a separate climate from the coast outside. Old insulation. Concrete. Stale containment.
She closed the door behind her and the sound of the ocean disappeared.
The hallway ahead was dark except for two weak fixtures farther down. Her headlamp came on with a soft click. Light found tile, painted walls, directional signage, a row of framed notices clouded behind their plastic covers.
The floor was cleaner than it should have been.
Not clean. Just not abandoned in the right way. Thin dust. No drifted paper. No blown-in debris. No corners full of dead insects and dirt. The neglect felt selective.
She adjusted the strap of her pack and moved forward.
Her boots made sound on the tile. Each step landed and went away too quickly. The hall did not hold the echo it should have.
She passed offices with narrow glass windows beside the doors. Most were dark. A few stood open by an inch or two. Inside one, a desk still held a monitor, a mug, a stack of paper gone gray at the edges. Another had been stripped almost bare except for a wall calendar stopped three years earlier.
At the junction ahead, a ceiling sign pointed deeper into the facility.
DATA ARCHIVE / LOWER ACCESS
That matched the file. The server rooms were internal. Less weather exposure. Better chance of intact storage.
She followed the sign.
As she moved farther in, the faint hum of standby power became easier to hear. It rose and fell in slow intervals, never quite establishing a pattern. Somewhere deeper in the building a relay clicked. Then another. Not synchronized. Not random either.
She passed a nurses’ station built into the wall. Empty drawers half open. Intake terminals dark. A plastic wristband still looped around one of the monitor stands. The label had faded too much to read.
The corridor narrowed, then opened again near a stairwell and a freight elevator with its doors half parted around a black gap. She did not look into it. The stairs to lower access were beside it, painted concrete, yellow edge strips on each step worn nearly white.
The air was colder below.
Her light caught a placard on the landing: AUTHORIZED PERSONNEL ONLY. Someone had put a second placard beneath it at some point, cheaper plastic, printed in a different font.
ARCHIVE STORAGE
A practical correction. The kind people made after the facility had become something other than what it had been designed to be.
The lower hallway was narrower and more heavily reinforced. No windows. No offices. Just mechanical rooms, locked cabinets, and finally a set of double doors standing open into the archive space.
The server room lights were on.
Every third fixture, maybe. Enough to create long bands of white over the tops of the racks and leave the aisles between them in partial shadow. Cooling had failed years ago, but some emergency circulation still moved a thin thread of air through the room. It carried the smell of dust, hot circuitry, and something faintly medicinal.
She stood in the doorway for a second and let her eyes adjust.
Rows of racks extended ahead in clean lines. Containment cages stood open where doors had been removed. Status LEDs pulsed green and amber in the dark.
Good. Better than expected.
She set her pack on the floor near the first active row and took out the extraction kit: portable terminal, fiber tap, power bridge, local storage array. The work steadied her attention. Connect. Wake. Handshake. Authenticate against dead credentials until the machine accepted that no one still living was coming back for it.
The first node came online slowly. The Meridian interface resolved on her terminal, old but intact. Archive trees. Department labels. Research partitioning. User logs frozen at dates that meant nothing to the job.
She loaded the recovery profile the client had flagged and started the scan.
A progress bar appeared.
Behind it, files began sorting by relevance: patient mapping outputs, memory architecture studies, clinical observation footage, internal correspondence. Too much to pull blind. The filter narrowed it. She started the first transfer.
The room remained quiet except for the low electrical hum and the occasional shift in load as old systems woke enough to answer her.
A light three rows over fluttered twice, steadied, then went out.
She did not look up right away. When she did, nothing had changed except the darker patch it left.
She moved to a second rack. Different cluster. Same procedure.
The timestamp on her terminal read 14:07.
That seemed early. She could not say why.
She continued.
At the third rack she noticed marks on the floor.
Not footprints. Just areas where dust had been disturbed and settled again. Broad, faint disruptions that did not align with the aisles or with any route a maintenance cart might have taken. She crouched and touched one with two gloved fingers. Fine particulate. Dry enough to lift. Nothing useful in it.
She straightened and resumed the transfer.
The second cluster completed. The storage array at her feet ticked softly as it wrote. She checked the packet integrity, verified, started the next.
Routine.
The room should have settled into that.
It did not.
She became aware, after a while, that the farther rows were darker than they should have been given the fixture spacing. Not by much. Just enough that the geometry of the room changed when she looked directly at it. Aisles seemed to run longer. Rack ends aligned, then failed to align.
She told herself it was the lighting.
Then the hum changed.
She stood still and listened.
The sound had moved. A second earlier it had seemed to come from above and to her right. Now it came from somewhere ahead. Not louder. Relocated.
A relay clicked in the dark.
Then another behind her.
She looked over her shoulder toward the doors. They remained open. The hallway beyond was visible in a thin vertical slice between the nearer racks. Pale wall. Empty.
Her terminal chimed. Third cluster complete.
She exhaled and flexed her hand once inside the glove. Too long in the same posture. That was all.
She started one final pull from the deeper archive partition. The directory tree there was less orderly. Fewer labels. More access restrictions. Experimental storage. She copied the partition map first in case the system failed under load.
The progress bar moved slower than the others.
She waited, watching it.
At 28 percent, she heard something that sounded like fabric against concrete.
Not a footstep. Too soft. Too close to the ground.
She turned toward the sound.
Nothing in the aisle beside her. Nothing between the rows. Her light passed over black server faces, cable bundles, dust, steel.
She stayed turned that way for several seconds.
The terminal did not chime. The hum continued. Somewhere a fan bearing scraped once, then stopped.
She went back to the work.
At 41 percent, the room temperature shifted.
Not the whole room. A local change. The air on the right side of her face cooled first, then her neck, as if a door had opened nearby into a colder space.
She looked to her right.
The aisle there ended in another row. No door.
Her own reflection, thin and distorted, moved across the black cabinet glass as she turned. For a second she thought there was a second shape behind it, offset from hers by half a step.
Then only her own light remained.
She unhooked the headlamp from the collar of her jacket and tightened the band around her head instead. The direct beam felt better.
The progress bar reached 53 percent.
She checked the room map on her slate.
Her current position marked correctly. The archive layout loaded from the building plans still showed a rear service wall fifteen meters ahead. Utility clearance beyond. No additional chambers.
She lifted her head and looked down the row.
It was longer than fifteen meters.
That could be perspective. The alternating dark and light distorted distance. She knew that. Still, she found herself counting the cabinet breaks, the access gaps, the cable drops.
Too many.
She opened the map again and zoomed in.
The plans did not change.
When she looked up, she was already moving.
She left the terminal attached and walked down the central aisle toward the back of the room. Her light passed over rack labels, warning stickers, old asset tags. Meridian internal numbering. All ordinary. The floor remained level beneath her boots. Dust. Cable guards. A dropped zip tie. A maintenance cart parked against a post as if someone had meant to come back for it.
Halfway down the row, the hum diminished until she could hear her own breathing under it.
She slowed.
The air ahead had the still quality of an enclosed place, but there was no visible barrier. Just the continuation of the aisle into a darker section where fewer fixtures remained active.
She checked the distance on the slate.
If the plans were right, she should already have reached the rear wall.
She had not.
She took three more steps.
The light found concrete.
A wall.
Smooth. Pale. Close enough now that she could see trowel lines in the sealant where a vertical seam had been filled and painted over.
She stood in front of it and said nothing.
This section did not exist on the plans. Or the plans were older than the modifications. Either way, someone had closed off part of the archive after the original build. The surface was newer than the surrounding concrete. Less stained. Less spalling.
She moved the beam across it slowly.
No handle. No badge reader. No visible access panel. Just continuous wall.
She became aware of a sound so faint it might have been nothing more than blood in her ears.
Not mechanical.
Not environmental.
A small, intermittent softness. Almost like someone drawing a hand over cloth.
She turned the beam back toward the aisle.
The path behind her remained open. Racks on either side. Alternating strips of light. The terminal’s status LED glowed faintly where she had left it, farther back than it should have been.
She started walking toward it.
After six steps she looked back.
The wall seemed farther away than it should have.
She stopped.
Looked forward again.
The terminal light remained fixed in the distance, small and green.
Her slate vibrated once in her pocket. An automated status ping from the extraction kit. She took it out.
Transfer stalled. Link unstable.
She frowned, turned back toward the wall, then stopped again.
The air had changed.
The colder patch was no longer behind her. It was beside her.
On her left now.
Close.
She rotated the beam slowly toward the adjacent aisle.
The light entered the gap between racks and showed only the first several feet: cabinet doors, cable bundles, floor, shadow.
Then something pale at the edge of range.
Vertical. Still.
Not part of the room.
Iris held the light there.
The shape did not resolve all at once. A line first. Then another. The suggestion of shoulders. Height close to human. Maybe a little too narrow. Or the posture made it seem that way.
The beam trembled slightly because her hand did. She stilled it.
No face yet. Just the sense of one turned toward her from the dark.
She took one step backward.
The shape did not move.
The room seemed to soften around the edges. The hum receded. Even the weight of the terminal in her other hand became less distinct.
Her body did something before her mind finished with it. Her free hand went to the inside pocket of her jacket where the emergency torch was clipped.
She never touched it.
A different motion entered the edge of the light.
A hand.
Pale at first glance, though not pale in any way skin should be. Too smooth. The joints wrong in their economy. It emerged without hurry, fingers slightly parted, reaching not with force but with certainty.
She tried to step back again.
The floor felt delayed under her boot.
Then the hand touched the inside of her wrist where her glove ended.
The contact was cool.
That was the first thing.
Not cold enough to hurt. Just cool, like metal that had been indoors.
Then stillness.
Not in the room. In her. Everything that had been moving in her body—the small adjustments, tension, breathing, the background readiness every person carried without noticing—flattened at once into something calm and level.
The light in her hand dipped.
She saw, with sudden clean clarity, the kitchen in the apartment she had lived in at nineteen. Late sun through blinds. A chipped yellow mug on the counter. Someone laughing in the next room.
Then another.
A hospital corridor with bad coffee in a paper cup. Her own hands around it. A voice saying her name from farther away than it should have been.
Then another.
A beach in winter. Rocks slick with ice. A red scarf whipping sideways in the wind. A girl turning back to look at her, face bright with something immediate and familiar.
The images did not arrive as thoughts. They rose whole. Not random. Weighted.
The hand on her wrist tightened very slightly.
Not enough to hurt.
The red scarf again.
A set of keys dropped into her palm.
Her mother leaning over a sink, shoulders shaking once, then still.
The kitchen light over the stove.
A bedroom door half open.
A feeling attached to all of it, large enough that it should have had shape. Something directional. Something reaching.
It began to lift.
Not the images. The thing inside them.
Iris heard herself make a sound that did not become a word.
The beam of her headlamp slipped across the dark and for one instant found a face.
Eyes set where they should be and looking too directly from a stillness no human face could hold. Mouth closed. Skin with a surface quality that suggested skin only until the light moved. Features learned from observation rather than born to them.
Then the beam hit the rack and all she saw was steel.
The pressure at her wrist deepened.
The red scarf vanished.
Not the image. The weight in it.
Something else rose in its place. A staircase. Two girls sitting on it with their shoulders touching. The smell of rain in old wood. The smaller one saying something fast and earnest and waiting for an answer.
That too began to lift.
No, Iris thought.
Or meant to think.
The stillness held her there. Gentle. Complete.
She knew, for one thin second, that something was being removed. Not memory exactly. Not only memory. The charge in it. The thing that made the memory belong to her in a way facts alone never could.
Then the second ended.
The room broke apart.
Light. Concrete. The sound of a relay snapping somewhere overhead. The storage array ticking as it wrote. Her own breath coming too hard.
She was on one knee beside her open pack at the near end of the archive room.
The portable terminal was folded halfway shut on the floor.
One glove was off.
Her wrist ached where skin met sleeve.
She looked at the terminal clock.
14:41.
She stared at it longer than she needed to.
The last time she had looked, the transfer had been at 53 percent.
Now the status line showed complete.
All requested clusters recovered. Integrity check passed.
She looked around the room.
Racks. Lights. Dust. Open doors to the hall.
Nothing else.
No wall at the back visible from here. No dark aisle with a shape in it. Just an ordinary industrial room.
Her headlamp lay on the floor by her knee. The elastic strap was damp with sweat where it had touched her hairline.
She picked it up and switched it off.
Her breathing slowed. Then slowed again.
There should have been a sequence in her head between standing in the aisle and kneeling by the pack. There was not. She tried once to build it and got only disconnected pieces: the older wall, a pale hand, red fabric moving in wind, then static.
She stopped.
The storage array showed green across the board. Data present. Full extraction. She packed the terminal, coiled the fiber line, sealed the drives in their foam sleeves. Her motions remained steady because she had done them enough times that they could continue without much from her.
When she stood, she had to wait for the room to settle.
No dizziness. Just a brief uncertainty about where the nearest aisle began.
Then it passed.
She slung the pack over one shoulder and walked to the doors.
The hallway beyond looked unchanged. Pale paint. dim fixtures. The freight elevator still half open around darkness. The nurses’ station empty. Her own boot prints faint in the dust where there had been none when she entered.
She went back the way she had come.
At the stairwell landing she stopped and checked the time again.
14:47.
She had been inside forty minutes.
That seemed wrong.
Not impossible. Just wrong.
Outside the side door, the wind hit her face with enough force that she had to blink against it. The ocean returned all at once, loud, ordinary, too real after the sealed quiet of the building.
She stepped out, closed the door behind her, and stood under the loading overhang with the pack hanging from one hand.
The clouds had lowered while she was inside. Fine mist moved across the lot. Her van remained where she had left it. Fence cut open. Road beyond.
Everything accounted for.
She crossed the gravel and unlocked the van.
The interior smelled faintly of coffee gone cold and old vinyl. She set the pack on the passenger seat, got in, and shut the door. For several seconds she did not start the engine.
Her hands rested on the steering wheel at ten and two, not from intention but because that was where they had landed.
There was a gap.
She could name that much.
Not a vague feeling. Not fatigue. Not distraction. A clean missing section where continuity should have been. She remembered entering. She remembered the first transfers. She remembered kneeling by the pack with the job complete.
Between those points, the line did not hold.
She started the engine.
The heater came on with a dry mechanical smell. Wipers dragged once across a windshield not yet wet enough to justify them. She turned them off, put the van in gear, and drove out through the gap in the fence.
The road from Meridian back toward Lowport followed the coast for a while before turning inland through warehouses and utility yards. The water stayed visible in broken sections between storage tanks and crane arms and stretches of scrub grass flattened by wind.
She drove on habit.
Signals. Turns. Speed adjustments. A truck merging badly ahead of her. A crossing light stuck on red though no train came. The city gathering slowly around the industrial edge of it.
She did not think about the building unless the thought arrived on its own, and when it did it brought only fragments. The wall. The sensation of stillness. Something at her wrist. A shape that had almost been a person and had not been one.
No full sequence.
At a light near the river crossing, she reached into the cup holder for the old bottle of water she kept there and found a photograph underneath it.
She must have knocked it loose when she got in.
Iris picked it up with one hand while the light held red.
It was a printed photo, not old enough to yellow but old enough that the corners had softened from being handled. Two women on a seawall somewhere in summer. Wind moving their hair. One of them—her—looking at the camera without quite smiling. The other leaning in toward her shoulder, smiling openly, as if the person behind the camera had said something stupid on purpose.
Her sister.
Lena.
Iris knew that immediately.
The recognition was clean and factual. Lena. Younger by a few years than she was in the picture now, but still Lena. Same eyes. Same mouth. The same insistence in the way she took up space near people she belonged to.
The light changed. A horn sounded behind her.
Iris set the photo on the dashboard and drove through the intersection.
At the next light she looked at it again.
Lena.
Her sister.
The words remained true.
She waited for what should have followed them. Some movement under the ribs. Warmth. Memory with weight. Affection arriving so automatically it would not usually register as an event at all.
Nothing came.
Not numbness. Not pain. Just absence. A place where response should have been, cleanly empty.
She kept driving.
Rain began in a fine mist and thickened slowly against the windshield. The wipers took over their steady rhythm. Warehouses gave way to older blocks near the center of the city, brick facades gone dark with damp, patched windows, marine supply stores, clinics, tired apartment buildings with rusting fire escapes. Lowport held its weather the way old metal held cold. Without drama. Constantly.
The photograph stayed on the dashboard.
Each time her eyes found it, recognition happened again. Lena. Her sister.
And each time the rest failed to arrive.
By the time she pulled into the lot outside her building, the gap had settled in beside her like something already established.
She turned off the engine and sat for a moment in the ticking quiet.
Then she took the pack, left the photo where it was, and went inside.
Chapter 2 — Caleb Ward
The second site sat three blocks inland from the river, behind a row of low warehouses that had been built in stages and never finished to the same standard twice.
Corrugated steel, patched brick, sections of poured concrete with mismatched aggregate. The loading yard held shallow puddles that reflected the gray sky in broken shapes. A forklift stood idle near the bay doors, one tire half deflated, its forks resting against the ground as if it had set something down and not picked it back up.
Iris parked along the curb and killed the engine.
The building in front of her had once been a small distribution center. The signage had been removed, leaving a cleaner rectangle on the wall where it had been. A newer placard had been bolted over it, cheap plastic, printed lettering:
HARBOR DATA STORAGE — TEMPORARY FACILITY
Temporary had been there long enough for the edges to curl.
She checked the job on her slate.
HD-12-3
On-site recovery
Partial network integrity
Local backup priority
Two-person access recommended
The second line had been the reason for the additional name on the assignment.
Caleb Ward.
She had worked with him before. Enough times that the pattern was familiar. He handled access, physical layout, and anything that required improvisation on site. She handled the systems. They did not need to coordinate much. The work separated cleanly.
She stepped out of the van and closed the door.
The air here carried less salt than the coast, more oil and old water. The river ran two blocks over, hidden behind storage tanks and a chain-link fence. The sound of it didn’t reach this far.
A side door stood open.
Caleb was leaning against the frame, one foot braced behind him, looking at something on his phone. He didn’t look up when she approached.
“Door’s already done,” he said. “Lock was decorative.”
His voice held the same casual tone she remembered. Slightly amused, even when nothing required it.
Iris nodded once and stepped inside.
He pushed off the frame and fell in beside her without comment.
“Power’s spotty,” he said as they walked. “Whole place is running off something they shouldn’t be trusting. Lights flicker every few seconds. If anything reboots, we start over.”
“Local copies,” she asked.
“Mostly. Some of it tries to phone home, but whatever it’s calling doesn’t pick up.”
“That’s fine.”
“Yeah,” he said. “Usually is.”
The interior was one large open space broken into sections with temporary partitions. Metal racks, plastic sheeting, cable runs stapled along walls that had not been built to carry them. The floor had been swept recently. Cleaner than Meridian.
Caleb gestured toward the back.
“Main cluster’s there. Smaller node off to the right. We can split it or you can take both.”
“I’ll take both.”
“Of course you will.”
There was no push in it. Just an observation.
They moved toward the racks together, then separated without needing to say it. Iris set her pack down near the central cluster and began laying out her equipment. Caleb moved to the perimeter, checking access points, glancing at a breaker panel mounted crookedly on a column.
“Let me know if anything drops,” she said.
“I’ll keep an eye on it.”
The first node came online quickly.
Better maintained. More recent hardware. The interface loaded without delay. She set the recovery parameters and started the transfer.
The system responded cleanly. No resistance. No hesitation.
Normal.
She moved to the secondary node and repeated the process.
Behind her, Caleb dragged something across the floor. A chair. He set it near a column and sat with it turned backward, arms folded over the backrest.
“Client say what they’re after?” he asked.
“Everything tagged to archive clusters four through nine.”
“Ambitious.”
“They usually are.”
He made a small sound that might have been agreement.
Silence settled into the room in a way that did not feel wrong. Just work. Equipment. Two people occupying separate tasks in the same space.
Caleb watched her for a while.
She felt it without looking up.
“You were out on the coast yesterday,” he said.
“Yes.”
“Meridian.”
“Yes.”
“How was it?”
“Intact enough.”
“That’s not what I asked.”
She adjusted a cable, checked the transfer rate, and let the question sit for a second before answering.
“Systems responded,” she said. “Data was recoverable.”
He tilted his head slightly.
“Anything weird?”
“No.”
He let that sit, then shifted his weight on the chair.
“Place has a reputation,” he said. “People don’t like going in there.”
“People don’t like most of these places.”
“Yeah, but Meridian’s a different flavor. Medical, memory work, that kind of thing. Makes people uneasy.”
“It’s not active.”
“That doesn’t mean it’s empty.”
She glanced at him then.
His expression hadn’t changed. Not pushing. Just watching to see what she would do with it.
Iris returned her attention to the terminal.
“It was empty,” she said.
“Okay.”
He didn’t challenge it.
The transfer on the first node reached thirty percent. Stable. The second node lagged slightly behind but held.
Caleb leaned back in the chair, balancing it on two legs.
“You sleep?” he asked.
“Yes.”
“How long?”
“Enough.”
“Define enough.”
She did not answer.
He let the chair settle back onto all four legs with a soft clack.
“Sorry,” he said. “Not trying to run diagnostics on you.”
“It’s fine.”
“You just seem—”
He stopped there.
She waited.
“—different,” he finished.
“How.”
He shrugged.
“Slower, maybe. Or just… quieter in a way you weren’t before.”
She checked the integrity readout again, though it did not require checking.
“I’m working,” she said.
“Yeah.”
“That’s the focus.”
“Yeah.”
He didn’t sound convinced. He didn’t sound unconvinced either.
He stood and walked a slow circuit around the room, hands in his pockets. He checked a loose cable, nudged a crate back into place with his foot, paused at a window cut into the wall that looked out onto another section of the building.
“Say something,” he said.
“About what.”
“Anything.”
She looked at him.
“Why.”
He smiled slightly.
“Because you usually would have by now.”
She considered that.
Then:
“The power distribution here is unstable,” she said. “If the secondary node drops, we lose part of the transfer.”
He nodded once.
“That tracks.”
“That’s not what I meant.”
“I know.”
The progress bars moved.
Caleb returned to the chair and sat again, this time facing forward.
“You remember that job on Dock Nine?” he said.
“Yes.”
“Guy who tried to run his whole operation through one unsecured relay?”
“Yes.”
“He kept telling us it was ‘temporary.’”
“That’s common.”
Caleb shook his head.
“He had that sign up in the office. Remember? ‘Temporary solutions become permanent problems.’”
“Yes.”
“And then he ignored it.”
“Yes.”
Caleb looked at her for a second.
“That was funny,” he said.
“It was inconsistent,” she said.
He let out a short breath through his nose.
“Yeah,” he said. “That’s one way to put it.”
The first node completed.
The terminal chimed softly. Iris verified the transfer, sealed the data block, and moved to finalize the second.
Caleb watched her work.
“You always do that the same way,” he said.
“Yes.”
“Same order. Same timing.”
“Yes.”
“Never seen you rush it.”
“It reduces errors.”
“Yeah.”
He nodded to himself.
“Good system.”
She did not respond.
The second node reached completion without interruption. Both transfers clean. No data loss.
Iris powered down the connections and packed the equipment back into her case.
“Done,” she said.
“That’s it?”
“Yes.”
He stood, stretching once.
“Easy day.”
“Relative.”
“Sure.”
They left the building the way they had entered. The air outside felt warmer than it should have. Or maybe the interior had been cooler. She did not check.
Caleb locked the door out of habit even though it had not held when they arrived.
“Food?” he said.
She paused.
“Brief,” she said.
He grinned.
“Diner around the corner. It qualifies.”
They walked without talking. The streets here were narrow, built for trucks that no longer ran regularly. A few shops remained open. A parts supplier. A small machine repair place. A diner with a hand-painted sign and a window that fogged at the edges even when the door stayed closed.
They went in.
The interior was narrow and long, counter on one side, booths on the other. The air smelled like coffee, grease, and something sweet that had been baked earlier and was no longer warm.
They took a booth near the back.
A waitress came by, set two menus down, and filled water glasses without asking.
“Coffee,” Caleb said.
Iris nodded.
The waitress left.
Caleb leaned back in the booth and looked at her across the table.
“You sure you’re good?” he said.
“Yes.”
“That’s your answer for everything.”
“Yes.”
