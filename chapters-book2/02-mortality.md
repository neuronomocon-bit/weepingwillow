Chapter 2 — Mortality

Lowport Public Health had moved since the winter, into the top two floors of a building that had been a bank, and the ground floor was still laid out like one. A counter ran the width of it with six positions and two of them staffed, and a rail still stood where a line had once been managed. The floor was the original stone, polished by eighty years of people coming in to be dealt with, and the polish ran in a path from the door to the counter and nowhere else.

Iris had asked for the appointment three times. The first request went unanswered, the second produced a form, and the third produced a slot on a Thursday morning at a quarter past nine. A quarter past nine was for someone who would be gone by ten.

She was early. She spent the time reading the noticeboard, because a room she was going to work in was worth knowing: two flu clinics, a helpline number, and a poster about hand hygiene with one corner curling away from the wall and an older poster showing at the edge behind it.

At nine sixteen a woman came down to collect her, and did not introduce herself, and took her up in an elevator that had been retrofitted into the stairwell.

***

Rourke’s office was smaller than the last one, and Iris understood the change at once: that room had been built for receiving people across a desk, and this one had been chosen for working. A desk sat against the window and a second against the wall. The second was where the work happened: three screens, a printer, and paper in stacks with the edges squared.

“Sit,” Rourke said.

Iris sat.

Rourke did not sit. She stood at the second desk with one hand on the back of the chair and looked at the middle screen for a while before she turned it.

“You wanted the caseload,” she said. “You’re not getting the caseload. I’m going to show you one line off it and then you’re going to tell me what you think, and I want you to understand before you start that I haven’t asked you here because I’ve changed my mind about anything.”

“All right.”

“Say that back.”

“You have not changed your mind about anything.”

“Good.” Rourke turned the screen the rest of the way.

The plot had time along the bottom and a count up the side, and two lines on it. One was flat and slightly rising, and it had been flat and slightly rising for four years. The other came off it about ten weeks back and went up.

“That’s my cohort,” Rourke said. “Four hundred and sixteen people carrying a presentation I have been calling behavioral flattening for two years. The flat line is what a cohort like that should do, and it is fitted on four years of everyone I see, not on them. Any population that size produces deaths. Old people, sick people, people who were already going to die of the thing they had before they ever came to me. Three in ten weeks. That is the number. That is what the model says and the model has been right since I built it.”

“And the second line.”

“Eleven,” Rourke said.

“Eleven in ten weeks,” Rourke said, “in a cohort of four hundred and sixteen, against an expectation of three. Nearly four times the count. That is not something I can wave off and it is not something I can publish either, because eleven is a real signal and a small number at the same time, and a small number is where you go and look for the boring answer first.”

“Which is.”

“A batch of records arriving late. A caseload reassigned off a clinician who left, because the deaths follow the file and arrive in a lump. A change in what gets coded as a cohort death.” She counted them off. “There was no late batch. Nobody left. The coding is the same coding I have used since I opened the cohort. And I ran the whole thing against last year with the same filters.”

“And it held.”

“It held.”

***

“I’ll read you the deaths,” Rourke said, “and you’ll see the problem before I say it.”

She read them from the screen, and she read them at the pace of something she had read enough times to have stopped hearing.

A man of fifty-one had stopped eating without once refusing a meal. He had gone eleven days on tea and half a sandwich a neighbor brought him. When the neighbor finally called someone he was cooperative and pleasant and answered every question. He had not been aware of the eleven days as anything that had happened to him. He died in the hospital of an electrolyte imbalance. That is what the certificate says and the certificate is correct.

A woman of thirty-eight had walked into traffic on the Halstead crossing. There was camera footage and Rourke had watched it eight times. The woman steps off, and a car is coming, and she does not hurry and she does not stop. There is no moment where she reacts to the car. The coroner had gone through it twice looking for whatever would let him call it deliberate and had not found it, because the woman had come off a shift, was carrying groceries, and had a dentist’s appointment in her calendar for the Tuesday.

A woman of sixty-four had died of sepsis from an infected foot. She had been seen for it. She had been given the antibiotics and the instruction to come back if it got worse. It got worse. She did not come back.

A man of twenty-nine had stopped taking a medication he had been on for nine years, without deciding to, and had not restarted, and had not noticed the difference until the thing the medication was for did what it does.

A man of seventy had sat down on a bench on the harbor road in the first week of February and had not got up.

“That’s five of the eleven,” Rourke said. “The other six are the same. Do you see it?”

“Yes.”

“Say it, please. So I know we’re looking at the same thing.”

“Every one of them has a cause,” Iris said.

“Every one of them has a cause,” Rourke said. “Every one of those certificates is correct. I could defend all eleven to a review board and I wouldn’t have to lie once. There is no signature. There is no toxicology. There is no anatomical finding that any two of them share. If you brought me these eleven and told me nothing else I would file them and go home.”

“The pattern isn’t in the deaths.”

“No,” Rourke said. “It’s in who is having them. I’ve been looking for the connection for eight weeks, and I’m a good enough clinician to know the difference between one I haven’t found and one that isn’t there. I haven’t decided which this is.”

She looked at the screen rather than at Iris.

“The position I’m in is worse than the number. I have four hundred and sixteen people who arrived without traveling. They did not spread from a point. They did not cluster around a source. They came up across this city at once, and I could never say why, and I’ve never once pretended I could, and nothing since the winter has changed my view of it.”

“That was the first thing.”

“That was the first thing.” Rourke turned the screen back toward herself. “This is the second, and the two of them are about the same people. I’ve been doing this for nineteen years. One unexplained finding is a gap in the literature. Two, on the same cohort, is a mechanism I do not have.”

Rourke took a single sheet off the second desk, and she did not hand it over.

“In February you gave me a list,” she said. “Nine names. You had no clinical standing, you had no business having half of those names, and you told me they were early. I filed it, and I filed it correctly. An unqualified person handed me a list of patients and told me they were going to get worse. There is one right answer to that and I gave it.”

“I remember.”

“The man who stopped eating is on it.” Rourke put the sheet down on the desk between them, face up, and Iris saw her own handwriting from five weeks ago. “Fourth line. You wrote *early, mild, no request for help* next to him, and I read that as a layperson describing someone who was coping.”

“It was.”

“It was also the thing that killed him. One out of nine is not a result. I know what one out of nine is. But it’s the reason you’re sitting in my office instead of reading another form.”

Iris looked at the fourth line of her own handwriting.

She had sat across a table from that man for forty minutes in February. She could produce the room: a kitchen with the extractor fan running because the switch was shared with the light. She had written *no request for help* because he had told her, pleasantly, that he did not need any. She had believed him accurately and recorded it accurately. Within five weeks he had stopped eating, and then he was dead.

She ran the check on him, a man she had met and marked and failed to do anything for, and got the result she had got on Lena’s message three days before.

The finding was useful. Her judgment in February had been sound, and she had handed it to the only person in Lowport who could have acted on it, and that person had filed it correctly. She could hold the whole of it and get no more out of it than she got out of the plot on the screen.

She put the sheet back down on the desk.

***

Iris waited while Rourke drank from a cup that had gone cold.

There was a version of the answer she could give in this room and a version she could not. The second required a name she did not have. The closest she could have come would have cost her the appointment and every one after it.

She gave the smallest true version instead.

“They didn’t seek help,” she said. “Not one of them. The man on the bench, the woman with the foot, all of them. Somewhere in each of those there was a moment where an ordinary person turns around and goes back inside, or gets up, or picks up a phone. The thing that would have made them turn around is what was taken.”

Rourke set the cup down.

“That’s not a mechanism,” she said.

“No.”

“It’s a description with a direction on it.” She was not finished, and she did not stop the way a person stops when they have dismissed something. “A description with a direction on it is what I have as well. Mine is that a population with reduced affective response has reduced health-seeking behavior and reduced risk aversion, and dies at an elevated rate as a consequence. That is the same sentence as yours in a register I can publish.”

“Yes.”

“Where mine falls apart,” Rourke said, “is that reduced is not absent. My people aren’t catatonic. They hold jobs. They answer the phone. The woman with the foot came in the first time, and she came in on her own, and she made the appointment herself. So whatever was working then isn’t working now, and I can’t tell you what changed between one and the other, and neither can you.”

“No.”

“Good.” Rourke picked the cup back up. “I’d have thrown you out if you’d said you could.”

***

She wanted a file from the room down the corridor and did not want to send the woman who had brought Iris up, so Iris went with her and stood inside the door while Rourke worked along the shelves.

It was a room the building had not been designed for. The shelving was free-standing, bought in a hurry and run in five rows with just enough space between them. It had been filled from the door end backward, so the oldest material was furthest in and the newest was where you tripped over it. Boxes stood in the aisle in two places with dates on the sides. The window at the far end was blocked by a filing cabinet.

Iris read the shelf ends while she waited. They were labeled by year and then by intake category. One category ran the length of the fourth row and turned the corner onto the fifth. The fifth was two thirds empty because it had not been filled yet.

Four hundred and sixteen people. She had known the number for six minutes and she had understood it as a number. The row was two hundred feet of paper and it was the same fact.

“It’s not indexed,” Rourke said from somewhere in the second aisle, without being asked. “It was indexed until eighteen months ago. Then the caseload went up and the funding didn’t.”

“How far behind.”

“Eleven months.” A drawer went in. “I know where things are. That works until I’m not here.”

***

Back in the office she gave Iris the curve: both lines, the ten weeks, the base rate and the four hundred and sixteen, with the identifying material stripped and each death reduced to a date, an age and a cause. It printed while they stood there and Rourke folded it once and handed it over.

“This is a scientist with a number she can’t explain,” she said, “handing it to the only person who ever offered her a wrong explanation that fit.”

“Understood.”

“Here is what I want. Bring me one before it happens.” Rourke leaned back against the second desk. “Anyone can build a story out of eleven deaths after the fact. I could build four. So bring me a person by name, off your own material, and I will tell you whether they are in my set. That is the only thing I will tell you.”

“And then.”

“And then you tell me what is going to stop in them. Specifically, and roughly when. Not that they will die.” She went on before Iris could take it any further. “Eating. A medication. A dressing they have been told to change. The appointment they keep moving. Name the function.”

“And you go and see them.”

“I go and see them, because that is what I am for, and I do whatever the visit turns out to need, and I do not tell you what I did.”

“Then the intervention takes the result out of the test.”

“The intervention takes the result out of the death. You will have told me what was going to stop. Either it had already started stopping when I got there or it had not. That is the part I can check, and it is the only part I have any business checking.”

“And if I’m wrong.”

“Then you were wrong, and I’ll have learned something, and you won’t get a second appointment.” Rourke shrugged with one shoulder. “That’s what a prediction is for.”

Iris folded the paper again and put it in her jacket.

It was a fair test, and it was not the test she would have designed. Hers would have been cleaner and would have required someone to die inside it. She could see the difference between the two and could not produce a reason to prefer her own. She took Rourke’s.

She was at the door when Rourke spoke again.

“How are you sleeping?”

Iris stopped.

Rourke had asked it in the tone she had used for the woman with the foot. Her weight had come off the desk, and Iris could see the assessment running.

“Six hours,” she said. “Sometimes seven. It’s not broken sleep.”

“Appetite.”

“Unchanged.”

“Are you working?”

“Yes.”

“Well?”

“Yes,” Iris said, and then, because the answer was incomplete and she had decided some time ago that she would not give this particular person incomplete answers, “the work is the only thing that hasn’t changed speed.”

Rourke went to the second desk, wrote three lines on a pad, and turned the pad face down.

“What did you write.”

“When there’s enough of it to be worth telling you,” Rourke said, “I’ll tell you.”
