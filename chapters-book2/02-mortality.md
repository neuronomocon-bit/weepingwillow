Chapter 2 — Mortality

Lowport Public Health occupied the top two floors of a building that had been a bank, and the ground floor was still laid out like one. There was a counter with six positions and two of them staffed. There was a rail where a queue had once been managed. The floor was the original stone and it had been polished by eighty years of people coming in to be dealt with, and the polish ran in a path from the door to the counter and nowhere else.

Iris had asked for the appointment three times. The first request went unanswered, the second produced a form, and the third produced a slot on a Thursday morning at a quarter past nine. No one offers a quarter past nine to a person they intend to spend long with.

She was early by eleven minutes. She spent them reading the noticeboard because it was there and because a room she was going to work in was worth knowing. Two flu clinics. A number for a helpline. A poster about hand hygiene with a corner curling away from the wall, and behind it, showing at the edge, an older poster no one had taken down.

At nine sixteen a woman came down to collect her, and did not introduce herself, and took her up in a lift that had been retrofitted into the stairwell.

***

Rourke’s office was smaller than the one she had been in during the winter, and Iris understood the change without being told, because the previous room had been chosen for meeting people and this one had been chosen for working. There was a desk against the window and a second desk against the wall, and the second desk was where the actual work sat: three screens, a printer, and paper in stacks with the edges squared.

“Sit,” Rourke said.

Iris sat.

Rourke did not sit. She stood at the second desk with one hand on the back of the chair and looked at the middle screen for a while before she turned it.

“You wanted the caseload,” she said. “You’re not getting the caseload. I’m going to show you one line off it and then you’re going to tell me what you think, and I want you to understand before you start that I have not asked you here because I have changed my mind about anything.”

“All right.”

“Say that back.”

“You have not changed your mind about anything.”

“Good.” Rourke turned the screen the rest of the way.

It was a plot with time along the bottom and a count up the side, and there were two lines on it. One was flat and slightly rising, and it had been flat and slightly rising for four years. The other came off it about ten weeks back and went up.

“That’s my cohort,” Rourke said. “Four hundred and sixteen people carrying a presentation I have been calling behavioral flattening for two years. Flat line is what the cohort should do. Any population that size produces deaths. Old people, sick people, people who were already going to die of the thing they had before they ever came to me. Three in ten weeks. That is the number. That is what the model says and the model has been right since I built it.”

“And the second line.”

“Eleven,” Rourke said.

She let it sit there long enough for Iris to do the arithmetic she had already done.

“Eleven in ten weeks,” Rourke said, “in a cohort of four hundred and sixteen, against an expectation of three. It is not a large number. I want to be accurate about that, because it is exactly the size of thing that turns out to be nothing four times out of five. Eleven is what you get from a bad month and a reporting change and one clinician retiring. So I checked the reporting change and there wasn’t one, and I checked the clinician and she didn’t, and I ran the whole thing again against last year with the same filters.”

“And it held.”

“It held.”

***

“I’ll read you the deaths,” Rourke said, “and you will see the problem before I say it.”

She read them from the screen, and she read them at the pace of something she had read enough times to have stopped hearing.

A man of fifty-one had stopped eating. Not refused. Stopped. He had gone eleven days on tea and half a sandwich a neighbor brought him, and when the neighbor finally called someone he was cooperative and pleasant and answered every question, and he had not been aware of the eleven days as a thing that had happened to him. He died in the hospital of an electrolyte imbalance. That is what the certificate says and the certificate is correct.

A woman of thirty-eight had walked into traffic on the Halstead crossing. Not run. There was camera footage and Rourke had watched it eight times. The woman steps off, and a car is coming, and she does not hurry and she does not stop. There is no moment where she reacts to the car. The coroner had gone through it twice looking for the thing that would let him call it deliberate and had not found it, because the woman had come off a shift, was carrying groceries, and had a dentist’s appointment in her calendar for the Tuesday.

A woman of sixty-four had died of sepsis from an infected foot. She had been seen for it. She had been given the antibiotics and the instruction to come back if it got worse. It got worse. She did not come back.

A man of twenty-nine had stopped taking a medication he had been on for nine years, without deciding to, and had not restarted, and had not noticed the difference until the thing the medication was for did what it does.

A man of seventy had sat down on a bench on the harbor road in the first week of February and had not got up.

“That’s five of the eleven,” Rourke said. “The other six are the same. Do you see it.”

“Yes.”

“Say it, please. So I know we are looking at the same thing.”

“Every one of them has a cause,” Iris said.

Rourke put her hand flat on the desk and left it there.

“Every one of them has a cause,” she said. “Every one of those certificates is correct. I could defend all eleven to a review board and I would not have to lie once. There is no signature. There is no toxicology. There is no anatomical finding that any two of them share. If you brought me these eleven and told me nothing else I would file them and go home.”

“The pattern isn’t in the deaths.”

“No,” Rourke said. “It is in who is having them. And I have no mechanism that connects the one to the other. I have been looking for eight weeks, and I am a good enough clinician to know the difference between not having found a thing and the thing not being there. I have not decided which this is.”

She looked at the screen rather than at Iris.

“You should also understand the position I am actually in, because it is worse than the number. I have four hundred and sixteen people who arrived without travelling. They did not spread from a point. They did not cluster around a source. They came up across this city at once, and I could never say why, and I have never once pretended I could, and nothing since the winter has changed my view of it.”

“That was the first thing.”

“That was the first thing.” Rourke turned the screen back toward herself. “This is the second, and the two of them are about the same people. I have been doing this for nineteen years. One unexplained finding is a gap in the literature. Two, on the same cohort, is a mechanism I do not have.”

Rourke went to the second desk and came back with a single sheet, and she did not hand it over.

“In February you gave me a list,” she said. “Nine names. You had no clinical standing, you had no business having half of those names, and you told me they were early. I filed it, and I filed it correctly. An unqualified person handed me a list of patients and told me they were going to get worse. There is one right answer to that and I gave it.”

“I remember.”

“The man who stopped eating is on it.” Rourke put the sheet down on the desk between them, face up, and Iris saw her own handwriting from ten months ago. “Fourth line. You wrote *early, mild, no request for help* next to him, and I read that as a layperson describing someone who was coping.”

“It was.”

“It was also the thing that killed him.” Rourke let that stand for a moment without decorating it. “One out of nine is not a result. I know what one out of nine is. But it is the reason you are sitting in my office instead of getting a fourth unanswered request, and I would rather tell you that than have you work it out on the way home.”

Iris looked at the fourth line of her own handwriting.

She had sat across a table from that man for forty minutes in February. She could produce the room: a kitchen with the extractor fan running because the switch was shared with the light. She had written *no request for help* because he had told her, pleasantly, that he did not need any, and she had believed him accurately and recorded it accurately, and ten months later he had gone eleven days without eating and had not been aware of it.

She ran the check. There was a place where something should have arrived about a man she had met and marked and failed to do anything for, and she went and looked at it, and it was smooth.

The finding was useful. It meant her judgment in February had been sound, and the reason it had produced nothing was that she had handed it to the only person in Lowport who could have acted on it and that person had filed it correctly. Both halves were true and neither was anyone’s fault, and she could hold the whole of it and get no more out of it than she got out of the plot on the screen.

She put the sheet back down on the desk.

***

Iris looked at the plot again while Rourke drank cold coffee from a cup she had clearly poured that morning.

There was a version of the answer she could give in this room and a version she could not. The second one required the word for the thing, and she had no word for the thing, and the closest she could have come would have cost her the appointment and every appointment after it.

She said the smallest true thing instead.

“They didn’t seek help,” she said. “Not one of them. The man on the bench, the woman with the foot, all of them. Somewhere in each of those there was a moment where an ordinary person turns around and goes back inside, or gets up, or picks up a phone. The thing that would have made them turn around is what was taken.”

Rourke set the cup down.

“That is not a mechanism,” she said.

“No.”

“It is a description with a direction on it.” She said it without heat, and she was not finished, and she did not stop the way a person stops when they have dismissed something. “A description with a direction on it is what I have as well. Mine is that a population with reduced affective response has reduced health-seeking behavior and reduced risk aversion, and dies at an elevated rate as a consequence. That is the same sentence as yours in a register I can publish.”

“Yes.”

“Where mine falls apart,” Rourke said, “is that reduced is not absent. My people are not catatonic. They hold jobs. They answer the phone. The woman with the foot came in the first time, and she came in on her own, and she made the appointment herself. So the thing that was working then is not working now, and I cannot tell you what changed between one and the other, and neither can you.”

“No.”

“Good.” Rourke picked the cup back up. “I would have thrown you out if you had said you could.”

***

She wanted a file from the room down the corridor and did not want to send the woman from downstairs for it, so Iris went with her and stood inside the door while Rourke worked along the shelves.

It was a room the building had not been designed for. The shelving was the free-standing kind that gets bought in a hurry, run in four rows with just enough space between them, and it had been filled from the door end backward, so the oldest material was furthest in and the newest was where you tripped over it. Boxes stood in the aisle in two places with dates on the sides. The window at the far end had been blocked by a filing cabinet someone had put there and no one had moved.

Iris read the shelf ends while she waited. They were labeled by year and then by intake category, and one category ran along the fourth row and then turned the corner onto the fifth, and the fifth row was two thirds empty because it had not been filled yet.

Four hundred and sixteen people. She had known the number for six minutes and she had understood it as a number. The row was two hundred feet of paper and it was the same fact.

“It’s not indexed,” Rourke said from somewhere in the second aisle, without being asked. “It was indexed until eighteen months ago. Then the caseload went up and the funding didn’t.”

“How far behind.”

“Eleven months.” A drawer went in. “I know where things are. That works until I’m not here.”

***

Back in the office she gave Iris the curve.

Not the files. The plot, both lines, the ten weeks, the base rate, and the four hundred and sixteen, with the identifying material stripped and the deaths reduced to a date and an age and a cause. It printed while they stood there and Rourke folded it once and handed it over.

“That is not a collaboration,” she said. “It is a scientist with a number she cannot explain, handing it to the only person who ever offered her a wrong explanation that fit.”

“Understood.”

“Here is what I want. Bring me one before it happens.” Rourke leaned back against the second desk. “Anybody can build a story out of eleven deaths after the fact. I could build four. Bring me a name off my own cohort and tell me that person is going to die in the next six weeks, and tell me why, and then let us both wait and see. If you can do that we are having a completely different conversation and I will give you everything in that room.”

“And if I’m wrong.”

“Then you were wrong, and I will have learned something, and you will not get a fourth appointment.” Rourke shrugged with one shoulder. “I am not being hard on you. That is just what a prediction is for.”

Iris folded the paper again and put it in her jacket. It was a fair test. It was better than fair, because it was the test she would have designed, and she was aware that Rourke had designed it knowing that.

She was at the door when Rourke spoke again.

“How are you sleeping?”

Iris stopped.

The question had not come out of politeness. Rourke had asked it in the tone she had used for the woman with the foot, and she was standing differently, and her weight had come off the desk. The assessment was running. Iris could see it running.

“Six hours,” she said. “Sometimes seven. It’s not broken sleep.”

“Appetite.”

“Unchanged.”

“Are you working?”

“Yes.”

“Well?”

“Yes,” Iris said, and then, because the answer was incomplete and she had decided some time ago that she would not give this particular person incomplete answers, “the work is the only thing that hasn’t changed speed.”

Rourke went to the second desk, wrote three lines on a pad, and turned the pad face down.

“What did you write?”

“When there’s enough of it to be worth telling you,” Rourke said, “I’ll tell you.”
