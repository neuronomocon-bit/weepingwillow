Chapter 7 — The Curve

Kade had called twice on Friday and once at seven that morning, and Iris had let all three go, because she had nothing to give him yet and had learned in the winter what happened when she talked before she had it. He got half a finding out of her voice and then carried it around for two days while she worked out that it had been the wrong half. It was cheaper for both of them if she waited.

She had three sets of material on the wall by nine and none of them had been built to sit next to the others.

Rourke’s printout was on the left, folded twice and flattened out again, both lines on it, the ten weeks, the base rate, the four hundred and sixteen. Under it she had pinned the redacted summaries from the winter, the ones Rourke had put on a tablet in February and had never asked for back, printed now because paper can be moved around a wall and a screen cannot.

Her own map was in the middle, where it had been since January. One line, no branches, the markers along it in her own hand.

The Meridian material was on the right and it was larger than the other two by a factor she had stopped estimating. Four hundred and eleven session records. Seventy-two months of consumption logs. The substrate group’s whole operational archive: maintenance logs, calibration records, consumables, shift handovers, two years of a small team keeping a machine inside its parameters and writing down that they had.

The three sets did not agree about anything, including what a case was. Rourke counted people who had presented to a clinician. Iris counted people she had sat across a table from. Meridian had counted nothing at all, because Meridian had not known there was anything to count.

She made coffee and stood at the window while it went cold, and got back to it at the point on the list where the list said to.

***

The first drive off the index had finished on Wednesday and she ran it before she started, because it took nine minutes and because there was a version of the day where it made everything else unnecessary.

Eleven percent came back readable, and the read errors distributed exactly as she had told Kade they would, in bands across the surface with the outer tracks worst. Eleven percent of a set that maps numbers to people demonstrates that the media can still be read. It does not produce a name.

She ran what she had against the identifiers she had anyway. Seventy-eight of the fragment’s records carried enough structure to resolve a field, and none of them was MN-R-0338, and there was no reason any of them should have been. The fragment was a strip off one drive of eleven, and the eleven were a fraction of the stack, and the stack had spent a winter in water.

She logged the null, put the second drive on, and went back to the wall.

***

The first pass took four hours and produced a curve, and she spent the next two establishing that the curve was hers rather than the world’s.

The measure was the problem. Magnitude was not in the record anywhere, because no one except her had a reason to think the thing had one. So she built the measure herself, coarsely, on the principle that a coarse measure applied identically beats a fine one applied unevenly. Five bands, defined in advance, written on a card before she scored a single case, together with the rule for what to do about a case she scored differently twice, so that she could not adjust a definition or a rule to fit what she was finding.

Band one was a single function reduced. Band two was more than one, with the life around it still running. Band three was the life around it no longer running. Band four was self-preservation gone, the band that killed people slowly and the one Rourke’s eleven had come out of. Band five was everything, at once, with the body still running.

Then she scored two hundred and six cases against date, and the plot came up as a line going up.

She looked at it for a while. Then she took the top of it apart, because the top was where the answer lived and the top was almost entirely hers.

The high scores were her own cases. Joel, whom she had interviewed for forty minutes in a kitchen with the extractor fan running because the switch was shared with the light. Lila Mercer. Caleb, three times. The two out of the outer zone. She had assessed those people in rooms, in person, with the whole picture in front of her, and had scored them off her own notes, and her own notes ran to pages. Rourke’s summaries ran to four lines with the identifying material stripped out.

A richer record scores higher, and what that measures is the recorder.

Her cases were also the recent ones, because she had only started in January.

She sat with that. The curve she had built could be produced in full by a woman who had begun paying attention nine weeks ago and had gotten better at it since, and nothing in the first pass could tell that apart from an escalation.

She wrote *first pass is contaminated, and by me* on a card, pinned it where she would see it, and started again.

***

The second pass used one source.

She dropped every case she had assessed herself, including the man on the north side, and scored only from Rourke’s redacted summaries. Rourke had given her the set entire in February, consecutive and with nothing lifted out: one clinician, one house style, four lines each, the same amount of information about every person in it. A set someone had chosen from would have been worth nothing, and she would have had no way to tell. A hundred and sixty-eight summaries, of which a hundred and thirty-one had all four lines filled in. Fewer than she wanted, and enough to work with.

She rescored all of them blind to the date, and blind to the date meant more than a strip of card over the date column. The summaries had reached her in the order Rourke’s system produced them, and that order was itself a date. So she gave every case a temporary number, shuffled the numbers, and worked the set in whatever order the shuffle handed her. She did the whole thing twice on separate evenings, reshuffled in between, and did not put either pass against anything until both were closed.

Her two passes agreed on a hundred and twenty-four of the hundred and thirty-one. The seven that disagreed she scored down, because the card said to and because a case you cannot place twice is a case arguing against your own finding.

It cost her most of two days. She could not have said, on Thursday, why she was doing it in the order she was doing it in, except that doing it in any other order would have produced a number she could not defend to a woman who had been doing this for nineteen years and had told her exactly what a prediction was for.

Then she took the card off the date column.

The line went up in steps.

There were runs of cases sitting at one band. Then a short interval with nothing in it. Then a run at the next band, longer or shorter, and another interval, and another band. The runs varied. The intervals did not vary much at all.

She measured them. Nineteen days. Twenty-three. Seventeen.

Then a stretch of a hundred and ten days with nothing in it whatsoever, in the second summer, which she circled and left alone, because she had no account of it and inventing one would have cost her everything else on the page.

After the gap the steps resumed at the band they had stopped at, and went on climbing, and the highest band Rourke’s set could show her was four.

Band five was a man in a chair on the north side, and it was not in this data, and it was never going to be, and she knew precisely why.

She put him back on the page in a different color, because he came off different evidence and the entire point of the last two days had been not to mix them. The interval from the last band-four case in Rourke’s set to Thursday morning was twenty-six days. That was one number off one man and it did not belong to the series.

It sat where the series said it would.

Then she put her own map against it, because Rourke’s set could tell her when and how much and could not tell her whether she was looking at one thing.

Her line had been built out of sites and dates and physical proximity and had nothing to do with clinical scoring. She laid the two against each other by date and worked down them together. The line moved when the curve produced cases. The line did not move when the curve did not. That held all the way along, for nineteen months, on two records that had no reason to agree at all, through too many changes on both sides to put down to accident.

It also did not move during the hundred and ten days.

She went back to that twice. Rourke’s set going quiet for a summer had four ordinary explanations and she could name all of them: a clinician on leave, a district that stopped referring, a change in what got coded, a run of people who simply never went to a doctor. Her own map going quiet in the same window could not be any of those, because her map was built from different evidence by a different person for a different purpose. The ordinary explanations for either record going quiet did not explain both of them going quiet in the same window.

So the gap was real. It belonged to the thing rather than to the record of it, and she had no account of it whatsoever.

She wrote the finding out longhand, because a thing written by hand has to be said word by word.

*Increases are stepwise. Each step holds across a run of subjects, then increases again. Intervals between increases are consistent. Something is establishing a result, holding it long enough to be sure of it, and then increasing.*

She read that back.

Then she wrote three words underneath it, looked at them, and could not account for them.

*A dosage schedule.*

She had not decided to write it. The phrase had been available, fully formed and correct, sitting in the place where the description belonged, and her hand had put it down before she finished considering whether it was hers. She had a doorframe full of cards about precisely this, and a rule she had made in the second week for precisely this, and the rule was that a thing arriving without an account of itself gets tested rather than trusted.

So she tested it. She went back through the band definitions, and the intervals, and the hundred and ten days, looking for the part of the description the phrase was doing badly.

There was not one. It described the whole of it, in the fewest words available, and it was accurate, and she could not say where she had gotten it.

She let it stand and moved the card to the doorframe, onto the row that held measurements, where it did not belong. There was no row for this.

***

The curve gave her Rourke’s test and took it away again in the same hour.

*Bring me one before it happens.* A name off her own cohort, a reason, and then both of them waiting to see. Iris had understood the terms in the room and had thought about them most days since, because they were the terms she would have set.

What she had now was better than she had expected and useless in the specific way the test cared about. The intervals gave her when. Nineteen, twenty-three, seventeen, twenty-six, and the last increase behind her by fourteen days, which put the next one inside two weeks and probably inside ten days. The bands gave her how much: whatever came next would come in above what Rourke had ever recorded, because the north side had already gone past the top of her scale.

It did not give her who.

She went looking for that for three hours and found nothing that sorted. She ran the hundred and thirty-one against age and got a spread that matched the city. Against sex, nothing. Against district, a scatter with no center and no path through it. She had already known that from her own map, because her own map had one line on it and the line went everywhere. Against referral route, against household composition, against the presenting complaint Rourke’s clinicians had written down first: nothing, nothing, and nothing.

There was a criterion. A hundred and thirty-one people had been selected out of a city of two hundred thousand and something had done the selecting, and whatever it sorted on had never been written into a field.

So she could tell Rourke that a person would be taken past the top of her scale within about ten days, and could not tell her which person, and a prediction with the name left out was not a prediction. It was a forecast about weather. Rourke would file it correctly, the way she had filed the nine names in February, and she would be right to.

Iris wrote it down as it stood, because a negative result is a result.

*Predicts magnitude. Predicts date. Does not predict subject. Not sufficient for R.*

She put herself on the plot before she closed it, as a point outside the sample, because leaving herself off would have been a decision and she did not want to find out later that she had made one.

Her date was January. Her band was two, and she could defend two, because she had scored it off the definition on the card rather than off how it felt, and how it felt was not evidence.

January, band two, three increases below the top of the range, in a set she was not otherwise part of.

It told her nothing she could use. She wrote it down and pinned it with the rest, because the record was the point, and went back to the archive.

***

The Meridian material gave her the other end of it that afternoon, and it gave it to her in the least dramatic manner available: a text search returning four hits.

She had been running the operational archive for durations all week. It was the only measure that crossed all three sets, because a duration is a duration whether you are recording a lab check or a conversation on a landing, and she had spent five weeks recording her own.

The access log for the substrate lab gave her a routine check on a Tuesday night, entered at 01:52 and logged complete at 02:19.

Twenty-seven minutes. The same check, run by the same technician, appeared eleven times in the preceding four months at between seven and nine minutes.

No incident report was attached to it, because nothing had happened that anyone had a category for. The check had been performed. The parameters were in range. The log said complete, and the log was correct.

Two days later, at the bottom of a night-shift handover, in the free-text box where a supervisor writes whatever he thinks the next man should know, there was this:

*Pell easier to work with this week. Didn’t raise anything on handover two nights running, which is a first. Long may it continue.*

Iris read it four times.

It was the earliest observation of the effect from the outside that she had, and she had spent three weeks looking for an earlier one. It was a compliment. It had been written by a man filling in a form at the end of a night shift with his mind mostly on getting home, and it was, as far as she could establish from anything else in that archive, the nicest thing anyone had ever written down about Pell.

She checked the date against everything else she had.

Three and a half years ago. Four months before the building shut, inside the building, on the lower level, at two o’clock in the morning. The earliest case in Rourke’s whole set came eighteen months after it, out in the city, four miles away, in a woman who presented to her own doctor because her husband made her.

Between those two dates there was nothing, and the nothing had a shape she could not read. A building closed with something inside it. Then, eighteen months later, people.

She had a surname, and that was the part she sat with longest, and it took her a while to work out why it was sitting with her at all.

The substrate group’s records used names, because they were staff. Pell had a name and a rotation and a supervisor who thought he had gotten easier to work with. Everything on the patient side of that building was a number, and she had been reading one of those numbers for a week and could not have told anyone who it belonged to.

She wrote *Pell, night rotation, staff* on a card and put it with the others, and did not write down that he was the first, because she was not certain and would not be until the index came up. A thing written down before it is certain gets read later as though it always had been.

***

She called Kade at twenty past six and he picked up on the first tone.

“Say it,” he said.

“I have three things and they go together. I want to give you all three before you ask about any of them.”

“Go on.”

“The first is that it takes more than it used to. A hundred and thirty-one cases, scored off one source in one format, with the dates covered while I scored them. The amount taken increases with time. That is not an artifact of my attention. I built it once out of my own cases and threw that version away for exactly that reason.”

“All right.”

“The second is how it increases. It goes up in steps. It holds a step across a run of people, and then it goes up again, and the intervals between the increases are consistent.” She had the card in front of her and did not need it. “Nineteen days. Twenty-three. Seventeen. Then twenty-six to the man on the north side, and that last one is off different evidence and is not part of the set. Something is holding a level long enough to see what it produces, and then raising it.”

Kade said nothing for a moment. She could hear him somewhere with a hard floor.

“And the third.”

“The third is that it has a starting point, and the starting point is inside Meridian.” She looked at the card with the surname on it. “A technician’s routine lab check three and a half years ago took twenty-seven minutes instead of eight, four months before that building shut. Two days after it his supervisor wrote in a handover log that he had gotten easier to work with. The earliest case in Rourke’s set is eighteen months later and four miles away.”

“You’re saying it started in there.”

“I am saying the record starts in there. And then it stops for eighteen months and I cannot read the eighteen months at all. After that it runs to a man in a chair on the north side on Thursday morning with one gap in it, a hundred and ten days in the second summer, and I cannot read that either.”

She stopped and let him have it.

“So it isn’t only moving,” Kade said. “It isn’t only taking.”

“It is taking more. It is taking more on a schedule. And a thing that raises a dose, after holding the last one long enough to see what it did, is a thing that has decided the last one did not work.” She looked at the card on the doorframe. “It is a dosage schedule.”

“That’s a medical word.”

“I know.”

The hard floor went quiet.

“What does that make it,” Kade said.

Iris looked at the wall. Rourke’s printout on the left with its eleven defensible deaths. Her own map in the middle with its single line. The Meridian material on the right, four hundred and eleven records deep, every one of them filed under a number, and a man’s surname sitting on top of all of it in her own handwriting.

“It makes it a method,” she said.
