Chapter 15 — RECOVERED: The Map

Terri had the closure pack on the counter before Marin got to it.

“I pulled it when it came through the queue,” she said. “I thought you would rather it was sitting here than have to come and ask me for it.”

“Thank you.”

“There’s no hurry on it. It sits for ninety days and nobody chases it.” She squared the corner of the folder with two fingers and left it where Marin could reach. “I was sorry about your sister. She was funny with me on the intake day and I have thought about it since.”

“She was funny with everyone.”

Terri put her glasses back down out of her hair, and the phone went on the desk behind her, and she answered it, and that was the end of it.

It was the last conversation Marin had in that building in which anyone treated her as a person whose sister had died. Nothing after it was unkind. The place simply went back to being a workplace, at the speed a workplace goes back.

***

Deceased-patient closure is nine pages and most of it confirms things the system already knows.

The clinical record closes and is retained under the clinical schedule, which is thirty years and is not negotiable. The working set is purged: the session material, the intermediate states, the operator notes, and anything the substrate was holding across the course. The outcome data is retained in anonymized form, because the outcome data is the entire point of running a program at all. Then someone with the authority signs the front sheet and the file stops being live.

Marin ran it herself.

She could have asked Feld’s team and they would have done it correctly and would not have minded being asked. She sat in the small office off records with the folder open and worked out that having someone else purge her sister’s working set would be worse than doing it herself. She was right about that.

The purge takes four minutes and it goes oldest first.

She watched it because leaving the room while it ran seemed like a way of pretending it was not happening. The terminal listed the items as it took them. Baseline consent, executed, retained under a different schedule and therefore skipped. Session one, the intermediate states for session one, the operator notes for session one. Session two. The notes were Feld’s and they were four to six lines each and Marin had never read them, because the clinical file was not hers, and she did not read them now either. She watched the line count go down.

By three minutes there was nothing left before the twelfth.

The twelfth was the one where Willow had made a joke at about nineteen minutes and Feld had taken a second too long to catch it. Marin had watched that recording four times in September. It went first, and then the note that recorded it, and then the intermediate states, in the order the system does it.

The confirmations scrolled past faster than she could read them. Then the list was empty, and the terminal said the working set had been purged, and asked her whether she wanted a copy of the log.

She said yes, because that is what you do.

Before the summary there were the flush confirmations, one per session, because the form asks the substrate to certify that it is no longer holding anything of the patient’s and the substrate answers in numbers.

Twelve lines. Residual coherence as a percentage of session load, measured at ninety minutes, against a tolerance of two percent.

Marin read all twelve. The highest was point four one and the lowest was point three eight, and the mean of them sat inside a hundredth of the mean of every other course the program had ever run, and every one of the twelve sat at roughly a fifth of the tolerance.

She ticked the box that said the flush had completed, and the box was correctly ticked. A number inside tolerance is a number inside tolerance, and the entire reason a tolerance exists is so that no one is asked to make a judgment about the difference between point three eight and point four one at nine in the morning with a folder open in front of them.

Virek had been bringing her the same figure since the spring. It had a name in her head by now, which was the shelf, and it sat where it sat and did not move and was not a finding.

Feld had signed her section three days earlier. Her closing note was two lines and Marin could see the first four words of it in the preview pane and did not open it, because the clinical file had never been hers and being the last living relative did not change the schedule it sat under.

At the end there was a summary. Working set purged. Outcome data retained. Clinical record closed and retained.

She read it three times. That was twice more than it needed.

The outcome data would go into the pooled results under the identifier, with no name on it, and would sit in the denominator of every figure the program published from here on. In eighteen months someone at a conference would put up a slide with four hundred and something in the corner of it. Her sister would be inside that number. Marin had built the pooling rule as well, and it is the reason the results mean anything.

***

The front sheet says the working set has been purged. Marin was not going to sign a sentence she had not verified, and verification means going and looking, so she took the folder down to the lower level.

It was four degrees colder down there and always had been. She put the identifier into the archive terminal expecting a short list of nothing.

The list was not short.

The purge covers the working set, and the working set is the treatment. A baseline map is not treatment material and never has been. It goes in at intake, before there is a course for it to belong to, and it sits in the research archive under the research schedule, which is fifteen years, because research data is kept.

Marin had written the paragraph that put it there. Not the number itself, since that came from the funder, but the two hundred words explaining which category things fell into and why, in the second year, on the page after the one about next of kin.

MN-R-0338. Baseline. Complete, one session, resident.

She looked at that line for longer than it took to read.

Then she opened the consent record, because there was a version of this in which the retention was an oversight and someone could be asked to fix it. It was not an oversight. Page six, initialed in the box, and Willow had asked two questions about that page and had been given accurate answers to both of them and had initialed it afterward rather than before.

***

She had said, at that table, with the pack open between them, that a version of Willow’s data would exist in a system she had access to, and that she would not be opening it, and that there was a log which would show it if she did.

Willow had asked whether anyone would read the log.

Marin had told her probably not, because that was true, and because the alternative was saying something reassuring that was not.

She sat in the cold in front of the terminal and put both of those things next to each other.

What she had come down for was verification. The front sheet made a claim and she had to know whether the claim was accurate, and knowing meant opening the record and looking at its state, and there was no procedure anywhere in the building that let a person confirm the condition of a file without opening it. Every part of that was true and none of it had been true when she made the promise, because when she made the promise her sister had been alive and sitting across a table putting her thumb over a barcode.

She opened it.

The log wrote a line. She did not see the line, because the log is not displayed, but she knew the field it went into and the format it went in, and she had specified both.

***

It came up whole.

The baseline map is the highest-resolution product the program makes, because everything afterward gets measured against it. It is taken slowly, across most of a day, by a technician with good hands who does not rush, on a patient who has not yet had anything done to them.

Every emotional structure her sister had was in it. Not descriptions of them and not summaries of them and not a clinician’s account of them. The structures themselves, indexed and addressable, at a resolution that exists nowhere else in the world and cannot be produced by any other route.

The target structure was in there at full charge, sitting where it had always sat, with nine years of load still on it.

Marin had spent twelve sessions taking that charge down and had watched the number fall every week and had been right to. What was on the screen was the version from before any of that had been done. It was the state of her sister on the fourteenth of the month at ten in the morning, with the fog on the window and the water going under the floor, holding all of it.

She did not do anything.

She sat with her coat still on and looked at a complete map of a person for a length of time she did not measure, and the room stayed at four degrees below the rest of the building, and the fans ran.

Somewhere in the middle of it she found that she had been reading the index rather than looking at the render, in the way she would have read anyone’s. She had got as far as the structures laid down before Willow was nine, the ones no one remembers acquiring. They were all there and they were all legible and she could have named the function of each one out loud.

She stopped reading and sat back and put her hands in her coat pockets.

***

The technicality took about nine seconds.

Closure purges the working set. The baseline is not in the working set. It is research data under a separate schedule in a separate archive, and the form upstairs made no claim about it in either direction. Purging it was not part of the procedure she had come to carry out. It was also not hers to purge, because the research archive belongs to about ten people, and she had said that out loud across a table with a consent pack open on it and had meant it as a reassurance at the time.

Every step of that was true.

There was also a route. Research material can be withdrawn on the request of the participant or of the executor of their estate, and Marin was the executor, and the request is a single side of A4 that goes to the archive custodian with a reason written in the box at the bottom. Simon would have processed it inside a week and would not have asked her a single question about it. She knew the form number. She had approved its wording.

So the accurate version was that purging it was not part of closure, and was not hers to do unilaterally, and was entirely available to her by a procedure she had helped write, and would take about ten minutes.

She built the shorter version standing up, in the cold, in about nine seconds, and she was aware of building it while she was building it, and not one of the steps in it got weaker for having been watched.

She logged out and went upstairs and signed the front sheet.

The signature confirmed that the working set had been purged. The working set had been purged.

She left at twenty to six with the rest of them and drove home in traffic and stopped for milk on the way, because there was no milk.

The evening was ordinary. She ate at the table with the radio on and did not take work out of the bag. At some point she washed up and dried the two things that do not go in the rack, and stood at the sink afterward with the cloth in her hand for a while, and then hung the cloth over the tap and went and sat down.

Nothing happened.

What had changed was that the file existed and she had seen the state of it, and there was no available arrangement of the rest of her life in which she went back to not knowing that.

***

The inquest sat on the Thursday of that week and took forty minutes and returned what the report had returned. Marin gave evidence for six of the forty and was asked three questions, all of them about dates, and answered all three accurately. No one asked her what her sister had been treated for and there was no reason anyone would have.

She did not go back down for eleven days.

She went to work and did the work. She signed a consumables order and chaired the Thursday meeting and rewrote a section of a protocol that had been annoying her since March. Virek brought her the shelf on the Tuesday and it was still at zero point four and they logged it. She slept badly and recorded that she slept badly.

She did not open the archive terminal. She did not think about it in any way she could have written down. On two separate occasions she noticed that she had not thought about it, and noticing is a different thing, and she recorded that as well, in a notebook, in a sentence with nothing on the end of it.

On the twelfth day she came in at ten past seven, went down to the lower level before the clinical wing opened, and put the identifier in again.

It came up the same. Complete, resident, unchanged, because nothing had happened to it and nothing was scheduled to happen to it for another fifteen years.

She looked at it for four or five minutes.

Then she logged out, and went upstairs, and started her day, and the log had two lines in it now, and no one read the log.

***

The clinical index came up on the Sunday, three days after Lila Mercer.

Not all of it. Sixty-one percent of the allocation table, with a fragment count on the remainder that told Iris the rest was either a winter’s work or was nothing. Sixty-one was enough, because what she wanted out of it was one row.

She had been carrying MN-R-0338 since the third week of March. It had come off a researcher’s working notes attached to a file that should not have had working notes attached to it, and she had read a woman’s entire course of treatment against it with no name on the front.

She ran the join.

It returned a man. Forty-four at intake, veterans’ cohort, course opened and closed out inside eleven months, and the whole of it sitting about three years before anything she had been reading. She checked the dates twice. The join was clean and the answer was wrong, and a clean join with a wrong answer is a specific condition with a name.

So she asked the index the second question. It was whether the number had ever belonged to anyone else. It had. The identifier had gone back into the pool when the allocation hold on it expired, which is a shorter clock than the one the record itself sits under, and it had been issued again, and the supersession flag that should have taken the closed record off the live table had never been set. The old record was sitting on the index in perfect condition, because nothing had touched it since the day it closed.

The live one was on the part of the media that had corroded.

She rebuilt the second mapping off the degraded slice. It took the rest of the afternoon and most of the evening, and she recovered the row in four passes with two fields she could not fill and one she filled from a checksum.

The name came up clean.

Willow Halloway.

Iris wrote it on a card, because writing it down was the procedure and the procedure did not have a step in it for anything else.

Then she sat in front of the card.

The room was the same. The rig was running on the bench with the fan going. Outside, a car went past and did not stop.

She checked, because checking was also a procedure, and she gave it the length of time she gave things now.

Location: nothing. Duration: none. Intensity: none.

She put the card above the doorframe with the others.
