Chapter 11 — Caleb

The phone went at ten past two and the screen said Caleb, and Iris looked at it for most of a second before she answered, because Caleb did not call.

He messaged. He had messaged her perhaps forty times since the winter and called her twice, and both of those had been about a job with a gate code on it.

“Caleb.”

“There’s someone here,” he said.

He said it the way he gave her freight times. Iris was already standing. She put the phone on speaker and set it on the table so that both her hands were free, and the part of her that did that noted she had done it before the part of her that understood the sentence.

“Where.”

“In the apartment. She came in about a minute ago.”

“Is the door open.”

“The door is open.” He gave her the next part without being asked for it. “I let her in.”

“Describe her.”

“A woman. Not tall.” He was quiet for a second and it was not the quiet of a man being frightened, it was the quiet of a man reading a gauge. “I can’t give you the face. I’m looking at it.”

“Caleb. Go out of the apartment. Go down the stairs and out onto the road and stay there and stay on the phone.”

“All right,” he said, and did not move, because there was nothing in the sentence that made moving more correct than not moving, and she heard the room stay exactly as quiet as it had been.

“Are you walking.”

“No.”

“Why not.”

He considered it. The consideration took about as long as it should have.

“I don’t have a reason to,” he said.

Then the line was open and no one was speaking on the other end of it, and it stayed like that for eleven seconds, and then it closed.

***

Iris put her jacket on and took the van keys off the hook and stopped at the light switch on her way past, because the card was there and the card was information.

*Caleb. Three weeks.*

The date on the back of it was Day 42. She had written it on Day 21 in her own hand. She had put the date on the back so that she could not talk herself out of it later. Day 42 had been a Tuesday, and on the Tuesday she had spent four hours and forty minutes performing a sister for Lena, and she had not gone.

Three days. The check had been due and had not happened, and Caleb had called on the third day after it, and she stood in the doorway for the length of time it took to hold those three facts next to each other and get nothing out of them at all.

She wrote it on a card before she went, because it was a measurement and measurements went on the wall.

*Scheduled check Day 42, not made. Subject called Day 45.*

Then she went down to the van.

***

The road inland out of the harbor runs through the older blocks and then past the salvage yard and then straight for four miles, and Iris had driven it enough times not to have to think about any of it.

She let her attention go loose at the front of her skull about a mile in, because there was nothing else to do with the drive, and because she had learned in the second week that holding it there was the only way the reading came clean.

It came up at the rail crossing.

Pressure, frontal, as it always was. She had felt it eleven times since January and had written down all eleven. The worst of them had been at the wall in her own apartment with the map in front of her, and it had been brief, and she had recorded the intensity as minimal because she had nothing to compare it against.

This one arrived at the level the eleventh had peaked at and went on climbing.

She noted it and kept driving. At the yards it was past anything on her scale and still rising. It had stopped being a sensation with edges on it and become a fact about the direction she was pointed in. She understood, correctly and completely, that she was driving toward the strongest reading she had ever taken.

The instruction to hurry arrived four seconds later.

She was through the intersection by then. The intersection was where the industrial road cut across and took two minutes off the run to the outer yards, and she had used it in January and in February and had used it on the trip out to Caleb in March, and she went past it at forty with her hands correct on the wheel and the turn behind her before the reason to take it caught up.

She did not brake. Braking at that point would have cost her more than the two minutes.

She wrote the interval down at the next light, because she was going to want it and because writing it down was the only part of it available to her.

*Retrieval, four seconds, route decision under load. Longest functional delay.*

The reading went on rising the whole of the last two miles, and she drove inside the limit, and arrived.

***

Caleb’s building was the converted block near the outer maintenance yards, three stories, brick painted over twice in colors that had both failed. The entry code was unchanged. She had noticed that in January and had noticed it again in March and it was unchanged now.

There was a woman at the far end of the lot, walking away toward the fence line, not hurrying.

Iris got the distance at about ninety meters. Dark coat, dark hair, a build that was ordinary in every direction. She was too far off for a face and the light was flat and the fog had come in off the water as it did in the afternoons, and none of that was the reason Iris could not describe her.

She had seen her twice before. Both of those had been closer than this.

Something in the woman had settled that had not been settled before, and Iris could not have said which part of her it was, and she stood in the lot with the van door open and made herself write down the only three things she could defend.

*Ninety meters. Walking. Not hurrying.*

The pressure was falling off the whole time she wrote it.

By the time she looked up the woman had reached the fence and was past the corner of the next building and there was nothing to look at, and Iris took the stairs two at a time and did not go after her, because there was a man on the second floor and the woman was already gone and only one of those was still available.

***

The door was open the width of a hand.

“Caleb.”

“In here,” he said.

He was in the chair by the window with his back to the door, and he had not turned around when she came in, and when she came around in front of him he looked up at her and his face did what a face does when a person it knows comes into the room.

“Iris.”

“Yes.”

“You came out.”

“You called me.”

“I did.” He said it as a confirmation. There was a cup on the counter behind him with nothing in it, and the cupboard above it was open, and the second cup was down off the shelf and standing beside the first one, and neither of them had been used.

She got the other chair and put it in front of him and sat down in it, close, knees almost touching his, because that was the position that let her see his eyes and his hands at the same time.

“I’m going to ask you things,” she said.

“All right.”

She ran the assessment.

Sleep. Memory. Appetite. Pain. The small structural questions in the order that does not sound like an order. He answered all of them. Six hours. No gaps he was aware of. He had eaten at eleven and could tell her what. No pain anywhere, and he checked before he said it, and the checking took the length of time that checking takes.

The answers came clean and immediate and correct, and not one of them ran past the edge of the question.

“Do you know what happened.”

“Yes.”

“Tell me.”

“She came in. She sat down there.” He looked at the arm of the couch, which was two feet from the chair. “She put her hand on my chest. It was there for a while. Then she took it off and stood up and left, and I stayed here.”

“How long was her hand on you.”

“I don’t have that.”

“Estimate.”

“I could give you a number and it wouldn’t be worth anything.” Which was true, and was the correct answer, and was the answer a good technician gives. “Longer than a minute.”

“Did it hurt.”

“No.”

“Did you want her to stop.”

He looked at that one for a moment, as he had looked at the freight schedule in January when she had asked him a question he had not been asked before.

“No,” he said.

Iris wrote it down.

“Caleb. I want you to tell me what is different since she came in.”

And he did. He told her as he would have told a duty manager about a shift, in the order it had happened, without a single word in it doing anything except carrying information.

He said that at the start of the afternoon there had been a list of what he had to do that week and now there was still a list and he could read it. He said that the freight change on the northern run was still a problem and he could still describe the problem and could no longer say what he thought about the problem. He said that his sister’s number was in his phone and he knew it was hers. He said that he had been sitting in the chair since the woman left because sitting in the chair had not yet been superseded by anything.

He said that he had noticed all of it in the first minute and had not written it down.

“Why not,” Iris said.

“I couldn’t see what it would be for.”

“I’m writing it down.”

“Good,” he said. “Someone should have the times.”

He gave her the times. He had the minute the door went and the minute the woman sat down and the minute she left, and he had them to the minute because he had been looking at the stove clock for the whole of it, and he did not say why he had been looking at the stove clock and Iris did not ask, because there were two answers and only one of them was worth having and she was not going to be able to tell which she had got.

Every sentence of it was accurate. He was a man giving a clear account of a system he was inside, at the speed he had always given accounts, and he had told her more in ninety seconds than Rourke’s four hundred and sixteen had told her in a year.

She reached for the card in her jacket with the bands on it and did not take it out, because she had written the definitions in advance and did not need to read them.

Band five was everything, at once, with the body still running.

She had built the band nine days ago off a man on the north side she had never met, from a paramedic’s account and a room she had stood in for eleven minutes, and she had defined it as the ceiling of a scale because a scale needs one and not because she had expected to use it.

*Band five*, she wrote. She wrote the date and the time next to it and nothing else, because nothing else was scoreable and she had rules about that.

***

His hands were cooler than they had been in January. She had not been checking for that and she found she had noticed it anyway, at some point in the last few minutes, and she put the back of her fingers against the inside of his wrist and counted for thirty seconds and doubled it.

“Forty-four,” she said.

“That’s low.”

“Yes.”

She took the other reading while she had her fingers on his wrist, because taking one had put her in the way of taking the other. The place where something should have started was smooth. It had been smooth in the café in January and smooth in the lot on the north side nine days ago and it was smooth now with her hand on a man she had known for six years, and the checking was procedure and the result was the result and neither of them was any use to him.

She took her hand back and wrote the pulse down.

“Am I going to the hospital.”

“I’m calling them now.”

“There’s no point.”

“That’s the part I can’t use.” She had the phone out of her jacket already. “A refusal is worth something because the person making it wants an outcome. You can’t produce one. I have been writing down what you can’t produce since I came through that door and it is the whole of what I have. So that isn’t a refusal. It’s a reading, and I am not allowed to take a reading as an instruction.”

He looked at that as he had looked at everything.

“That’s fair,” he said.

She called it in from the chair with her fingers still on his wrist, and gave them the address and the code for the entry panel and a pulse of forty-four in a man of thirty-nine with no history, no injury and no complaint. The woman on the line took her through the questions in the order they come. Was he conscious. Was he breathing. Was he in pain. Had he taken anything. Iris answered all of them accurately and not one of the answers moved the call up a category, because every one of them was a no, and a conscious man breathing comfortably in a chair is not what the system is built to hurry for.

Twenty-five minutes. The yards were the far end of everything and the fog was in and there was one crew on that side of the river.

“Twenty-five minutes,” she said.

“That’s about right for out here.”

She put the phone face up on the arm of the chair where she would see it light, and did not look at it again.

Nothing in the room changed.

“You’ve seen this before,” Caleb said. He was not arguing and he had not been arguing. He was asking her for the operational answer, because she was the one in the room with the information.

“Twice.”

“And.”

“Once at this magnitude. He died in a chair about six hours after, and no one was called, and I do not know whether that is why.”

“All right,” Caleb said.

He took that as he had taken everything since January, without friction, and she watched him take it and could not find the place where the taking should have cost him something.

“Do you want me to call anyone,” she said.

“Who is there.”

“Your sister.”

“She’s in Halifax,” he said. “She’d be eleven hours.”

That was the arithmetic and it was correct and it was not an answer to the question, and Iris let it stand rather than asking again, because asking again would have produced a second correct arithmetic.

***

She stayed.

She had a coat and a chair and a man in front of her and forty-one minutes on the clock in the corner of the stove, and she used the time as she used all her time, which was to keep asking, because the asking was the last piece of work in the room and she was the only one left to do it.

He answered everything.

His pulse came down through the forties and into the thirties over about twenty minutes and he reported each stage of it accurately when she asked, and once without being asked, which she recorded because it was the only unprompted statement he made after the first two minutes.

The light went off the window at some point in the middle of it. There was a stretch where neither of them said anything for four minutes by the stove clock, and he sat in it without any sign of finding it long, and she sat in it counting, and the building made the sounds a building makes at four in the afternoon.

At thirty-eight she asked him to hold his hand out and he held it out and it was steady. At thirty-four she asked him the date and he gave it, and the day, and the time to within two minutes without looking at the clock.

His speech did not slur. His sentences did not shorten. At thirty-one beats a minute he told her, correctly, that the freight change on the northern run was going to cost the yard a day a week until someone rewrote the schedule, and that the person who should rewrite it was a man called Denny, and that Denny would not.

Somewhere after that she stopped asking about the freight.

She kept asking about him.

“Is there anything happening now.”

“No.”

“Are you frightened.”

“No.”

“Were you.”

“No.”

“Is there anything you would like me to do.”

He looked at her. His eyes were still finding her when she moved, and there was no lag in it, and he answered the question she had asked and not the question she had wanted to ask.

“You’re doing it,” he said.

Iris did not write that down.

She sat for a while with the light going flat orange through the window and the fog coming up the road from the water, and at some point she noticed that she had not asked him anything for several minutes, and she did not start again.

Then she did, once more, because it was the only one left that was not on any list.

“Do you want anything,” she said.

“No.”
