Chapter 6 — Too Much

The coordinates came in at ten past one with no message attached, because Kade never attached one, and Iris was in the van inside four minutes.

She had started the first caddy at seven that morning. It was running now on the bench with the enclosure sealed and the log writing to the array, and it would still be running when she got back, because the whole point of nine days of building was that the rig did not need her standing over it. Nothing off it would be readable for a week. She had told Kade that twice.

The address was on the north side, past the yards, in the blocks that went up when the port was expanding and had been waiting ever since for the second half of the plan. Four stories, brick, external stairs at both ends, a strip of grass with a bin store on it. She knew the type. She had been inside eleven of them for work.

There was an ambulance in the bay at the near end with its back doors shut and no urgency anywhere around it.

That told her most of what she needed before she had the handbrake on. A crew that is working moves in a particular manner. A crew that has finished stands differently, and this crew was standing.

***

The paramedic at the back of the ambulance was writing on a tablet with her sleeves pushed up, and she gave Iris the flat once-over that an unmarked van and a case in the hand earn.

“You can’t be up there.”

“I know. I’m not asking to go up.” Iris stopped at a distance that made it easy to end the conversation. “Was he found sitting down.”

The paramedic’s pen stopped.

“Who are you with?”

“Nobody. I’m not press and I’m not family and I’m not going to write your name anywhere.” She let that stand for a second. “I have been following this since the winter. Nobody in this city is counting them and I am. If you tell me to go I’ll go.”

It was a bad answer by every professional standard and it was the true one. She had learned in the winter that the true one worked about a third of the time on people who had been on shift too long.

The paramedic looked at her for a while.

“He was found sitting down,” she said.

“Was this the first call or the second.”

“How do you know there was a second?”

“Because you’re still here and you’ve got the doors shut,” Iris said. “What time was the first.”

***

The first call had come in at twenty to seven that morning.

The crew arrived at ten past seven and ran everything.

“He was oriented,” the paramedic said. “Name, date, where he was, why we were there. He got all of it. Blood pressure was fine. Sats were fine. Blood sugar was fine, and I did it twice, because that is usually the answer with an older man sitting in the dark.”

“Was he slow.”

“No. That’s the thing I keep going back to. He wasn’t slow and he wasn’t confused. He answered like a man answering.” She turned the tablet face down on the step. “I asked him how he’d been feeling and he said fine. I asked him whether anything had changed and he thought about it properly. He took his time over it. Then he said no.”

“And you offered him transport.”

“Twice. He declined it twice.” Something moved across her face and was put away. “He had capacity. He knew where he was, he knew what I was offering, he knew what declining meant. I can’t take a man who says no and answers every question I’ve got. There isn’t a form for that.”

“No,” Iris said. “There isn’t.”

“He apologized to us on the way out.” The paramedic said it evenly. “He said he was sorry we’d been called out for nothing.”

Iris held that.

It was ordinary and it was correct and it was the most accurate sentence anyone had said about the whole of it, because from where he was sitting it had been nothing. There had been no pain and no fear and no loss registering anywhere in him. He had been asked whether anything had changed. He had gone and looked, honestly, with whatever he had left to look with, and had found nothing to report.

“The second call,” she said.

“Eleven thirty. Same neighbor. Same chair.” The paramedic picked the tablet back up. “Same position. He hadn’t moved and he hadn’t got up and there was nothing in the room to say anything had happened. He just wasn’t answering by then.”

“Was there an event.”

“There was no event.” She said it like a woman who had already been asked and expected to be asked again by people with more authority than this. “No arrest. No fall. Nothing to work on. He was sitting in the same chair four hours later and he had stopped.”

“What will go on the certificate.”

The paramedic looked at her properly for the first time since she had got out of the van.

“That’s not mine,” she said. “He’ll go for a post-mortem, because there’s no cause and he wasn’t under a doctor for anything. And they will find one. He was sixty-eight. There is always something in a man of sixty-eight if you go looking, and whatever they write down will be true. It will be the reason his heart stopped. It will not be the reason he was sitting there.”

“Yes,” Iris said.

“You already knew that.”

“I wanted to hear how you would say it.”

***

The neighbor was standing at the bottom of the stairs at the far end with a cigarette he was not smoking, and he started talking before Iris had finished asking.

He had gone up because the door was standing open, and it never was. He wanted that understood as the reason. It was not curiosity and it was not a habit of checking on people, because the man had never wanted checking on and would have said so. The door was open at twenty past five in the morning and it was open at six thirty and that was the whole of it.

“He was sitting in the chair,” he said. “Lights off. I said, are you all right, and he said he was fine.”

“Did he look at you when he said it.”

“He did.” The neighbor turned the cigarette over. “That’s what I keep, that is. He looked right at me and he said it properly. If he’d been slurring, or if he’d been on the floor, I’d have had my phone out in the hall. He answered me like anyone answers anyone. So I went back down.”

“And then you called.”

“An hour after. Because it was still bothering me and I couldn’t have told you what about.” He looked at the bay. “I sat in my own kitchen for an hour arguing with myself about being a nuisance.”

“When did you last see him before this morning.”

“Tuesday. On the stairs, coming up with his shopping.” He said it quickly, with the readiness of a man who had already been asked twice and would be asked again. “We had a conversation about the bin store. Four or five minutes. He was completely fine. He was the same as he has been for years.”

“You’re certain about Tuesday.”

“I’m certain about Tuesday.”

Iris wrote it down. Tuesday to Thursday. Thirty-six hours at the outside and possibly a great deal less, and inside it a man had gone from a conversation about a bin store to a chair.

“Should I have gone back up?” the neighbor said.

She considered the question as asked, because it had been asked as a real one.

“No,” she said. “He answered you correctly and he answered the crew correctly, and they had more to work with than you did and they got the same result. There was nothing available to you at six thirty that would have changed the afternoon.”

He nodded and did not look any better, and she registered that the answer had not helped him, and that she had given it because it was true rather than because it would help. There had been a time when she would have known the difference before she opened her mouth.

***

The officer came down at two thirty and pulled the door to behind him, and it did not catch.

Iris went up at twenty to three.

She did not touch anything. She stood inside the door and worked the room in the order she worked any room, left wall to right, near to far, and wrote it as a list without conclusions attached, because a conclusion written at a scene is a conclusion you have to argue yourself out of later.

A chair by the window, angled toward it rather than toward the television. A side table with a cup on it, and the cup dry, and a ring under it that was older than the cup. The kitchen doorway beyond, and the counter visible through it, clear and wiped down. A calendar on the wall by the light switch with nothing written on it for this month or the last one, still turned to the right page.

Along the wall inside the door, four bags for a charity shop, filled and tied at the neck, standing upright in a line. The floor around them held a dust edge that had been there long enough to have a shape to it.

Under the window, a box with the flaps folded open and photographs in it, and two piles on the floor beside the box. One pile was large. The other was four or five deep, squared up, with a smooth stone set on top of it to stop it moving.

Nothing in the room had been knocked over. Nothing had been left mid-task except the box, and the box had been left mid-task a long time before this morning. She did not turn the piles over and she did not look at what was on top of the small one, because it was a scene and she was not going to be the person who moved something in it.

Then she ran her own check, standing with her attention loose at the front of her skull, and got nothing at all.

She wrote that down too. It was worth exactly as much as any other null. She had no measurement of how long a reading lasted after the fact, so a clean room at eight hours meant that nothing had been there in eight hours, or it meant that eight hours was long enough. She could not tell which and did not pretend to.

She pulled the door to behind her the way the officer had left it.

***

She sat in the van in the bay with the engine off and the window down and did the comparison, because she had the comparison and no one else in the world did.

Joel, in February, case ref K-01. Something specific removed and the rest of him running. He had gone to work. He had answered his wife in the correct order and at the correct length, and Leah had spent four months trying to name what had left and had never once been able to.

Lila Mercer. The same operation, further along and slower, and eight months of her sister driving over on Sundays to sit with a woman who was perfectly pleasant to her.

Caleb. Three weeks after, settled at the level he had been left at, giving her the freight times twice and asking how she was doing with the shape of the question and none of the reason for it.

Herself. Sitting inside her own case and taking the reading was the only advantage the year had given her, and what it returned was a specific removal and a large amount of remainder. Function intact. Judgment intact, mostly, on a schedule that was slipping. Thirty-five days of wall.

Every one of them was partial. Every one of them was survivable, and every one of them was slow, and the slowness was the reason there was anything left of any of them to examine.

This man had been fine on Tuesday. He had been fine on Tuesday and by twenty past five on Thursday morning he was sitting in a chair with everything gone, and the body had carried on for six hours after that without anything in it to steer.

The same operation. The same absence of injury, the same absence of an event, the same politeness. What had changed was quantity.

She had written *the difference is not direction* in a case note in the winter, about Caleb, and had meant a longer or a shorter distance along one line. This was the same line. The step was not the same size.

The effect had a magnitude. She had never had cause to think of it as having one, because until this morning every case she held had come in at the same size, and she had taken that for the nature of the thing rather than for a setting on it.

The magnitude had changed. That was a fact about the thing and not about the man, and it meant the four cases behind her were not a series of one event. They were the low end of a range.

***

There was a second thing and it took her another minute to reach, and when she reached it she sat with her hands on the wheel.

He was not on Rourke’s list.

He had never presented. He had never been flagged, never been referred, never carried a marker for behavioral flattening, and had never given a clinician anywhere a reason to write his name on anything. Rourke’s four hundred and sixteen were people who had survived a partial taking long enough to be noticed being strange. That was the entry criterion. That was the only entry criterion there had ever been.

So the curve on Rourke’s middle screen counted deaths among the survivors of the small dose, and it could not see this at all. It had never been able to. A man taken to completion in one pass does not enter the cohort, because he is never a patient, because there is no interval in which he can be observed being different.

And there was nothing behind him. No referral, no episode, no stretch of months in which he had been slightly less than he was. The neighbor had four or five minutes on a staircase on Tuesday and years of the same man before that, and the crew had a full set of correct answers, and none of it described a person who had been touched before.

Whatever had come into that flat had come into it once.

Iris held the whole of it and could find no way to put a number on it. There was no denominator. She had eleven deaths in a cohort of four hundred and sixteen and one death in a population of a city, and the second of those was not a rate.

***

The bay was empty by three, and the rain still had not arrived.

She stayed there a while longer and did the part she had driven out there for and had not done yet: work out what the last six hours had actually been like.

She was the only person alive who could.

He had been awake for all of it. He had been oriented, correct, capable of answering, in possession of every fact about himself except the ones that would have made any of them matter. He had been asked whether anything had changed, and he had gone and looked and found nothing, because the part of him that would have recognized a change as a loss was the part that was no longer there to do the recognizing. He had not been frightened. He had not been in pain. He had not been sad about the four bags standing by his door, or about whoever had filled them, or about the photographs he had stopped sorting and put a stone on.

He had sat in his chair and he had been fine, and he had gone on being fine until the fine ran out.

There was no one to say that to.

Rourke would take it as a description with a direction on it and would be right to, and would put it in a drawer with the eleven she could defend. Kade would take it and carry it around and it would cost him something and it would not help either of them. There had been one person in Lowport who would have received it as an account rather than as a symptom, and three weeks ago she had sat in his clean apartment and confirmed that whatever would have done the receiving had been lifted out of him.

She got the card out and wrote it standing against the side of the van. The doorframe held measurements and the light switch held instructions, and this was neither of those, and she wrote it anyway and put it in her pocket rather than in the case.

*The part of me that would mind is the part that was taken. Something in me still refuses. I cannot identify what is doing it.*

Then she sent Kade two words, because he had sent her the address and would be waiting, and because none of the rest of it belonged in a message.

*It’s worse.*

She drove back toward the harbor inside the speed limit and got in at twenty to four, and the rig had run clean all day and had eleven hours left on the first drive.
