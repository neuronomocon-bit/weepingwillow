Chapter 4 — Intake

The fog had not lifted by ten, and the coast road ran close enough to the water that Marin drove the last mile with the wipers on intermittent, clearing salt rather than rain. Willow had her feet up on the dash, which she had known Marin hated for about twenty-five years.

“You could put those down.”

“I could.” She did not.

North of the container terminal the road gave up on the town entirely. There were nine miles of it out to the site, past the tank farm and the two dead marinas and a stretch where the guardrail had been replaced in sections by three different contractors, in three different colors of galvanizing. After that there was nothing but scrub brush and the drop to the water on the right. Gulls stood along the rail in the fog with their backs to the traffic. Marin had driven it four hundred times and still could not have said where the town officially stopped.

The site came up on the left without announcing itself: a low concrete building set back from the road behind a chain fence, three stories at its tallest point and mostly one, laid out along the bluff so that the long face of it took the weather off the water. Meridian had bought it as a shell. Someone had made cable there once, or fittings. Marin had never gotten a straight answer. Whatever it had been, it had left behind a floor plan that resisted every attempt to make it feel like a clinic. Salt had already gotten into the newer window seams on the seaward side. The sign over the main entrance was recent enough to still be legible. Everything under it had been patched.

Willow looked at it for a while as they came up the access road.

“It’s very ugly,” she said.

“It’s a converted plant.”

“I’m not criticizing. I’m just saying, for the amount of money, you’d think.” She took her feet down at last, pulled the visor mirror open, and shut it again without looking at herself. “It looks like somewhere you’d go to identify a body.”

“That’s the harbormaster’s office. Other end of town.”

“Oh good.”

The lot had been laid out for a workforce four times the size of the one that used it. Marin took her usual space in the second row, turned the engine off, and sat for a moment with her hands still on the wheel. The fog was thick enough that the far end of the building was barely visible. Beside her, Willow had gone quiet, and Marin had learned some years ago not to question it.

“You can say if you want to go home,” Marin said.

“I don’t want to go home.”

“You can say it anyway.”

“I don’t want to go home.” Willow got her bag off the floor between her feet and fought the strap over her shoulder without standing up first. “I want to complain the entire time and then do it. That’s the arrangement. You’ve met me.”

***

Inside was an improvement on the outside. The entrance corridor had been finished properly, wide and evenly lit, with a floor that took a shine and a row of frosted panels along one side that let a gray suggestion of the weather in and kept the parking lot out of it. It smelled of nothing much, which had been expensive. Marin had sat in three separate meetings about the smell.

Terri Roth was on the desk, as she was most mornings, with her reading glasses up in her hair and two screens angled where no one standing at the counter could read them.

“Morning, Dr. Halloway.”

“Morning. This is Willow.”

“Willow Halloway.” Terri found it on the screen without looking down for long. “Ten o’clock, baseline and consent. You’re going to be with us most of the day, I’m afraid.”

“Everyone keeps saying that with a little apology on the end of it,” Willow said. “How bad is it, really?”

“It’s mostly sitting.”

“Sitting I can do. Sitting is arguably my main thing.”

Terri smiled at that and turned a tablet around on the counter. Marin watched her sister take it and hold it slightly too far away, the way she did with anything official.

“Do people ever come out of here funnier?” Willow said.

“I haven’t had a report.”

“Because that would be worth knowing going in.”

“I’ll flag it,” Terri said, “as an outcome measure.”

Willow laughed, properly, the short surprised one. Marin felt the morning get about ten percent easier.

They went through to the intake suite, two doors down, built out of what the original plans still called a supervisor’s office. It had a table rather than a desk, four chairs, and a window that showed the water when the fog allowed. The room had been designed by people who thought carefully about how to keep a room from feeling like an interview. It worked on almost everyone. It had never once worked on Willow, who sat down in it, looked around, and said, “This is a room for bad news.”

“It’s a room for paperwork.”

“That’s what I said.”

Marin took the chair across from her instead of the one alongside, because sitting beside a patient was a clinical position and she was not there as a clinician. She had thought about this for longer than she was going to admit to anyone.

***

The consent package ran to eleven pages. Terri had left it on the table in hard copy as well as on the tablet, because a proportion of people signed better on paper. Willow went straight for the paper.

“Do I have to read all of it?”

“You have to be given all of it. What you do after that is between you and the law.”

“That’s a very lawyerly answer for someone who’s supposed to be my sister today.”

“I’m being both. It’s not going well.”

Willow turned to the second page and started reading aloud in a voice, a deep flat institutional drone she had been doing since she was fourteen, when it had been used on school letters. “*The procedure is intended to produce attenuation of the affective valence associated with target memory structures, without alteration of the declarative content of those structures.*” She stopped. “Who writes this.”

“A committee.”

“*Affective valence.*”

“It means the feeling attached to it.”

“I know what it means, Marin, I’m asking who let them say it like that.” She went on down the page, still in it, then dropped out halfway through a sentence about session frequency and read the rest silently.

Page three was the schedule. Twelve sessions, weekly, with a review at six. Willow read it twice and asked what happened at the review. Marin told her: the treating team looked at the outcome measures and decided with her whether to continue. Willow said that “with her” was doing a lot of work in that sentence. It was, Marin said. She offered to get it in writing. Willow said no. She’d take it.

Page four was the medication history. It ran to two columns and asked for dates.

“I’m not reading this one out,” Willow said.

“You don’t have to. Terri already has most of it from the referral.”

“Then why is it here?”

“Because you have to confirm it.”

Willow looked at the page for a moment longer. Then she took the pen off the table and started filling in the gaps. She did not talk while she did it. It took a little under four minutes. Marin looked out at the fog for as much of that as she could manage.

There were things she knew about her sister that were not on that page, and things on that page she had not known, and she had decided in advance to notice neither.

“There,” Willow said, and turned it over.

***

The rest went quickly, because most of it was procedural: identity confirmation, data handling, the withdrawal clause, contact preferences, next of kin. Willow put Marin down for next of kin, then held the pen over the box a second longer.

The program had started as a veterans’ cohort. It had opened up two years earlier, when the results held and the second-year data survived review. Willow had come in through the expansion, on a referral from a practice in town. There was a line about that on the form as well. She read it and did not comment.

Then she got to the page about where the data went, and she put the pen down.

“Can you see it?”

Marin had expected the question at some point in the day. She had not expected it here, and had to reorganize slightly.

“See what?”

“Any of it. The mapping. The sessions. Whatever comes off me today.” Willow was not doing a voice now. “You work here. Can you look me up?”

“Not your clinical file. That sits with your treating team and I’m not on it. I asked to be kept off it and they agreed before I finished asking.”

“That’s not quite the same answer as no.”

It was not, and Marin was not going to pretend otherwise, because they had spent thirty years catching each other doing exactly that.

“The baseline map goes into the research archive,” she said. “Under a research identifier, not your name. That side is mine, or it’s mine and about nine other people’s.”

“A research identifier.”

“A number. It’s on the second page of your pack, in the box under the barcode. It goes on everything that is not your clinical file.”

Willow turned back two pages and found the box. She read it out in the institutional voice. “MN-R-0338.”

“That’s you, on that side of the building.”

“That’s a parking space.”

“It’s how the work gets done without your name attached to it.”

“I know what it’s for. I’m telling you what it sounds like.” She put her thumb over the box and took it off again. “Fine.”

“So the honest answer,” Marin said, “is that a version of your data will exist in a system I have access to, and I will not be opening it, and there’s a log that would show it if I did.”

“Would anyone read the log?”

“Probably not.”

Willow considered that. “Okay,” she said, and picked the pen back up. “I mostly wanted to know if you’d tell me the truth about it.”

“I’d rather you asked.”

“I did ask.”

“I’d rather you keep asking,” Marin said.

Willow made a small dismissive noise and signed the page. That was that.

***

The window had gone completely white. Somewhere below the bluff the swell was working at the rocks, and the sound came up through the floor more than through the air, a low irregular knock that everyone who worked there stopped hearing inside a week.

Willow got to the last page and did not sign it. She squared it up with the edge of the table instead, then squared the whole stack. Then she said, without looking up:

“Is it going to change me?”

Marin had an answer for this. She had given it perhaps two hundred times, to patients, to funders, to a hostile ethics panel in the second year, and to her own reflection in a train window once when the panel had gone badly. She had never had to say it to someone whose first word had been her name.

“It removes the charge,” she said. “It doesn’t touch the memory. You’ll have everything you have now. You’ll know what happened, you’ll know when, you’ll know it was bad. What comes off is the load that arrives with it.”

“That sounds like the same thing.”

“I know it does. It isn’t.”

“Explain why not, then, and don’t do the leaflet.”

“I wasn’t going to do the leaflet.” Marin put her hands flat on the table. “A memory is information. What you’ve got attached to yours is an alarm that never got told the emergency ended. Every time the information comes up, the alarm comes up with it, at full volume, on the assumption that the thing is happening now and you have to survive it now. It’s been running like that for nine years and it is going to keep running, because nothing in the system has any way of finding out that it’s over.”

Willow was listening. She had gone still, and she stayed still, and she did not reach for the pen.

“What we take out is the firing,” Marin said. “Not the file. Afterward you’ll be able to think about it. You’ll be able to bring it up on purpose, look at it, decide what you want to do about it, and put it down. You’ll be the one deciding. At the moment it decides, and you find out afterward.”

“And I’ll still know it was bad.”

“You’ll know exactly how bad. You’ll be able to say so out loud, at a normal volume, in a room full of people. You just won’t be back inside it.”

Willow turned the pen over once and set it down parallel to the edge of the page.

“Here’s my thing,” she said. “It hurting is how I know it counted.”

“I understand that.”

“Do you? Because that’s the actual thing. If I can talk about it like it’s the weather then it wasn’t anything, and if it wasn’t anything then the last nine years were me being dramatic, and I would rather have it hurt than have it turn out I did all of that for no reason.”

Not answering straight away was the correct thing to do and also all Marin was capable of.

“It counted,” she said. “And it counts whether or not it’s still costing you. Those are two separate facts and you’ve had them welded together so long you think they’re one. The record of what happened to you doesn’t need you to keep paying for it in order to stay true.”

Willow looked at her.

“That’s quite good,” she said.

“I’ve had practice.”

“No, I mean it, that’s a good line. Did you write that or did the committee?”

“I wrote it. The committee took it out.”

“Of course they did.” Willow took the pen up again and turned to the last page. Then she stopped again, with the pen an inch off the paper. When she spoke it was in a different register, quick and light, so that it could be taken as a joke if she needed it to be.

“What if I don’t like who’s left.”

“Then we’ve got twelve sessions to work with and a review at six. You can stop at any of them. It’s on page nine.”

“That’s not an answer either.”

“It’s the only one that’s true, and you’d have known if I’d made a better one up.”

Willow signed her name. She had a terrible signature, a long collapsing scrawl with the Ls going up like a fever chart, and she had never fixed it because she thought fixing it was for people who cared about being taken seriously.

She put the pen down and pushed the stack across the table.

“Okay,” she said. “Where do you want me.”

***

The mapping suite was at the end of the corridor that ran the length of the clinical wing. Marin walked her down. The corridor was long and even, with doors at regular intervals, each one with a frosted panel beside it and a printed identifier at chest height. The overhead light held steady from one end to the other, so that no stretch of it fell darker than the rest. That had been specified on purpose. People walking somewhere frightening should not have to walk through a shadow to get there.

They passed the nurses’ station, where a woman Marin knew by sight was restocking a drawer. They passed the freight elevator, and then the stairwell. The stairwell went down as well as up. Marin did not think about the down until they were past it, and then only in a filing sense. The substrate work was on that level. Virek would be down there now, doing something meticulous and unhurried with a decay curve. There was no reason to take her sister that way.

“You’ve gone quiet,” Willow said.

“I’m thinking about the afternoon.”

“You’re thinking about the afternoon.”

“I have a two o’clock.”

“You’re a bad liar and you’ve always been a bad liar, and the tell is that you get more specific.” Willow bumped her shoulder once into Marin’s arm as they walked, the same as she had done in school corridors. “Two o’clock. Very good. What’s it about?”

“Procurement.”

“See, now I believe you, because nobody would make that up.”

The mapping suite had a technician waiting, a chair that reclined a little too far back, and an array on a jointed arm that looked worse than it was. Willow stopped in the doorway and had a good long look at it.

“Okay, that’s a machine.”

“It’s a scanner. It doesn’t touch you.”

“It’s on an arm, Marin.”

“So is a dentist’s light.”

“That is not the reassurance you think it is.” But she went in. She put her bag on the floor by the chair and shook the technician’s hand, and then asked him whether he had ever dropped the arm on anyone. He said not yet. She said that was the correct answer.

Marin stood in the doorway. That was as far as she was going.

“It’s about four hours,” she said. “There are breaks. If you want me to come back at any point you tell them and they’ll get me.”

“I’m not going to want you to come back.”

“I know.”

“I might want you to come back.”

“Then tell them.”

Willow sat down in the chair and immediately made herself look ridiculous in it, sliding down until her head was below the rest of her and her knees were up. The last thing Marin saw before the door closed was her sister pulling a face at her.

It worked, more or less.

***

Marin went back up the corridor.

She got as far as the stairwell and stopped with one hand on the rail. A porter came past with a cart. She stood a while longer after he had gone.

What she felt, and she identified it accurately because identifying things accurately was the whole of her training and most of her personality, was relief. It sat in her chest like something set down, clean and specific and enormous. She had driven up the coast road that morning carrying dread, and had assumed that the best available outcome for the day was hope. Relief had not been on the list.

She had been carrying her sister for nine years. Not metaphorically: the phone at two in the morning, the drives, the third job in two years and then the fourth, the apartment above the laundromat that Willow had never left because leaving would have required one clear month in which nothing went wrong, the birthdays that had to be checked in advance for whether they were survivable. Marin had built a whole second life out of load-bearing. She had gotten so used to the weight that she could only feel it on the rare mornings when it lifted. It had not lifted in a long time. It had lifted now, because at the end of that corridor a technician with good hands was taking a baseline, and after that there would be twelve sessions and a review at six. For the first time since it started, someone else was going to hold it.

She stood with her hand on the rail and let that be true.

Then she looked at her watch. She had forty minutes before the procurement meeting, and if she went now she could get through the consumables list and be ready for it. She took her hand off the rail and went up.
