Chapter 10 — Calm

Marin drove inland on the Saturday with a box on the passenger seat and had to check the address twice, because she had only been to the new place once and the turning came earlier off the county road than she expected.

It was a low brick block set back behind a strip of grass, six units, built in the seventies for people who worked at the mill and kept afterward for people who worked wherever there was work. Second floor, on the end. A parking space came with it and Willow’s car was in it, so she was up, and at eleven on a Saturday that would not have been true in March.

She had lived above the laundromat for six years. Marin had gone over the reasons with her perhaps thirty times across those years, and every one of them had been correct, and none of them had ever been the reason. Leaving would have taken one clear month in which nothing went wrong, and Willow had not had one clear month since she was twenty-three. Then she had three in a row and she moved in April.

Marin carried the box up.

“It’s open,” Willow said through the door.

Inside, the apartment was clean.

That was not a small thing and Marin let herself have it. A table with two chairs at it, a couch, a lamp, and a rug from their mother’s that looked better here than it had ever looked there. The counter was clear. The mail sat in a stack on the end of it with the envelopes slit and the contents put back, sorted into a system, and the system had been running long enough to have produced a stack.

On the fridge there was a calendar with her shifts written on it in the small square handwriting she had used since school.

Six weeks in and there was nothing on the walls yet, and no boxes either. Marin looked for the boxes out of habit, because there had always been boxes, and then registered that there were none because everything had been unpacked.

“You’ve done all of it,” she said.

“There wasn’t much.”

“There was a lot. I carried it.”

“You carried the heavy things,” Willow said. “The rest was mine to do and I did it.”

She said it agreeably and it was accurate. Marin set the box on the table and started taking things out of it: a colander, two mugs, a lamp bulb of the type the bedroom fixture needed, a bag of the coffee.

“I brought you an actual colander. You’ve been draining pasta with a plate.”

“Thank you.” Willow took it and looked at it and then put it in the cupboard under the sink, on the left, where a colander goes.

Marin waited a second, and then went on unpacking the box.

***

They had the coffee at the table.

Willow talked about the store. She was on four to close five days and had been moved off the register onto ordering, for better money and worse hours. The man who did the ordering before her had left a spreadsheet that referred to items by codes that existed nowhere else in the building. She explained the spreadsheet clearly and she had solved most of it. When Marin asked whether the man had been an idiot, Willow said he had probably been doing his best with what he had.

A paperback lay face-down on the arm of the couch, about a third of the way in.

“Any good?”

“It’s about a family who buy a hotel.” Willow considered it. “The middle section is stronger than the beginning. It’s set in the fifties and the research is very thorough.”

She did not say whether she liked it. Marin waited to see whether she would, and then asked something else.

Their mother had not come to see the apartment. She had said she would come in June, when it was warmer, and the apartment was twenty minutes from her and the heating worked and it had been warm since the second week of May.

“Has she said anything else about June?”

“She said the roads.”

“The roads.”

“The roads are bad in spring.” Willow turned her cup a quarter turn on the table. “It’s fine. She’ll come or she won’t.”

Marin looked at her.

There was a version of that sentence with an edge on it and it would have used the same nine words. She played the recording back in her head and could not find an edge that fit. Willow had said it as a person says a thing they have finished thinking about.

Six months ago this would have taken a week. Marin knew what the week looked like, having lived through eleven or twelve of them. The call the first night. The second call the next night, with the better version of the argument that had occurred to her in the meantime. The third night, when it had become about something from 2019. Then the Sunday when it stopped mattering and neither of them mentioned it again.

Marin had spent a great deal of that decade wishing her sister could put something down.

“That’s good,” she said.

“It’s not really good or bad. It’s just the roads.”

Marin heard herself ask how she had been sleeping.

“Fine. Seven, seven and a half.”

“Appetite?”

“Fine.”

“Do you think about it?”

“No,” Willow said. “Not really. Not unless someone brings it up.”

She was not being brave about it. She said it as she had said the thing about the roads, and she picked up her cup, and she drank some of the coffee, and she waited to see whether Marin had another question.

Marin did not have another question. What Marin had was the fourth item on a review schedule, delivered in her sister’s kitchen on a Saturday, and she put it down and did not pick it up again.

At intake Willow had asked what would happen if she did not like who was left. She had said it lightly, at the end, the way she said the things she meant. Marin had an answer for it now and there was no version of the room in which she could ask the question in order to give it.

The date was in three weeks.

Marin had known that since before she got in the car, and had cleared the day around it, and had worked out on the drive what she was going to suggest and how to suggest it so that it did not sound suggested. For nine years the run-up had been its own event. Willow checked dates in advance the way other people check the weather, to find out what was survivable, and the two weeks before were worse than the day, and the day was bad.

“It’s the twenty-first in three weeks,” Marin said.

“It is.”

“I’ve got that Saturday. I thought we could go up to the lake, or not go anywhere. We could decide on the day.”

Willow thought about it, and it took her no time at all.

“Sure,” she said. “Either’s fine. I’m off Saturdays now.”

That was all of it. She had not braced, and she had not gone quiet at the number.

Marin sat with a plan she had built over three days and rehearsed in a car, and there was nothing in the room for it to be for.

“Okay,” she said. “I’ll call you that week.”

“Okay.”

“Walk?” she said instead.

“Sure.”

***

The path north of the city ran along the top of the shore for about two miles before it turned inland at the culvert. They had walked it since Willow was small, and they walked it now in the flat bright light of the last Saturday in May with the tide most of the way out.

The water was doing the thing it did in that light, which was not much. Two men were down on the flats with buckets. Someone had put a bench in since the last time, with a plaque on it, and the plaque carried a name Marin did not recognize.

Willow was good company. That was the part Marin kept coming back to afterward. She was easy to walk with and she was interested in things when they were pointed out to her. She noticed a hawk before Marin did and stopped so they could both watch it. She was not tired and she did not want to turn back early. None of the low static came off her that had come off her for nine years, and Marin had gotten so used to it that she had stopped being able to name it until it was not there.

Somewhere past the first headland Willow said the store was putting two people through the supervisor course in the fall, and that Neal had asked whether she wanted one of the places.

“That’s a real job,” Marin said. “That’s salary.”

“It is.”

“Are you going to take it?”

“I might. I said I’d think about it.” Willow stepped around a wet patch without breaking stride. “It’s more hours and the hours are worse, and the money’s better. It comes out about even, so it depends what I want.”

“And what do you want?”

“I don’t mind either way.”

She said it without apology and without appeal, as a person states the result of a calculation already run. Marin had spent nine years watching her sister be unable to choose anything, because every option had a trapdoor under it and the trapdoors were not always in the same place twice. The woman walking beside her now could take the job or leave it and would be fine.

A year ago the same question would have produced forty minutes on the phone at eleven at night and a conclusion neither of them could hold on to by morning.

“You should take it,” Marin said.

“Maybe.”

About a mile along, the town had put a sign up on a post at the top of the beach steps.

Marin stopped in front of it.

For six or seven years her phone had collected photographs of signs. Willow took them and sent them without comment, at all hours, from wherever she was: a laminated notice in a doctor’s waiting room, a hand-lettered thing outside a garden center, an entire page of terms and conditions from the back of a parking receipt. The joke was never explained and did not need to be. The last one had come in February.

She had the shape of this one before she had read it properly. She took a breath and did the voice.

“*This area is closed to the harvesting of all species of shellfish,*” she read, in a deep institutional drone, flat and slightly nasal, four inches below her own register, “*including but not limited to mussels, clams, quahogs and carnivorous snails, pursuant to a determination of the Department.*”

She held the last two words longer than the sentence needed.

She was not good at it. She had never been good at it, and that had always been half the point, because the voice was not hers. It had belonged to Willow since she was fourteen. It had been used on school letters, on the back of appliance warranties, and, memorably, on the entire order of service at their grandmother’s funeral in the car afterward. The arrangement had always been the same. Marin did it badly, Willow shoved her, and then Willow did it properly and made it worse.

She was still turned toward the sign and she was waiting for the shove.

“That’s funny,” Willow said.

She was smiling. The smile was on her face at the right moment and it was the correct size for the joke and it reached her eyes, and it stayed for about as long as a smile stays.

“Carnivorous snails,” Marin said.

“I know. It’s a real thing they had to write down.”

They walked on.

Marin got about forty yards up the path before she noticed she was composing the next one. A second sign stood at the culvert, about dogs, with at least three clauses in it, and she had already started arranging which of them she would lean on.

She stopped doing that.

Setting up a second one would have made it a test, and a test would have required a result, and there was no version of the afternoon in which she wrote a result down about her sister on a coastal path in May. She put her hands in her pockets and looked at the water and let the sign at the culvert go by without reading it.

She walked the rest of the way to the turn deciding whether she had imagined it.

The evidence was not good. Willow had answered correctly and promptly, she had known what the joke was for, and she had said the accurate thing about it, and none of that was the behavior of a person who had missed anything. Marin had been braced for the shove because she had been braced for the shove for twenty years. A person does not shove you every time. A person is sometimes just walking.

There was also this. Her sister was three months out from a course of treatment that had done precisely what it was built to do, on schedule, with the outcome measures to show for it. Nothing in the mechanism touched anything but the target structures, and Marin had read the mechanism more times than anyone alive.

If it was not nothing, then it was that.

She looked at that for a moment, from a slight distance, as she would have looked at a result someone brought her that did not fit. Then she put it where she had put the review questions in the kitchen, and they turned inland at the culvert, and Willow said something about the man with the spreadsheet, and Marin laughed.

***

They came back along the road because the tide had turned.

Somewhere on the last half mile Marin caught herself waiting. She had been doing it since about the eighth week. Not for anything she could have named on a form. She had wanted a sister who was not in pain and she had one, walking beside her in good light, talking about a spreadsheet, and Marin kept holding a space open next to all of it for the rest of her to come back into.

Three months was not long. People took a year to come out from under nine.

At the car Willow said thank you for the colander, and for the lamp bulb she had needed for six weeks and had not gotten around to, and for coming out on a Saturday. She said all of it warmly. She stood with one hand on the roof of the car while she said it, and she meant it, and Marin could not have said which part of it she would have changed.

“I’ll come out again in a couple of weeks,” Marin said. “I’ll bring the other lamp.”

“You don’t have to.”

“I know.”

“Okay.” Willow stepped back off the car. “Drive carefully. The roads are bad in spring.”

Marin laughed at that, properly, because it was funny.

She was still half laughing when she looked up, and Willow was walking toward the door with her keys already out, and she got them into the lock on the first attempt.

***

Marin drove back with the sun going down over the water on her right the whole way, which happened four or five times a year on that road and was the reason she took it.

She got in at seven. She ate standing up, read forty minutes of a paper on cohort attrition that had been in her bag since Thursday, and made the notes she had promised Raman for Monday. Then she sat for a while with the television on and the sound low.

For nine years the phone had gone at about eleven. Sometimes twice a week and sometimes four nights running, and it was never about anything: a thing on television, a man at the store, a question about whether their grandmother’s maiden name had two Ls, and once, memorably, forty minutes on whether a hot dog was a sandwich. Marin had complained about it to colleagues. She had said, more than once and meaning it, that she would give a year of her life for eight uninterrupted hours.

At eleven she rinsed the cup and turned the lights off in the front room and went to bed.

She slept through.
