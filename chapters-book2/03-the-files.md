Chapter 3 — The Files

The unit was in the third row of a storage yard off the industrial road, one of forty identical bays behind a gate that took a code and did not record it. Kade had it under his own name and had been paying for it monthly for three years. He told her that in the car park before he opened it, in the flat voice of a man getting a thing said early so it would not have to be said later.

The roller door went up to about chest height and stuck, and he ducked under it and hit the light.

Two hundred square feet, concrete floor, breeze-block walls painted once. Pallets down both sides to the height of her shoulder, shrink-wrapped and gone milky with age. A stack of flat crates against the back wall. In the near corner, where the roof sheet met the wall badly, there was a dark patch two feet across on the floor and a builder’s bucket sitting under it with an inch of water in the bottom.

“That’s been like that since the second year,” Kade said. “They know. It’s on the account.”

“Is anything under it.”

“No. I moved that row in the first month.” He said it without looking for credit. “There’s a light in the ceiling that works and one that doesn’t. Don’t stand on the pallets, they’re not banded, they’ll go.”

Iris put her bag down on the dry side and started at the door end.

***

The pallets were not sorted. They had been loaded in the order things had come off a truck, and the truck had been loaded in the order things had come out of a building, and the building had been emptied by people who were being made redundant on a schedule they had been given in writing. That produced a particular kind of stacking. Heavy at the bottom, correct. Fragile on top, correct. And then, at some point in every column, a run of four or five identical cases that had clearly gone on together because they had come off a shelf together, followed by something that had no business next to them.

She read the labels along the second row. Most of them were printed and had survived. A few were handwritten on tape, and the tape had lifted and taken the ink with it.

Every label was an asset code. None of them was a description.

“It’s all like that,” Kade said, from the door. “I’ve been through half of it.”

“MN-C-0412.”

“That’s a code for a thing that lived in a room. That’s all it ever was.” He came in and crouched at the end of the row. “The manifests are the same. We moved codes. The client generated a schedule, the schedule had codes on it and a destination against each one, and the crew matched the code on the crate to the code on the sheet and put it where the sheet said. Nobody wrote down what a thing was, because nobody in that chain needed to know and it would have taken an extra week.”

“Do you have the schedules?”

He had the schedules. He had them in a plastic wallet inside a box file, and they were paper, because even then a contractor’s copy was still paper.

Iris read the first sheet. It was three columns, and it was exactly what he had said it was: a code, a destination, and a date. Each line had been ticked by hand. At the bottom of the third page the same hand had written *38 pallets ex. clinical wing, ex. lower level as marked* and a set of initials.

“These are the siblings,” she said.

“To yours. Yes.” Kade did not soften it. “The job I sent you on was MN-44-7. Same client, same schedule series. You were retrieving out of one of the sites these things went to. I didn’t know that when I sent you and I knew it eleven days after.”

“And the codes still open.”

“My credentials still work.” He straightened up. “Three years. I’ve never used them for anything, and they’ve never been revoked, and I’ve stopped assuming that’s an oversight.”

He left it there and went back to what he had been doing, and Iris let him, because he had told her the useful part and everything after it was weather.

***

She spent the first hour on condition, because condition determined everything else and there was no point building a plan on top of a guess.

The good news was on the third and fourth pallets in. The research archive had left that building on archival cartridge, in rigid cases, in cardboard outers with the desiccant sachets still rattling in the bottom. That media had been specified deliberately and paid for out of a research grant, and the budget had held, because grants have line items that survive the people who wrote them. She opened four cases at random. The cartridges were clean, the shells were undistorted, and the write-protect tabs were where they had been left.

She set up on the flat crates and read one, because condition you can see is a guess and condition you have read is a fact.

It came off at full rate. No retries, no soft errors, a clean index at the head and a directory structure that had been thought about by someone who expected it to be read. She let it run four minutes and stopped it.

“This is fine,” she said. “This is better than fine. Whoever bought this bought it to last thirty years and it’s three years old.”

“Good.”

“It is not going to stay fine in here, though.” She put the back of her hand against the wall and held it there. “This bay has no climate control. It is whatever the weather is, plus what a metal roof does to it, and it has been that for three years. Archival media is rated to conditions. It is not rated to a shed.”

“I couldn’t put it anywhere with a paper trail.”

“I know. I’m not telling you that you were wrong, I’m telling you what it cost.”

“I want to see what came out of the mirror site.”

He knew which pallets and he did not want to walk her to them, and he walked her to them.

The second class of material was in the back stack, and it had been in water.

Not submerged. Standing damp, for a period that was never measured, in a transfer point on the eastern spur where a service duct backed up in the second winter after the shutdown. The cases had been dried and restacked, and whoever did it had done a decent job with what they had. The outers were gone. The drives were consumer-grade, spinning disks in plastic caddies, the sort you buy in a hurry when the thing you actually specified is on a six-week lead time.

She turned one over and read the corrosion on the connector pins with her light.

“This is the index,” she said.

“That’s what I thought.”

“No, I mean I can tell from the media.” She set it down and picked up another. “Nobody puts a clinical record set on this. This is what a department buys with what’s left in a budget in the last quarter. So somebody decided which of these two things was worth archival media and which one wasn’t, and they were not wrong at the time, because one of them had resale value and a citation trail and the other one was paperwork about people.”

Kade looked at the stack for a while.

“Say the part I’m not getting.”

***

“There are two sets in this room,” Iris said. “They do different jobs and neither one is any use without the other.”

She put her hand flat on the good pallet.

“That is the research archive. Session data, baseline maps, substrate logs, whatever the facilities side was reconciling. It is complete, or near enough, and I will have most of it off inside three weeks because the media wants to give it to me. And every single record in it is filed under a research identifier.”

“Meaning a number.”

“Meaning a number.” She moved to the back stack. “This is the clinical and administrative index. It is the set that maps a number to a person. Name, intake date, next of kin, referral. It is on the worst media in the building and it spent a winter in water, and I will get some of it, and I do not know how much, and I am not going to be able to tell you until I have imaged the first four platters and seen how the read errors distribute.”

“How long.”

“Weeks for a first answer. Months for all of it, if all of it is there.” She looked at him. “If the index comes off, everything in this room is about people. If it doesn’t, everything in this room is about numbers, and I will be able to tell you exactly what was done and to how many and in what order, and I will not be able to tell you to whom.”

Kade said nothing for long enough that she looked over. He had picked up one of the caddies and was holding it in both hands, carefully, having just been told what it was worth and being unable to see it.

“That’s the whole difference,” he said.

“Yes.”

“Between a thing that happened and a thing that happened to somebody.”

“Yes.”

***

He started talking about twenty minutes later, while she was building an inventory and he was breaking down the empty outers so they would stack.

“There was a row,” he said. “Second week of the decommissioning. Twelve crates, all one code series, and the schedule had them going to secondary, and the code on the crates was one character off a series that was going to the mirror site.”

Iris kept writing.

“So I asked. That’s the correct thing to do and I did it. I went to the coordinator on the floor and I said, these say secondary and they look like mirror, and she checked her sheet and said secondary. And I went back and I looked at it again, because it still didn’t sit right, and about an hour later I asked her a second time.”

“What did she say?”

“Secondary.” He folded a flap flat and creased it with his thumb. “Same answer, no hesitation, no irritation about being asked twice. She had a sheet and the sheet said secondary. So I wrote *queried twice, confirmed secondary* on my own copy, with the time, and I signed it, and we moved them to secondary.”

He put the flattened outer on the pile.

“I still have that copy. It’s in the box file. I’ve had three years to decide whether I did the right thing and I decided about four years ago that I did. I asked twice. Nobody asks three times. There isn’t a version of me on that floor in that week who asks three times.”

“No,” Iris said. “There isn’t.”

“What I did not do,” he said, “was open one.”

He said it as a finished thing, something he had done all his feeling about years ago, and that was the only reason it was bearable to be in the room for. He was not asking her to absolve him. He had not built up to it, and he went on breaking down cardboard while he said it.

Iris could see the whole structure of what he was doing. He had brought her here, and paid for this unit for three years, and kept the queried copy, and told her about the row before she found it herself, and every one of those was the same act performed four different ways.

She had nothing to put against it. The thing a person is supposed to have ready in that moment was not available to her, and she was not going to manufacture a version of it for him, because he would have known and it would have been worse.

“The crates you moved were manifest lines,” she said. “You matched a code to a sheet. That is the job. If you had opened one you would have been fired that afternoon and the twelve crates would have gone to secondary anyway, with somebody else’s initials on the line.”

Kade laughed, once, with no humor in it at all.

“That’s not comfort.”

“No.”

“It’s better than comfort,” he said. “Don’t get better at it.”

***

She worked until the light through the roller door had gone orange, and then she built the plan out loud so he could hear the parts that would cost money.

Image everything before reading anything. Nothing gets powered up in this unit, because this unit has a wet corner and no bench and one working light. The archival cartridges go out in four batches so that a fire in her apartment costs her a quarter of the material instead of all of it. The wet drives do not get powered up at all until she has built a rig for them, and she was going to build the rig properly, and that would take nine days she was not going to shorten.

“Nine days,” Kade said.

“You’ve had it three years.”

“Fair.” He put the caddy back on the stack, squarely, in the space it had come out of. “Say the rest of it. You’ve been building to something since the wall.”

She had been, and he had noticed, and she was going to have to revise upward what he caught.

“The cartridges are stable. Whatever they lose in a shed, they lose over decades, and I have decades.” She turned her light on the back stack and left it there. “That is not stable. Corrosion does not stop when a thing dries out. It slows down and it keeps going, and every winter in an unheated bay walks it a little further along, and the platters are where the damage lands last and worst. There is a version of that stack I can read today. There is a smaller version I can read next winter, and I cannot tell you how much smaller, and there is no point at which anybody rings a bell.”

“So it’s a race.”

“It’s an appointment I don’t know the date of.” She picked up her bag. “The archive will wait for me. The index is the only thing in this room that can put a name on anything, it is the only copy, and it is spending every day I do not get to it becoming a smaller answer.”

Kade looked at the stack for a while, and then he did the arithmetic she had done in the car park, out loud, because he was a logistics man and it was the only way he knew to hold something.

“Three years in here,” he said. “On my account. Because I didn’t want a paper trail.”

“Yes.”

“All right,” he said, and did not say any of the rest of it.

She stopped at the top of the third row on the way out and looked down it, because it was worth looking at once without a task attached. Thirty-eight pallets. A room a company had put its own contents into on a schedule, correctly, one code at a time, with a tick against every line.

She put her hand on the shrink-wrap. Under it the second case down had a desiccant sachet that had gone hard, and the label on the outer said MN-C-0447.

She wrote the code in her inventory. She ticked the line, the way the coordinator had ticked hers, and pulled the roller door down behind her.
