Chapter 12 — After

The crew got there at seven minutes past four and worked on him for nineteen minutes and stopped.

They were good. There were two of them and they were quick and neither of them wasted anything, and the older one asked Iris the questions in the order she had asked Caleb them, which meant the order was standard and Iris had learned it in the same place he had. She gave them the pulse at forty-four and the pulse at thirty-one and the interval between, and the times off the stove clock because Caleb had given her the times, and the older one wrote all of it down and thanked her for having it.

“Most people don’t,” he said.

At twenty-nine minutes past four he said the sentence they say and wrote the time on the form, and the time on the form was correct, and it was the last accurate time anyone recorded for Caleb Ward.

Iris stood in the corridor while they did the rest of it and looked at the two cups on the counter through the open door.

***

The administration took the weekend and she did most of it.

Someone had to call his sister. The older paramedic asked her to find a next of kin while he watched, because that was his form to fill in and she was the only person there who had known the man. Iris found the sister in Caleb’s phone and gave him the number and then asked whether she could make the call herself, and he said yes. She made it from the stairwell at ten past five because the stairwell was the only part of the building with no one in it.

Her name was Dana Ward and she was eleven hours away and she had been about to start a shift.

Iris told her plainly and in order. What time, where, that it had been quick at the end and had not been painful at any point that Caleb had reported, and that a crew had been there. Dana Ward asked four questions and Iris answered all four accurately, and then she asked the fifth one.

“Was he on his own?”

“No,” Iris said. “I was there for about fifty minutes.”

She heard what that did on the other end of the line, and she registered that she had supplied the only useful thing available in the whole call, and that supplying it had cost her nothing and had not been kindness in any sense she could locate.

“Thank you,” Dana Ward said. “Thank you. I don’t know who you are and thank you.”

“I’m going to give you a number for the coroner’s office,” Iris said, and gave it, and made her read it back.

***

After that it was forms.

The landlord wanted to know about the apartment and Iris told him what she knew and gave him the sister’s number and did not give him the sister’s number until she had asked the sister. The yard wanted to know whether Caleb had been on shift. A woman from the coroner’s office called twice and was apologetic both times, and Iris told her twice that she did not mind. It was true, and it was not true for any reason the woman would have wanted to hear.

There would be a post-mortem, because there had to be. They would find a cardiac cause and it would be real and it would go on the certificate, and when it came Iris would not be able to argue with a line of it.

She thought about the paramedic on the north side nine days earlier, standing at the back of an ambulance with a tablet turned face down, saying that they would find something and it would be true and it would not be the reason he was sitting there.

They would find it. It would be true.

The service was going to be on the Thursday. Iris worked out on the Sunday that she was not going to go, and worked out an hour after that that she would be able to explain not going to everyone’s satisfaction including her own, and decided that being able to explain it was not the same as it being all right.

***

She checked on the first day at nine thirty in the evening, sitting at the table with the screens off.

The procedure was the one she had used since January. She went and put her attention on the place, and waited the amount of time that a thing takes to arrive if it is going to arrive, and it did not.

*Day 46. Checked for response to CW. None.*

She checked on the second day at about the same time and wrote the same card with the date changed, and she noticed while she was writing it that she had used the initials both times rather than the name, and she looked at that for a while and could not decide whether it meant anything, and wrote a third card saying that she could not decide.

On the second night she took both cards down off the frame and read them together, because two null results in the same hand are a series and a series is worth reading.

*Day 46. Checked for response to CW. None.* *Day 47. Checked for response to CW. None.*

They were accurate. They were in the shortest form that would still mean something to her in a week, which was the standard she had set in January and had kept to, and she read them twice and got out of them exactly what was in them.

She put them back up.

She had written *the difference is not direction* in the winter, about him, and had meant that he was further along one line than she was. The line had ended. She had been on it that whole time and she was still on it, and the correction that needed making to the note was a correction of tense and nothing else.

She made it. It took eleven seconds.

***

She told Kade on the Monday morning, at the unit, because she had asked him to come out and he had not asked her why.

Kade interrupted people. He listened to the whole of it without interrupting, and then put the box he was carrying down on the flat crates instead of on the floor where it belonged.

“He was on the site log,” Kade said. “In the winter. I sent you his name.”

“Yes.”

“That’s how you got to him.”

“That’s how I got to him.”

He stood with his hand flat on the top of the box.

“I have been through those manifests eleven times looking for what I moved,” he said. “I never once went back and looked at what I sent you toward.”

There was an answer to that and Iris had it before he finished the sentence. She checked it for accuracy, and it was accurate, and she gave it to him.

“You sent me to a working site to interview a supervisor about a schedule,” she said. “It was a correct assignment and I would have found him inside a week without you. The interval you are looking for is the one between a woman walking into his apartment and him stopping in a chair, and it is about two hours long, and there is nothing in it that you did.”

Kade did not say anything for a while.

“That’s the same thing you told me about the crates.”

“It’s the same shape. It’s also true.”

“I know it’s true.” He picked the box back up. “Three weeks ago I told you not to get better at it.”

“You did.”

“You’ve got better at it,” he said, and took the box down the row.

***

Rourke’s office on the Monday afternoon had the blinds down on the window side because the afternoon came in hard on that face of the building, and the second desk had four stacks on it instead of three.

Iris put the folder on the desk and did not open it.

“Caleb Ward,” she said. “Thirty-nine. He is in your set. You confirmed it in February when I gave you the nine names, and he is the second line on that sheet.”

“I know where he is.”

“He was assessed by me on the third of March and he was stable. I have three years of him and nine weeks of him at close interval. On Tuesday he was working. On Friday at two he called me, and at four thirty he was dead, and between those two times he answered forty questions correctly and could not produce a reason to leave a room.”

Rourke did not sit down.

“Cause on the certificate,” she said.

“Cardiac. It is correct and I have no argument with it.”

“Then what are you bringing me.”

“The interval,” Iris said. “Tuesday to Friday. And the fact that he was already in your set, and had been stable in it for nine weeks, and was not on the way anywhere.”

Rourke stood with her hand on the back of the chair, which was where she put it when she was not going to like the next part, and looked at the folder without touching it.

“You’re telling me it came back,” she said.

“I’m telling you he was one thing on Tuesday and another thing on Friday, and that the first thing had been steady for nine weeks, and that I do not have a mechanism.”

“No.”

“No.”

“And this is not the thing I asked you for.”

“No,” Iris said. “I never got you a name in advance. I could tell you the magnitude and I could tell you the interval and I could not tell you which person, and a prediction without the person in it is not a prediction.”

Rourke let that sit.

She did not say that the terms had not been met, because they had not, and they both knew the sentence and neither of them was going to spend the afternoon on it. She did not withdraw the terms either. The test simply stopped being the thing that was happening in the room, and it did that without either of them putting a word on it.

“Nine weeks stable,” Rourke said.

“Nine weeks.”

“And nothing in the interval. No infection, no drug, no event, no one new in his life.”

“One person new in his life,” Iris said. “For about a minute and a half, on the Friday.”

Rourke looked at her for a long moment.

“I am not going to be able to use that,” she said.

“I know.”

“I want you to understand what I mean by that. I am not saying it is not true. I have four hundred and sixteen people and eleven deaths on that curve I can defend and a twelfth one on the desk in front of me, and a curve I have never once been able to explain, and I have been carrying it for eight weeks and I am tired, and I am telling you that I cannot use it.” She stopped. “Those are two different sentences and I have stopped being able to say the first one.”

***

She went to the second desk and stood there and did not pick anything up.

“What is it like,” she said.

Iris waited to see whether the question was going to become a clinical one.

“The delay,” Rourke said. “You told me in March that the work hadn’t changed speed. Everything else has. I have asked four hundred people about this and I have never once asked it of someone who could tell me.”

“It isn’t slowness,” Iris said. “Slowness would be a thing I could feel happening. The instruction arrives and it is correct and it is late, and I only know it is late because I have the record. On Friday I went past a turning that would have saved two minutes and the reason to take it caught up with me four seconds after the intersection. I did not experience anything at the intersection. I experienced it afterward, as an entry.”

“And the rest of it.”

“The rest of it is a place where something used to start. I check it. It is smooth. It has been smooth since January and I have never once caught it happening.”

Rourke did not move for a while.

Then she nodded, once, and went on standing there, and Iris understood a few seconds later that the thing that was not happening was that Rourke had not written any of it down.

The pad was on the desk under her hand. She had picked it up in March in front of Iris, written three lines and turned it face down, and she had it under her hand now and left it there.

***

“Everything in the records room,” Rourke said. “All of it. The whole cohort, unredacted, and the eleven, and the intake notes, which I have never given anyone outside this department.”

“All right.”

“You will not tell anyone where it came from. If you are asked I will say you did not get it from me, and I will keep saying that, and I will be believed, because I am the one they will ask.” She said it without any drama on it at all. “I want you to know in advance that I will deny it, so that when I do it is not something that happened to you.”

“Understood.”

“Take it in batches. If it all goes missing at once, someone notices.”

Iris put the folder back in her bag, and Rourke picked her coat up off the second desk and put one arm into it and stopped with the other arm out.

“You asked me in March what I wrote on the pad.”

“Yes.”

“Two lines were the presentation and the date. The third was a date, nine weeks out from the day I wrote it, and a question mark.” She got the second arm in. “I have thought about that piece of paper every day since.”

She took her keys off the desk and went out.

***

Iris drove back with the window down two inches because the van had been in the sun.

Rourke had written it on Day 24. Nine weeks was sixty-three days. That put the date on the pad at Day 87, and today was Day 48.

Thirty-nine days, from a clinician who had been doing this for nineteen years and had refused, in front of her, to say one thing she could not defend.

She held it the way she held everything now. She looked for the place where a number like that arrives and does something, and went and put her attention on it, and waited the length of time it takes.

Then she wrote *thirty-nine days* on a card and put it above the doorframe with the readings that had not moved, and it was the sixth card in that row.

***

She did not check for him that evening.

She noticed it at about eleven, standing at the sink, and the noticing was not a feeling and did not turn into one. She had checked on the first day and the second day and had written both of them down, and the third card was still blank on the table where she had left it that morning, and she had not written on it because she had not run the check, and she had not run the check because she had not gone to the table to do it.

She stood with the tap running and worked out what she was looking at.

It was the first measurement she had abandoned since January.

She could reconstruct the case for abandoning it. Two null results are a result, a third adds nothing, and the schedule had been hers to set and hers to stop. It was a good case. She had built it in under four seconds and it was sound and she could have submitted it.

She turned the tap off.

Then she went back to the table and picked the blank card up and looked at it, and put it down again without writing anything on it, and left it there.
