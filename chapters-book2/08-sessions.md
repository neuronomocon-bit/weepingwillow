Chapter 8 — Sessions

Marin took the far chair, because the near one belonged to the witness. The observation room had been a storage closet on the original plans, and someone had put a window into one wall, run power for a monitor stand, and left it at that. It held two chairs because the protocol required a witness for the first four sessions of any course.

The paragraph about family was on page six. Marin had written it herself in the second year. Immediate family of a patient should not observe, for the patient’s sake and for their own, and where observation cannot be avoided it should be disclosed to the treating clinician in advance.

She disclosed it to Dr. Feld in advance. Feld heard her out, said, “You’ll hate it,” and went in to start the session.

Feld was good. Marin had checked, in the two weeks between the baseline and the first Tuesday. She had read six years of Feld’s outcome data, gone through her review notes, and asked two people she trusted what she was like in a room. None of that had been proper and she had done it anyway. The answers had been consistent. Thorough, slow, hard to rush, and a little cold on paper. Warm in person. Marin had wanted someone excellent and had been prepared to accept someone unkind. She had not needed to.

The first session was orientation and went nowhere near the target structures. Feld spent forty minutes on how the room worked, another forty on what Willow was allowed to stop, and the rest on nothing much at all. The array came down off its arm twice and did not touch her. On the monitor stand beside Marin, the session load climbed off zero, held at a number that meant almost nothing was happening, and came back down. The witness that week was a second-year fellow who signed the sheet, sat down with a tablet, and did other work for the whole two hours.

Willow spent a good deal of the hour being funny about the chair.

Marin sat in the far seat and did hate it, accurately and without surprise.

***

She went down twice in the second week, because the research side needed her signature on a consumables order and because the archive had flagged three baseline maps for identifier collisions, hers among them. The lower level was colder than the clinical wing by four degrees and always had been. The cooling load down there was enormous and the corridor had never felt like anything but the outside of a machine.

Virek was at his terminal with three curves up and a sandwich he had clearly forgotten about.

“Dr. Halloway.”

“Simon. The collision on the baseline set. Did that resolve?”

“It resolved. Two of them were the same person entered twice by different referrers.” He turned in the chair. “The third one was yours. MN-R-0338 has been issued before. Veterans’ cohort, course closed out three years ago, and the identifier recycled on schedule when the retention window ran out. That part’s fine. What didn’t happen is the closed record coming off the live index when it went.”

“So the number is sitting on two people.”

“The number is sitting on two people. One of them finished a course three years ago and one of them started one two weeks back.” He said it the way he would have said a stock level. “It’s hers now. The old entry’s the one that’s in the wrong place, and I’ve put it to records.”

“Will records do it?”

“Records will get to it.”

Neither of them said anything about how long that usually took.

“Did anything open it in the meantime?”

“Nothing opened it. I checked the log because I assumed you’d ask.” He said this without any edge on it. “It’s clean. Two reads, both mine, both timestamped after I raised it.”

“Thank you.”

“It’s what the log is for.”

She signed the consumables order against the wall, because there was no surface on the lower level that was not already holding something, and went back up.

***

The second session went further in. The third one arrived on a Tuesday with rain coming sideways off the water, and Marin sat in the far chair with the telemetry scrolling and watched her sister do a thing she had not been able to do in nine years.

Feld took her to it slowly. She spent a long time first on ordinary logistics, on bus routes and shift patterns and a landlord. None of it was filler. Then she asked her to start at the beginning and go through it in order, and to stop whenever she wanted to.

Willow started at the beginning and went through it in order.

Marin had heard her sister try this four times. Once at twenty-three, in a kitchen, and it had ended with Willow leaving the house. Once to a clinician who had not been good enough. Once at their mother’s, badly. Once in a car outside a hospital, at three in the morning, and that time she had gotten perhaps a third of the way and stopped, and Marin had driven them both home without either of them saying anything else.

This time she got to the end of it.

She had the sequence right. She corrected herself once on a date, went back and fixed it, and carried on. The correction cost her nothing. She used the past tense the whole way through. She did not stop in the middle. Twice she said something so exact about what it had been like that Marin, on the other side of the glass, had to sit forward and put her elbows on her knees.

The session load on the monitor rose steadily through it and held near the top of the working band. The rise was the substrate taking the charge off the structure while Willow told it.

Marin found that she had started the timer on the stand at the beginning of the account.

Eleven minutes and forty seconds. She wrote it down. It went into the observation column as a duration, because that was the column it belonged in.

When it was finished Willow sat back and let out a breath and said, “Is that it?”

“That’s it for today,” Feld said.

“No, I mean.” Willow looked at her. “Is that it. That’s the whole thing. I’ve just said the whole thing out loud in a room.”

“You have.”

“Huh,” said Willow.

The fellow entered ninety minutes in the field the form gave him, signed out at the door, and went to whatever was next. Marin sat in the far chair for some time after they had all gone, until the cleaner came to the door and found her there, and apologized, and went away again.

Feld came back for a tablet she had left on the monitor stand. She saw Marin still sitting there and did not remark on it.

“You’re keeping your own number,” Feld said.

“One number.”

“I know which one.” Feld put the tablet under her arm. “Ask me at six sessions and I’ll have something worth telling you.”

“That wasn’t what I was going to ask.”

Feld waited. She could stand in a doorway indefinitely.

“She got to the end of it,” Marin said.

“She did.”

“She has never once gotten to the end of it.”

“I know. I read the history before I took her.” Feld shifted the tablet. “You’re allowed to be pleased about it. It doesn’t compromise anything.”

***

By the fourth week Terri knew her by name and had a running thing with her at the desk.

“Any word on that outcome measure?” Willow said, signing in.

“Nothing yet.”

“It’s been a month, Terri.”

“These things go to a committee.”

“Everything here goes to a committee. I read eleven pages about it.” Willow put the stylus down. “I’m just saying, if I do come out funnier, somebody’s going to want to know, and it’s going to be too late to start collecting the data.”

“I’ve made a note.”

“You have not made a note.”

“I’ve made a note,” Terri said, “and it says *patient asks weekly,* which is itself a finding.”

Willow laughed and went through. Marin, waiting by the corridor door with two coffees she had no intention of admitting she had bought forty minutes early, held one out to her sister as she came past.

“You’re loitering,” Willow said.

“I have a legitimate reason to be in this corridor.”

“You have two coffees.”

“One of them is mine.”

“They’re both the same order, Marin, you’ve been buying me the same coffee since I was nineteen, you don’t even know what I drink now.” She took it anyway and drank it as they walked. “It’s fine. It’s what I drink now.”

***

The fourth session ran short.

Feld came out first, said it had gone well and that her notes would be up by the evening, and went to find her next patient. Willow came out about a minute behind her, moving unhurriedly, with her jacket over her arm.

They walked back up the clinical corridor together. The overhead light held even from one end to the other, no stretch of it darker than the rest, exactly as specified.

“I’ll tell you the strange part,” Willow said.

“Tell me the strange part.”

“It’s still there.” She said it easily. “All of it. I could give you the whole thing right now, standing here, dates and everything. Feld had me do it twice today just to check nothing had gone missing and nothing had. It’s all completely there.”

“That’s what it does.”

“I know that’s what it does. You explained it at me for twenty minutes with your hands flat on a table.” Willow shifted the jacket to her other arm. “But here’s what I can’t get across to her, and maybe I can get it across to you. It’s still there. I just can’t find the handle on it anymore.”

Marin turned that over.

“Say more about the handle.”

“It used to have one. You’d go near the thing and there’d be a place where it grabbed you, and that’s how you knew to go the other way. Now I go near it and it’s smooth. It’s the same object. There’s just nowhere on it that catches.” She thought about it. “It’s good. I put my hand on it this morning and nothing happened to me. Do you understand how long it’s been since nothing happened to me?”

“I do.”

“So it’s good.”

“It’s good,” Marin said.

They got to the end of the corridor. Willow had a bus to catch and a shift starting at four, which had not been true at Christmas. She took the stairs down two at a time and shouted something rude about the coffee from the landing.

Marin went back to her office and opened the session file. The outcome measures for week four ran to a page: retrieval intact, sequence intact, affective load reduced against baseline, patient-reported distress reduced against baseline, no reported change to declarative content.

A free-text field at the bottom asked for patient-reported experience. She typed: *Patient reports target structure fully accessible with no experienced affective engagement. “It’s still there. I just can’t find the handle on it anymore.” Patient volunteers this as a positive outcome.*

She read it back. It was an accurate record of what had been said. She saved it and closed the file. Then she sat for a while with her hand still on the mouse. Virek had asked her that morning to come by when she had twenty minutes, so she went down to the lower level.

***

The substrate hall ran two hundred feet and had been a plant room before it was anything else. Cabinet runs went down both sides at chest height, in a gray that had come with the cabinets. The cooling overhead was loud enough that people who worked down there raised their voices out of habit and went on doing it in the stairwell. The level had no windows, and the light at four in the afternoon was the light at midnight.

The substrate itself was in the third and fourth runs and there was nothing to see. Marin had brought a funder down once, early on, and he had walked the length of it and asked when they were going to get to it.

Virek had two stations pushed together and a curve up on the middle screen.

“Thank you for coming down,” he said. “This is probably nothing.”

“All right.”

“It’s inside tolerance. Everything I’m about to show you is inside tolerance and I’ve checked it twice, so if the answer is that it’s inside tolerance, I’ll take that and go home.”

“Simon.”

“Yes.”

“Just show me.”

He brought the curve forward. It was residual coherence against time, from session close, with the model overlaid in a lighter trace.

“Flush cycle,” he said. “Session ends, we flush the substrate, the patient’s material comes out. The flush window is ninety minutes. By the end of the window residual coherence is supposed to be under zero point one percent of session load, and the tolerance we operate to is two percent, so there’s a lot of daylight in there.”

“I know what the flush is, Simon.”

“I know you do. I’m saying it out loud because I’ve been down here on my own with it for a week and I’ve stopped being able to tell which parts are obvious.” He put his finger on the first part of the curve. “So. It drops. It drops fast and it drops on the model, all the way down to about here.”

“And then it stops dropping.”

“It stops dropping.” He traced it flat. “It shelves at about zero point four. Then it sits there. It’s still sitting there at the end of the window. It’s still sitting there the next morning, and then the next session starts and the whole thing goes up again and comes down and shelves at about the same place.”

“Zero point four against a tolerance of two.”

“Against a tolerance of two. That’s why I said it’s probably nothing.” He scrolled back. “But the model doesn’t shelf. The model goes to the floor. The number’s fine. The shape is the part I can’t account for, and I can’t make the model produce a shelf no matter what I put into it.”

Marin looked at it for a while. Behind them the cooling changed pitch as something upstream cycled, and changed back.

“How many sessions?”

“Nineteen that I’ve got clean data for. It’s across the board, every patient on the schedule.” He hesitated. “Your sister’s is in there. I didn’t pull it out and I didn’t look at anything except the coherence trace, but I’d rather tell you it’s in there than have you find it.”

“Thank you.” She meant it. “What have you ruled out?”

“Instrumentation first. I swapped the sampling head on run three and got the same shelf, so it isn’t the head. Then I thought it might be the flush not completing, so I ran a manual flush after the automatic one on four sessions last week. Same shelf. Then I ran a clean cycle with no patient material in the substrate at all, and it goes to the floor, on the model, every time, so it isn’t the substrate holding a bias.” He turned his palms up. “Which leaves the material. Something in the patient material is decaying slower than the model expects it to.”

“Is the limiter reporting?”

“The limiter’s reporting green and I’ve read the log line by line for the whole nineteen. No integration events. Not one.” He said it with some relief. “Which is the thing that makes me comfortable, more or less. If the limiter were passing then I’d be up in your office instead of asking you to come down.”

He had the order of concern right and Marin said so. The integration limiter was the partition. The substrate was built to take a patient’s emotional structure in, hold it, work on it, and hand it back. The limiter kept it holding that structure as the patient’s rather than absorbing it as its own. Material that had never been integrated could be flushed. That was the whole basis of the flush. In Marin’s opinion it was the single most important component in the building, and it was also about four hundred lines of software, substantially unchanged in six years.

“And autonomy’s still at two,” Virek said.

“Autonomy’s at two.”

“So there’s nothing running down here between sessions anyway. It can’t hold structure without an operator session open. Whatever’s shelving, it’s shelving in a room with the lights off.” He scratched the back of his neck. “I’ve told myself that about four times this week and it’s held up every time.”

“Have you taken it to Raman?”

“I was going to take it to you first and see if you told me it was nothing.”

“I’m not going to tell you it’s nothing. I’m going to tell you it’s inside tolerance, which is different, and then I’m going to log it.”

***

Raman came down at ten past five with his coat already on, because he had been on his way out and had turned around, which was the most that could reasonably be asked of a program director at ten past five.

He looked at the curve for longer than Marin expected.

“Zero point four,” he said.

“Zero point four. Tolerance is two.”

“Nineteen sessions?”

“Nineteen clean. There are more, I haven’t got clean data for all of them,” Virek said.

“Instrumentation?”

“Ruled out. Sampling head swap, manual flush, clean cycle.”

“Good. That’s the right order.” Raman stood back from the screen with his coat still buttoned. “So here’s what I think, and tell me where it fails. That model was fitted on the veterans’ cohort. Four years of it, and every one of those courses was running a session load somewhere between a third and a half of what you’re putting through the substrate now. We opened the cohort up and the expansion cases come in heavier. Considerably heavier. A tail that fattens when you double the load isn’t a surprise. It would be more of a surprise if it didn’t.”

Virek thought about it. Marin watched him think about it, and watched the exact moment where it went from a thing he was holding to a thing he had put down.

“That would produce a shelf,” he said slowly. “If the decay is load-dependent and the model treats it as fixed. Yes. That would do it.”

“It would also mean the model’s out of date rather than the substrate being interesting, which is duller and likelier.” Raman looked at Marin. “You’ll log it?”

“I’ve logged it.”

“Then what I’d rather not do is take the substrate down for three weeks and re-fit a model to tell us that a number inside tolerance is inside tolerance. If it moves, that’s different. If it goes to one, I want to know the same day.”

“It hasn’t moved in nineteen sessions,” Virek said. “It shelves within a couple of hundredths of the same place every time.”

“That’s steady, then.” Raman stepped back toward the door. “Steady and inside tolerance and explained by the load. Keep watching it. Tolerance is tolerance, Simon.”

“Tolerance is tolerance,” Virek agreed.

It was a good answer. It accounted for the observation, it named a mechanism, it made a testable prediction about what the residue would do if the load went up, and nothing in it required pretending the shelf was not there. Marin had sat through a great many worse answers from a great many worse directors.

Raman said something about the procurement cycle and went home.

***

Marin logged it properly before she left, which took nine minutes. The entry carried the curve, Virek’s exclusions, Raman’s account and its prediction, and the review threshold of one percent. She copied Feld in on nothing, because it was not a clinical matter. She left her sister’s identifier in the dataset, because pulling it would have been the only irregular act in the building that day.

The lot was almost empty by the time she got out to the car. The fog had come back in over the water and taken the far end of the building with it, and the sodium lights along the fence line had that dirty orange they got before they were fully warm.

One window was lit on the lower level, at the north end, where the substrate hall came up to grade.

Virek staying late, then. He would be re-running the decay curve, because that was what he did with a thing he had agreed to put down. It would come back inside tolerance and he would go home.

She got in the car, put the heater on for the windshield, and sat waiting for it to clear.

Her sister had gotten to the end of it. That was the thing sitting in her chest, still, at twenty to six on a Tuesday. Nine years, and four attempts, and a car outside a hospital at three in the morning, and then eleven minutes and forty seconds on the other side of a pane of glass, and then a question about whether that was it.

The windshield cleared from the middle outward. Marin put the car in gear, drove down the access road to the coast road, and turned south toward the town.
