Chapter 13 — The Water

The call came at twenty past three on a Thursday afternoon in September, and Marin was in a procurement meeting when it went, and she let it go because she was in a procurement meeting.

It went again eleven minutes later. She took that one in the corridor.

The woman on the line was from the county and she was careful and she was reading from something. She wanted to confirm that Marin was the person named on a form. Marin confirmed it. She wanted to confirm the relationship. Marin confirmed that too. Then she said the rest of it in the order it is said in. Name first, then found, then where. Marin stood in the corridor outside the room she had just walked out of and listened to all of it and did not interrupt.

She had written the paragraph about next of kin herself, in the second year, on page seven. It was two sentences long. It said that the field was to be completed in the patient’s own hand and that the patient was to be given a moment with it, because people put down whoever they have and sometimes discover while they are doing it who that is.

Willow had put her down and then held the pen over the box a second longer, in a room with a window that showed the water when the fog allowed.

The woman on the line asked whether Marin could come.

***

The corridor at the county hospital was on the ground floor and had a bench that had been put there for people who needed to sit down suddenly, and a poster about hand hygiene with a corner curling away from the wall.

Marin sat on the bench for forty minutes and answered forms.

They were good to her. A woman brought her tea she did not drink. A man in his fifties told her what they had, in order, and stopped every so often to check that she was still following. She was still following.

She had gone in off the rocks below the bluff on the north stretch, about a mile and a half up the coast from the site. A man walking a dog had seen a coat in the water at ten past one and had called it in from the road. The tide had been running out for two hours by then. She had been found the same afternoon, a little under half a mile down, which was consistent with the tide and with where she had gone in.

There was no note. There was nothing in her pockets but keys and a bus ticket.

No one had seen her go in.

The man in his fifties said the word accident twice, and both times he said it as a category rather than as a finding. Marin noticed that the care was practiced. He had done this before and would do it again on some other afternoon for someone else.

“There’ll be an inquest,” he said. “That’s routine. It doesn’t mean anything by itself.”

“I know what an inquest is.”

“Yes,” he said. “I thought you might.”

He asked whether she wanted to see her sister and she said yes, and a woman took her down and stood in the doorway while she did it and did not watch her.

He asked whether there was anyone he could call for her and she said no, and he asked again in a different way about twenty minutes later, and she said no again, and he let it go.

She drove herself home. It was one of the four or five days a year when the sun goes down over the water on that road, and she watched it do that for eleven miles, and got in at twenty past seven.

***

For six years she had heard that water through the floor of the intake suite.

It comes up through the building rather than through the air on that stretch, a low irregular knock under the boards, and everyone who works there stops hearing it inside a week. She had sat in that room with her sister for two hours in the fall and had not heard it once.

She thought about that on the Friday, and then she stopped thinking about it, because it was not information and it was going to be available to her for the rest of her life.

***

The report, when it came, said accident.

The tide was running. The rocks on that stretch are wet at the top for two feet at low water and there is weed on them and people go on them anyway, and four people had gone into the water off that mile of coast in nine years and three of them had been walking. There was no note. There was no message. There was nothing in the eleven days before it that anyone could point to.

The report is probably right.

Marin read it twice, once when it came and once six weeks later, and could not fault a line of it, and understood both times that the report was not answering the question she had.

***

She went to the apartment on the Sunday, because the landlord had asked and because it was going to have to be someone.

The mail was on the mat inside the door in a drift. She picked it up and sorted it out of habit, and it went back three weeks, and in the middle of it was a reminder for the electricity that had been sent because the first one had not been answered.

In April the mail had been in a stack on the counter with the envelopes slit and the contents put back.

The calendar was on the fridge where it had been. Marin took it down and looked at it. June had shifts on it in the small square handwriting, and July had shifts on it for the first two weeks, and after that the boxes were empty. August was empty. September was empty. It was the same calendar and the same pen and the writing simply stopped.

She checked the fridge because she was standing in front of it. There was food in there that had been bought and not opened. A chicken. A bag of salad gone to liquid at the bottom. Two yogurts inside their date and one a week past it. Someone had gone to a shop and made a list of things a person eats in a week and bought all of them.

The book was on the arm of the couch, face-down, at page four.

It was a different book from the one in April. Marin turned it over and the receipt was still in it, folded once, from the twelfth of August, and it was in the fold at page four.

She stood in the front room with a paperback in her hand and worked out that everything she was looking at could be read two ways, and that she was going to do the reading anyway, and that there was no version of the rest of her life in which she stopped.

***

She found the course paperwork in a drawer with the takeout menus.

Two places on the supervisor course, starting the second week of September, and a form with Willow’s name printed at the top and a line for a signature and nothing on the line. Neal had given it to her in June. It had been in the drawer since June.

Marin looked at that for a while and then put it back in the drawer, because she could not think who it was for.

The last thing she took out of the apartment was the calendar, and she did not know why she took it, and she took it.

***

On the Tuesday she opened the session file.

The research side was hers. It had always been hers, and she had told her sister so at a table with a consent pack open between them, and she had said *on everyone who doesn’t already know* and had known what that meant when she said it.

The twelfth session had run to fifty-eight minutes and there was a recording, because there was a recording of all of them.

Willow was fine in it.

She was fine at the start and fine in the middle and fine at the end. She answered Feld’s questions properly and made one joke at about nineteen minutes that Feld took a second too long to catch. The outcome measures at the end of it were the best of the course. Affective load against the target structure reduced, patient-reported distress reduced, no reported change to declarative content, and she said, when Feld asked her the standard closing question, that she was doing well.

Marin watched it four times.

She watched it once through, and then twice more at the section around the nineteenth minute, and then once through again from the beginning. On the fourth pass she made herself write down what she was looking for, because a search with no defined target is not a search, and she wrote *any deviation from baseline behavior in the recorded sessions* and looked at it and knew before she started that it was going to come back empty.

It came back empty. Willow was fine in all twelve.

That was not evidence of anything. A person can be fine in a room with a clinician on a Tuesday and it tells you about the room.

***

Neal called her on the Thursday, about the form.

He had heard on the Monday from a girl on the evening shift who had seen it in the paper, and he had waited three days because he had not been able to work out whether it was his place, and he said all of that inside the first twenty seconds like a man getting rid of it.

Marin asked him how she had been.

She heard herself do it. She heard the shape of the question while it was still in her mouth and she knew that it was an interview and she asked it anyway.

Neal thought about it properly. She had not expected that. He said she had been good. July had been fine and August had been fine and she had picked up two extra shifts in August that she had not been asked for. He said she had been quieter than she was in the spring, and then he said that a lot of people are quieter in August. He said she had turned the supervisor course down like someone who might come back around to it.

Marin asked whether she had said why.

“She said she wanted to think about it,” Neal said. “People say that. I didn’t push it.”

“No. There’s no reason you would have.”

He was quiet for a second and then he said that he had liked her, that she was the only one on the floor who could make the eleven o’clock delivery funny, and that he was sorry, and that he would say something on Friday if that was all right with the family.

Marin said that it was, and that there was only her.

She put the phone down and stood with her hand still on it.

That was the best account anyone was ever going to be able to give her of the last eight weeks of her sister’s life. It had come from a man who runs a store. There was nothing in it, and it was not his fault, and there was nothing after it.

***

Meridian had a process for it and the process ran.

A patient death inside twelve months of a completed course goes to review whether or not it looks related, because the alternative is a program that decides in advance what is worth looking at. Raman told her so in his office with the door shut, and Marin said that she knew, because she had written the trigger criteria in the third year.

She recused herself before he asked her to, and was glad afterward that it had gone in that order.

The review took nine days. It looked at the course, the outcome measures, every session record, the six-month review she had attended in August, the flush logs for all twelve sessions, and twelve-month mortality in the treated population against the referral population. It found no association and said so in four pages.

Every line of it was correct.

It could not have found anything else. There is nothing in a session record that reports on a Thursday in September. The outcome measures had been the best of the course. The population figure was small enough that the interval on it was wide enough to drive a van through, and the review said that too, in the section where a review says that.

Raman brought her the four pages himself instead of having them sent.

“There’s nothing in it,” he said. “I’m giving it to you anyway. You would have had it out of the system by Friday and I would rather you had it from me.”

***

She understood it standing in the kitchen with the calendar on the counter, and it took about four seconds, and it did not change shape afterward.

There is a moment in which a person stops. It is not a decision and it does not look like one from the outside and there is no name for it in any literature Marin had ever read. It is a thing that has kept people alive when they were standing somewhere they should not have been standing, and it does not announce itself, and she had never found a way to measure it, because it only shows up when it is absent and then the person is not available to be asked.

For six years Marin had removed the charge from structures that were firing when they should not have been firing. She had done it correctly. She had done it on a program that worked, with consent, under review, on four hundred people and then on her sister.

She was never going to know whether those were the same mechanism.

She built the study anyway, standing there, because building the study was what she did. It would need the same person twice. It would need one of her treated and one of her not, in the same September, on the same stretch of rock, and everything else held. There is one of her. There was one of her.

There is no design. She went through it three times looking for one and there is no design, and she was better at designing studies than almost anyone she had ever worked with.

She put the calendar in a drawer.

***

Her sleep latency went from about eleven minutes to somewhere over an hour and stayed there for five weeks.

Her appetite was unaffected. That surprised her, and she recorded it as unaffected. She lost no weight. She did not drink more than she had drunk in August. She did not cry at the service on the Friday, twenty-six people at it, most of them from the store, and Neal spoke for two minutes and did it well.

Her functional impairment at work was minimal and she monitored it. She did not miss a deadline in the quarter. She re-ran a consumables reconciliation in October that she had already run in September and did not notice until the second one came out identical, and she wrote that down and watched for it and it did not happen again.

She could have written all of it up. It would have been a clean case note. Everything in it was true and in the right units, and there was not one word in any of it that touched what had happened, and she knew that, and she went on using the units because they were the ones she had.

***

The Monday was four days after the call. She went to work.

She was in at ten past eight, her normal time, and she had the consumables order signed by nine and took the ten o’clock, and at two she was on the lower level with Virek looking at a curve that had not moved.

“You didn’t have to come back in this week,” he said.

“No.”

“I mean it. Nobody would have said anything.”

“I know.”

He let it go. She noted that and was grateful for it, and he turned the screen so she could see the flat part of it.

The shelf was at zero point four.

It had been zero point four when Virek first brought it to her and it was zero point four now, across thirty-one clean sessions instead of nineteen, and it had not moved by more than a couple of hundredths in either direction in all that time.

“Still steady,” Virek said.

“Still steady.”

“Tolerance is tolerance,” he said, and it was not a joke and he did not say it like one, and Marin looked at the flat part of the line for a while longer.

“Log it,” she said.

“It’s logged.”
