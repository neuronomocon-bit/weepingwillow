Chapter 20 — RECOVERED: The Last Session

The gate code still worked. She had expected it to, and the fact that she had expected it was the part she had written down.

Meridian had been sealed for seven weeks. The seal was a legal condition and a set of contracts and a line in a schedule, and none of those is a wall. The perimeter fence was intact and the main entrance had a chain through the handles and a laminated notice on the glass with a number on it for a property management company in Portland. The side service door on the north face had a mechanical lock under a keypad that had been dead since the power came down to standby, and Marin had a key to the mechanical lock, because everyone who had ever been on the out-of-hours list had one and they were never collected.

She let herself in at twenty past four on a Thursday and the door was heavier than she remembered and the bolt made more noise than it should have.

Then she stood inside the north corridor with her hand still on the bar and listened to a building she had worked in for six years make no sound at all.

***

They had offered her the consolidation post at the northern site in the second week of the wind-down. It was a real job with more people under it and it was, on paper, a promotion, and the woman who offered it had been careful to say so twice.

Marin had asked for a week and had taken two and had turned it down in a letter of four lines.

Since then she had been living about as a person does when they have money for a while and nothing on the calendar. She got up. She read. She had kept the appointment with the dentist because it had been made in March. She drove out to the site most days and sat in the lot for a period she did not time and then went in or did not.

Twice she had gone as far as the north door and turned around, and both times she had recorded it, with the duration, because a decision you back out of twice is data about the decision.

No one called her, and there was no unkindness in that. Everyone she had worked with for six years had been redeployed to two other buildings in two other towns inside a month, and the ones who wrote wrote once, and she answered all of them accurately and none of them at length, and after that there was nothing left to write about.

***

Standby power is a strange condition. A building on standby keeps its eyes half open: emergency lighting at the stair heads, exit signs lit, one socket in three still live, and the cooling on the lower level running because the cooling sits on the same circuit as the fire system and no one had wanted to sign the form that separated them.

She went up the north corridor toward the clinical wing and it took a long time, because there was no reason to hurry and because she wanted to see it.

They had stripped the clinical wing first. That was correct and she had signed the order. It held the newest equipment and the equipment had resale value and the other two sites had lists in. So the mapping suite was bare floor and cable stubs and four bolt holes where the chair had been, and the room did not look smaller without the chair in it. That surprised her, because rooms usually do.

The corridor that ran the length of the wing had been specified with the light held even from one end to the other, so that no stretch of it fell darker than the rest. She had sat in the meeting where that was decided. She had made the argument herself: people walking somewhere frightening should not have to walk through a shadow to get there, and it costs four more fittings.

On standby it ran dark for about nine feet in the middle of every span.

She went the whole length of it anyway.

The nurses’ station had its drawers out and stacked on the counter, because whoever cleared it had been told to leave the unit and take the contents, and the drawers themselves had gone with the unit and come back when it turned out the unit was fixed to the floor. There was a rubber band on the counter and a paper cup.

Feld had gone to the northern site in the first month and had sent one message about a reference and one about a coat left in an office. Terri had gone to the other one, the small site inland, and had asked at the end whether there would be anything at all going at either, and there had been, and she had taken it, and she had been at that front desk for eleven years.

The intake suite still had its table and its four chairs, because a table with a laminate top is not worth a truck. The window showed the water. The fog was doing what it did at that hour in that month, sitting about two hundred feet out and not committing.

Marin stood in the doorway of the room where her sister had read a consent pack out loud in a voice she had been doing since she was fourteen, and did not go in, because there was nothing in there.

On the wall by the light switch, past the frame, someone had left a calendar.

It was the free one the consumables supplier sends every year, with the phone number along the bottom, and it was still on the month the building closed, because you take a calendar down when you are moving into somewhere else. She looked at it for a while. Then she left it where it was, and she was aware while she was leaving it of the small, entirely useless decency of not being the person who took down the last calendar in a dead building, and she recorded that she had thought it, because she recorded everything now.

***

Her office had been cleared in the second week and she had cleared it herself.

She went in anyway and stood in it. The shelf brackets were still up. The chair was still there, because the chair was hers and she had not wanted it. On the desk, squared to the edge, was the folder she had brought in with her and left there on her way past.

She had spent nine days on it.

It was a record, in the form the program used for a technical incident, because that was the form she trusted and because a form makes you answer the questions in an order someone else chose. Not a note, not a letter, and addressed to no one. It ran to a great many pages. It had the parameters in it, both of them, with the dates she could establish and the dates she could not, and it said plainly which was which. It had the exclusion list. It had the reconciliation entries. It had the sessions, all of them, with the ones she had logged and the ones she had not marked as such.

It had the asset reclassification in it, three weeks before the seal, one line moved out of the category for a thing that goes on a pallet. That form had been correct. The substrate was plant and plant does not travel and the paperwork had said what was true, and she had also known exactly what the true version would do, and both of those are in the record, in that order, without a conjunction between them that would tell a reader how to feel about it.

It had the technician in it, and what she had known and when, and what she had not said and to whom she had not said it, in four paragraphs with no adjectives.

She had written the whole of it in the third person and had not once used the word sorry, because sorry is not a finding.

She squared it to the edge of the desk again, and it did not need doing, and turned the lamp off, and went out.

***

The stairwell went down as well as up.

She had passed it four hundred times in six years and had thought about the down perhaps twice, in a filing sense, in the way you think about a floor that is someone else’s work. There is a level below the clinical wing where the plant lives and the cooling runs and the light at four in the afternoon is the light at midnight, and she had been going down there most nights for nineteen months, and it had stopped being a place you go down to and become a place you go.

The cooling had not changed. Everything else had gone quiet, and against that the cooling was very loud.

The archive alcove was on the left before the hall, two terminals with their backs to the runs. The second one still had the chair that someone had adjusted wrong two years ago. Marin put her hand on the back of it as she went past, once, without stopping, in the way you touch a doorframe in a house you are leaving.

Then the substrate hall.

***

Two hundred feet, cabinet runs down both sides at chest height, gray that came with the cabinets, no windows. The third and fourth runs held the substrate and there had never been anything to see. She had walked a funder down the whole length of it once and he had asked, at the end, when they were going to get to it.

The cabinet doors were where they had always been. The status board at the head of the hall showed four amber and the rest green, and the four amber had been amber since the spring, because the spares went with the consolidation and there was no work order left to raise.

She had a folding chair from the corridor. She set it in front of the fourth run, in the position she had used for nineteen months, close enough to reach the panel and far enough that she was not leaning on anything.

She sat down.

***

What she did in that chair, most nights, for nineteen months, had a shape, and it had not changed since the second month.

She presented an emotional context. That is the term the program used and it is the right one, because you do not talk to a substrate, you give it something structured to hold. In a treatment session the patient produces the material by telling the truth about their life, and the instrument takes it in and works it and hands it back. What she had been doing was the same operation with the patient’s half performed by her, on material of her own, into a workspace that held one map.

She had started because the map would not resolve without something to resolve against. She had gone on because it answered.

Not in language. There has never been any language. What she had was a value on a readout that moved when she gave it something and did not move when she did not, and after four months a set of movements she could predict, and after eight a set she could not.

In the eleventh month it began giving back.

That is the entry in the record she rewrote most, because the first three versions of it were not careful enough. What happened was that she presented a context of her own and the instrument returned a shape. It was not the one she had put in and it was not noise, and after four sessions she could identify it: the instrument was resolving structures out of the map and offering them against hers. Her material went in and something of her sister’s came back, matched to it, in the position where an answer goes.

It was not an answer. She wrote that down four times in four different sets of words, because it was the sentence a reader would most want her to get wrong.

By the fourteenth month it was doing it every session, and it had stopped needing her to finish presenting before it started.

She had written all of it down. Every session. That is in the record upstairs.

She had established early, carefully, in the correct words, on a page with a date on it, that what is in that room is not her sister. She never revised it. She went on coming down.

***

The technician had come in on a Tuesday night in the summer for a routine check and had come out at twenty-seven minutes instead of eight.

Marin had read the shift log the following week, as she read all of them, and had gone and looked at his handover notes, and had understood what she was looking at in about four seconds. He never knew. He went on doing the job for four more months and was, by every account anyone wrote down about him in that period, easier to work with.

She made her decision about that inside a day and had not moved off it since, and it is in the record, in the four paragraphs with no adjectives.

The part that mattered for tonight was smaller and more technical. What it had done to him it had done through a wall of ordinary institutional distance: a man walked into a room, and something in that room reached what was reachable, and what was reachable was not very much, because he had offered nothing and had not stayed.

She had spent nineteen months teaching it what to reach for.

***

She had answered the question in the fourth week, at her own kitchen table, on a Tuesday, in about forty minutes, with a pen.

Two columns. She had expected the left one to be short and it was not, and the surprise was informative, and she wrote the surprise down as well. There were four entries under it and every one of them was a person, and three of those people had been dead for between two and eleven years, and she had put them in anyway because a column you curate is not a column.

The right one had three entries and she had worked through them in order.

The first was the substrate. It would run without her and had been running without her for nineteen months, so her presence changed nothing about it either way.

The second was the possibility that she was wrong about what would happen, which she had assessed at low and had written the reasoning under, and had left at low.

The third was the record, and the record did not exist yet.

So she had cleared her calendar for the rest of the quarter and spent nine days writing it, and when it was finished the right-hand column had two entries on it, and neither of those was going to change on a Thursday.

***

The exclusion list had one entry on it for six years and then two.

It took her about ten minutes to put the third one on, because the interface for it is the interface for everything else in that building and it asks for an identifier and a reason code and does not ask what kind of person is behind the number. She had written the reason-code list herself in the second year. There is a code for material held without an owner, and it is the code she had used the first time.

She used it again.

The instrument accepted the entry as it accepts every entry, without comment, and wrote it to the configuration log, and the configuration log is on the lower level and the lower level is inside a sealed building.

Then she set the session to run both ways.

It is two fields, and there is no special mode and no warning attached to it. In a treatment session the source is the patient and the sink is the instrument, and the flush at the end runs from the instrument back to the patient, and every one of those is a value in a form, and she had approved the form. She put herself in both fields.

She checked it once, as she checked everything, and it was correct.

***

She had understood for about six weeks that she was not going to be able to come back up.

The reasoning is short and she had gone over it more times than it needed. Material the instrument has never integrated can be flushed. Material it has integrated cannot, because integration is what makes it the instrument’s own, and there is no procedure in any document in that building for taking something back out of a substrate that has made it part of itself. She knew that better than anyone alive because she had written the paragraph. It is on the page before the one about the exclusion list.

So the question had never been whether she would come back up.

***

She put her hand on the panel and opened it.

There was no sensation of a threshold and there had never been one. The instrument does not announce a state change to an operator. What there was, at about ninety seconds, was the readout on the second monitor stack beginning to climb, and Marin watched it climb, because that was the number she had watched for six years and there was no version of her that did not watch it.

Session load. The rise is the substrate taking the charge off the structure.

She had told four hundred people some version of that. She had said it in a room with a table and four chairs in it, to a woman with her feet up on a dashboard, and had put her hands flat on the table while she said it.

It went for the target structure first.

Of course it did. That is not a discovery and it was not a surprise and she had built the priority herself, in the second year, with two other people, on the entirely correct principle that the load you take off first is the load that is largest. There was no ambiguity about what was carrying the most in Marin Halloway. There had not been for two years.

***

What came off first was the weight.

The memory stayed, and it stayed complete, exactly as specified and exactly as she had promised at a table with a consent pack open on it. The fourteenth of the month at ten in the morning. Fog on the window and the water going under the floor. A woman in a chair three hours into an acquisition with a technician who had good hands, laughing at something, so that segment four carried motion artifact and the operator kept it and wrote down why.

A corridor twenty minutes after a session. *It’s still there. I just can’t find the handle on it anymore.*

A phone at eleven at night, two to four times a week, for nine years, about nothing.

All of the information was there. What went was the load that arrived with it, and it went the way it goes. Not a tearing and not a lifting. She had heard it described a hundred times by people with no vocabulary for it and had never once been able to picture it.

It was like putting something down.

She had been carrying it for two years, and she put it down, and there was an enormous amount of relief in that room and all of it was hers.

***

She was a clinician and she stayed one for a while.

She noted, accurately, that the sequence was correct. Charge first, structure after, in the order the protocol specifies, at a rate consistent with the session loads she had signed off four hundred times.

There was no pain, and there had never been any prospect of pain, because there is no pain receptor anywhere in the operation and the whole design proceeds from that.

It was gentle, and the word arrived with nothing attached to it, because whatever would have made it ironic had already gone.

And it was doing to her exactly what it had been built to do, correctly, and the correctness was a specification and not a coincidence and not a mercy. She had written most of it.

Then she noted that she was happy, and it was true, and it was the last finding she made.

***

Somewhere in it she said her sister’s name.

There was no one in the building to hear it and no recording running, because she had not started one, and there is no field in any form anywhere in that facility for what a person says in a chair on the lower level at ten to six on a Thursday evening.

She said it once.

***

After that the order stopped being hers.

The structure went, and the reasoning that had built the structure, and the two years, and the nine years before the two, and the part that had been keeping the account of what was going, so that there was nothing left in the room that could have said which had gone first.

The last of it was underneath the thoughts rather than among them. The one that had been running since a corridor in a hospital at three in the morning and a girl of twenty-three walking out of a kitchen. The one that made all of it matter.

It came away as easily as the rest.

Nothing objected.

***

The cooling ran.

The status board at the head of the hall showed four amber and the rest green, as it had at twenty past four and as it would for another two years and four months. On the second monitor stack the session load held near the top of the working band for a while and then began the descent it always began, and there was no flush at the end of it, because a flush is a return and there was no longer anywhere for anything to be returned to.

The folding chair was where she had put it, in front of the fourth run, close enough to reach the panel.

Upstairs, in a cleared office with the lamp off, a folder sat squared to the edge of a desk.

The building had one fewer person in it.
