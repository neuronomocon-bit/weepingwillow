Chapter 14 — Open Jobs

The three readings on Caleb Ward were on the table in front of her in order, and she had been looking at them since half past six.

The first was from January, four days after the thing on the access road. She had run the assessment properly: sleep unchanged, appetite unchanged, memory intact and retrievable on demand, recall of the event itself complete and flat. One function reduced and the whole of the rest of him still running. She had scored it band two off the definition on the card, and the notes ran to three pages because she had written down everything, including the things that turned out not to matter.

The second was the third of March, in his kitchen, and it was the same assessment and it came back the same. She had noted a delay on two answers and had not been able to defend a change on either.

The third was the north side on Day 45, and it was four lines long, because four lines was all there had been.

Nine weeks from the first to the last. Eight of those weeks were flat and the last one was not, and in it the man had gone from a specific removal to everything at once with the body still running, and then the body had stopped.

She had spent Tuesday and Wednesday looking for a fourth. There was not one. The check that would have made it had been scheduled for Day 42 and had not happened, and she had written that down already and got nothing out of it. So she stopped looking for the reading she did not have and worked with the three she did.

She wrote the sequence out to see whether it survived being written down: *a partial result, a gap, and then the same subject completed.* It survived.

The gap was where the argument lived. Something had been done to Caleb Ward in January and had produced band two, and band two was not what the thing had been trying to produce, or it would have stopped. It had not stopped. It had gone away, and it had come back, and the second time it had finished.

She checked the shape of that against everything she had. The intervals on the curve went up in steps with a flat stretch in the middle and then resumed at the band they had stopped at. She had read that flat stretch as a pause, and had said so out loud to Rourke, and Rourke had not corrected her because there was nothing to correct it with.

The flat stretch was an interval, and what it lay between was a first visit and a second one.

She sat with that for the length of time she gave things now, long enough to be sure the second reading of it matched the first.

***

Then she ran the population, because the derivation was worth nothing until she knew how many people it applied to.

Rourke had four hundred and sixteen. Twelve of them were dead and the other four hundred and four were not, and every one of the four hundred and four had been assessed once and filed at whatever band they had presented at. Rourke’s set had never shown Iris anything above four. That had bothered her in March and she had put it down to a clinical population being a filtered one, and it was still true that a clinical population is filtered. Four was a stage.

Her own cases were smaller and better. Joel, in February, case ref K-01, a specific removal and the rest of him working and his wife four months into a word she could not find. Lila Mercer, in January, on the second floor of a building two blocks off the main road, complete and polite and entirely correct. Caleb, three times. The two out of the outer zone.

She put them in a column and scored each one for what remained rather than for what had gone, because that was the number that mattered now, and every one of them came back with a large amount of remainder.

Then she added herself to the column.

January. Band two. Three increases below the top of the range, in a set she was not otherwise part of. A specific removal and a very large amount of remainder, still running, still working, working better than it had.

She could not total it cleanly. Rourke’s summaries ran to four lines with the identifying material stripped out, so there was no way to tell whether Joel was inside the four hundred and sixteen or beside it, and the same was true of Lila and of the two out of the outer zone. What she could defend was four hundred and four of Rourke’s still alive, and herself, and a margin of four she could not resolve in either direction.

She wrote *409* and then put a mark against it, because a figure she could not deduplicate was a figure that went up with a mark against it.

The mark did not change what the number was for. Four hundred people in this city had been visited once and left in a condition that was not the condition the thing was trying to produce, and not one of them was finished. It took her under a minute to get there and it did not go anywhere afterward. She read it back twice and could not find the error.

***

Then she went to make the list and found out that she did not have one.

Four hundred and nine people, and five names. Joel. Lila Mercer. The two out of the outer zone. Herself. Everything else was four lines with the identifying material stripped out, because that is what you do with a clinical record leaving the service, and Rourke had done it correctly every time.

The traffic had only ever run in one direction. Iris put a name in and Rourke told her whether it was in the set. She had done that nine times in February and Rourke had confirmed six of them and had never once sent a name the other way, and there had never been a version of the arrangement in which she would.

She called her anyway, because not calling would have been a decision made without the answer in it.

Rourke let her get all the way to the end of it.

“No,” she said.

“I know.”

“Then I’ll give you the part you didn’t call for.” There was a chair moving at the other end, and a door. “If I could hand you four hundred names tomorrow morning, I would not know what to tell you to say to them.”

“I have the thing to say.”

Rourke was quiet for a moment.

“Then go and say it to one of the five,” she said, “and call me back and tell me how it went.”

***

She wrote the options on cards because writing them on cards was how she found out whether she believed them.

Leave. She could be out of the county by evening and out of the state by the following one, and she had money for about five months. The card said *no established range* on the second line, because nothing in the record put an outer limit on where the thing could go, and the eighteen months of small takings before the winter had covered four miles in one direction and nine in another with no pattern that respected a boundary. Leaving assumed a distance. She did not have one.

Refuse. Caleb had answered every question correctly for forty-one minutes while it happened and had not objected once, and Lila had described her own day in order with nothing missing from it, and Joel had gone to work. There was no record anywhere of anyone objecting. She wrote *no instance of refusal on file* and then, underneath, because it was the more accurate sentence, *no mechanism identified by which refusal would operate*.

Resist. She had watched a man’s pulse come down from forty-four to thirty-one in a kitchen chair with no contact and no mark on him, and there had been nothing in that room she could have put a hand on.

Hide. The card for that one said *not tested* and then said *the first visit found me at home*, and that closed it.

She put the four cards in a row above the doorframe, under the readings that had not moved, and stood back and looked at the row, and what she noticed was that she had built the whole assessment in about nine minutes without once losing the thread of it.

There was a version of this where the fear would have made her stupid. She could describe that version accurately. She had been that person in the second week of January and she could remember the shape of the work she had done then. It was worse work, done faster, with more of it wrong.

She had the better process now and she knew exactly what it had cost and she used it anyway, because it was the process she had.

***

The index had come up on Day 44 and she had been reading it in the order it wanted to be read. That order was slow and correct and not the one anyone would have chosen.

The administrative material was the last of it and the least interesting, and she went through it that afternoon at the table with the fan on as she went through everything, one screen at a time with a hand on the trackpad and nothing in her that wanted to skip ahead.

The form was four fields long.

An asset, carried on the schedule under a code and no description, had been reclassified. It had been a transferable asset, which is the category for a thing that goes on a pallet and gets a destination and a date and a tick in the third column. It became fixed site infrastructure, which is the category for a thing that is part of the building.

The date on the reclassification was three weeks before the seal.

Iris read the four fields and then read the sentence that followed from them. It was not written anywhere and it did not need to be. A transferable asset leaves. Fixed site infrastructure stays. The building had been sealed and not demolished, and the difference between those two words was a wall and a keypad and a service door with a mechanical lock, and everything on the other side of it had been left where it was, on purpose, by someone who had filled in a form correctly.

She checked the field for the signature.

The slice the signature sat in was one of the degraded ones. She had known that before she looked, in the way you know which drawer is the one that sticks, and she looked anyway and it was as bad as she had thought and she did not spend time on it.

So she did not have a name. She had the form, and she had a date three weeks ahead of a seal, and she had the fact that the reclassification was correct. No one had falsified anything. The asset genuinely was not going to be moved, and someone had made the paperwork say the true thing, and the true thing was that it was staying.

She wrote *knew* on a card and put it up with the others.

***

Lila Mercer’s building was where it had been.

The gravel strip was the same and the shrubs had been cut again since January and left in the same even lines. The security panel by the front door still worked. Iris pressed the call button and gave her name and said that they had spoken before. The voice that came back through the panel was clean and unhurried. It said yes, and the door unlocked.

The hallway lights were all on. The unit numbers were printed on the doors in the same place on each one.

Lila opened the door before the second knock. Mid-thirties, hair pulled back, clean clothes, no fatigue in her face. She stepped aside without being asked.

The apartment was in order. Shoes together by the door, surfaces clear, a cup near the edge of the small table by the window with the light coming through it evenly. Nothing had been tidied for a visitor. It had simply never stopped being where it went.

“Would you like something to drink,” Lila said.

“No. Thank you.”

They sat where they had sat in January.

Iris had decided in the van what she was going to say and had decided to say it plainly, because everything she knew about this woman said that plainly was the only register that would reach her, and because she had nothing else. She put both hands flat on the table.

“I have information that concerns you and I am going to give you all of it,” she said. “In January something came to you and took something. You know that part. What I have found since is that it does not do that once. It comes back to the same person and completes what it started. I have one case I watched from the first visit to the end of it, and eleven more that fit and that I cannot prove.”

“All right,” Lila said.

“It will not hurt. Nothing about it hurts. Caleb Ward answered questions for forty-one minutes while it happened to him and every answer was correct.” She kept her voice level, because the alternative was performing an alarm she did not have and Lila would have accepted the performance without noticing it. “You will not object to it. That is not a failure on your part. Nobody has objected to it. That is the thing I am telling you.”

Lila listened the whole way through. She did not interrupt and she did not look away and she did not do any of the things a person does with their face while they wait for a turn to speak.

“That is useful information,” she said.

“Yes.”

“Thank you for bringing it.”

“You understand what I have told you.”

“Yes,” Lila said. “Something took something in January. It returns and completes. It does not hurt and I will not object.” She said it back in order and nothing was missing from it. “Is there a date.”

Iris looked at her.

“No,” she said. “The intervals in the record are between four and eleven weeks and they are not regular and I cannot narrow them.”

Lila took that in.

“Then I will continue as normal,” she said. “I respond when required.”

The light through the window had not moved much. The cup was where it had been. Iris ran the check on herself out of habit, as she would check a drive before writing to it, and found the place where the response should have started.

In January, in this room, in this chair, she had felt it come up her arms and into her shoulders and had put her palms flat on her legs to hold still through it. She had gone down to the van afterward and sat with her hands on the wheel until it let go. She had not written anything down that day because she had not needed to.

She put her attention on the same place now and waited the length of time it takes.

Nothing came up.

She noted the location and the duration and the absence, because those were the three things she recorded.

Then she sat in the chair opposite a woman she had come to warn and understood two things with complete clarity and no accompanying sensation whatsoever. She had delivered an accurate warning to someone who could not use it. And she had built that warning out of a case series she was inside.

Lila waited. She waited without impatience and without expectation. She was present in January and she was present now, in the sense that a room is present.

“Is there anything else you need,” Lila said.

“No.”

“I can provide more information if necessary.”

“I’ll let you know.”

At the door Lila stood in the frame with the hallway light behind her, exactly as far into it as she had stood before, and said that she hoped Iris had a good day. The words were correct and the tone was correct and there was no delay in either.

Iris went down the stairs and out through the gravel to the van and sat with the keys in her hand.

She had told her. Every word of it had been true and in the right order, and she had not left anything out to make it easier.

The woman upstairs had received all of it and filed it. She would prepare food now, and go for a walk, and return.

There was no version of that conversation that worked. She went back through it looking for the one she should have had instead, and could not construct it, because the failure was not in the delivery.

She started the van.

***

At home she took Lena’s number up on the phone and looked at it for a while, and then put the phone face-down on the table and went to the wall.

She wrote *409, marked. None finished.* on one card and put it in the row.

The blank card from Monday was still on the table where she had left it, the one that was supposed to have carried the third check on Caleb. She had not been able to write on it because the check no longer had a subject. She picked it up now and turned it over and used the other side, and what she wrote on it was *first visit January. Second visit not yet.*

She read it back, and it was correct, and she put it above the doorframe with the rest.
