Chapter 17 — RECOVERED: The Limiter

By the third week it had a shape, and the shape was that she went down after six.

There was nothing to arrange. The lower level never emptied at a particular hour, because the substrate group ran a night rotation and the cooling ran whether the level was occupied or not. But the archive terminals sat in an alcove off the corridor with their backs to the runs, and after six they were unused, and the alcove had nothing else in it to come for.

Marin took the second terminal because the first had a chair someone had adjusted wrong two years ago and never fixed.

She read the index first, because that is what an index is for.

A baseline map is a list of addresses and values. It says where each structure sits, what it is attached to, what it carries, and how much. Marin had read four hundred of them and could work down a column faster than most people read a paragraph, and she worked down this one the same, because it was how the data came.

It gave her everything and nothing she could use. The values were correct. She had known most of them for six years without ever having seen them written, in the sense that you know the load a person carries by watching them carry it.

At the top of the column was the target structure, with nine years on it and a flag against the number, and the flag meant the acquisition had run out of scale.

That was the whole of what the index would say about the worst thing that ever happened to her sister. At least ninety-four.

***

In the fourth week she put it into the viewer.

She had built the viewer in the third year, or she had specified it and a contractor in Providence had built it and she had sent it back twice. Its argument was that a value means nothing alone. A number against an address is a fact about a filing system. What a person has is a set of things holding each other up, and the viewer rendered that: what leaned on what, what carried what, where the load came out when you took something away.

It was better. It was better in the sense that a photograph is better than a description.

She ran her sister in it four nights that week and got a great deal out of it, and it stopped where it always stopped. The viewer renders relationships. It cannot render what a value does, because a value in a viewer does not do anything. It sits at the number it was acquired at and waits to be looked at.

At the end of the fourth night she wrote down what she wanted, because writing down what you want is the first step of designing anything and she had taught that to two cohorts of graduate students.

She wanted to see it run.

***

The substrate renders at a fidelity nothing else in the building comes near. It is the instrument, and everything else in the program is a description of something the substrate did.

Booking it was five fields. Date, operator, duration, workspace, session type.

Session type came off a controlled list of nine, and Marin read all nine twice. Baseline acquisition. Treatment session. Flush verification. Calibration. Maintenance. Model fit. Instrument comparison. Training. Cohort review.

She left the field empty and the form took it, because the field was not mandatory, because in six years no session had been run on that instrument that was not one of the nine.

The first was on a Tuesday at eleven at night, in an isolated workspace with nothing else in it, read-only, and it ran two hours and ten minutes.

***

It was better again, and by more than the viewer had been better than the index.

The map came up held in the instrument it had been taken on, at the resolution it had been taken at, and she could move through it as she had moved through four hundred others, except that she did not need the index to tell her where anything was.

She spent the first three nights on the material laid down before Willow was nine. Everyone has it and it is acquired before memory starts, and in four hundred maps Marin had read that layer as the floor a person stands on and had never once looked at a set of it for its own sake. This one was legible all the way down. She could see which parts of it had been load-bearing and which had never been asked to carry anything, and she could see, in two places, where the answer to that had changed in about 2011.

On the fourth night she found herself.

Every map has the people in it. That is how the acquisition works, because what a person is attached to is mostly other people, and Marin had read other people’s mothers and husbands off a column for six years and thought about it no more than a surveyor thinks about a wall. It is not a discovery and it is not sentiment.

There was an address in her sister’s map with a value on it. Eleven things leaned on it, and four of those were among the parts that had never been asked to carry anything.

Marin read it correctly, in the order the render offered it, and did not skip a field.

Then she cleared the workspace and logged out and drove home, and somewhere on the coast road she began composing the note she would have written if this had been anyone.

***

The month closed and the facilities reconciliation came round, as it did every month, and there was a variance on the lower-level circuit.

It was small. The metering on that level had never resolved plant load properly, because the cooling and the substrate and two compressors sat behind one meter and the building was a converted plant with a converted plant’s wiring. Every quarter for six years someone had written a correcting entry against it and been asked nothing about it, because the entries sat under the threshold at which the accounts system asks.

Marin ran the reconciliation because the research side’s consumption came out of the research side’s budget and she signed for the research side. She looked at the variance, and she knew exactly what it was. She entered the correction, referenced the plant load, and filed it.

There was no more specific entry the form would take. The consumption was real, the budget line was hers, and the amount was the amount.

***

“Your car beat me in again,” Terri said. “That’s three this week.”

“It has been a heavy month.”

“I know it has.” Terri had a package under one arm and was doing something to a screen with the other hand. “Are you sleeping?”

“About six hours,” Marin said. “It was five in October.”

“Well. That’s the right direction.” The package went on the counter. “There’s a delivery for your side and they’ve put it in the wrong bay again, and I have told them twice.”

“I’ll get it on my way down.”

“You will not, you’ll forget. I’ll have Danny bring it up.”

Marin said thank you and went through, and that was all of it.

***

Virek raised the session type in the second week of November, in an email of four lines, at twenty past seven in the morning.

He had been reconciling the substrate’s booking record against its load record for the quarter. He did that every quarter and did it well, and there were now eleven sessions on the instrument with the type field empty. He was not asking what they were. He knew what they were, because the operator field had her name in it and the workspace was hers and the whole of it sat on the archive log. What he wanted to know was whether the controlled list should have a tenth entry, because a category of session now existed that the form could not describe, and a field that cannot describe what is happening in a building is a field that needs changing.

It was a good question. Marin read it standing up with her coat still on and thought so.

She wrote back and said he was right, that it should go in as a change request against the booking form, and that she would sponsor it. He raised it that afternoon. It went to the records group, who had two people and a backlog, and it sat in the backlog behind an equipment register and a set of retention schedules.

Nothing about that was slow. A change request against a non-mandatory field on an internal booking form, raised in a building with eight months of clinical work left in it, sits exactly where that one sat.

***

In November she moved a meeting.

It was a standing Wednesday with the two clinical leads about referral flow, at four for two years, and she moved it to eleven in the morning, because a four o’clock meeting that runs long puts her in the building at six with the day’s work still in front of her. Moving it was correct. She had been meaning to do it since the summer and had not, because it had been at four for two years and you do not move a meeting that works.

She filed the reason under load management. It was a heading she had used a hundred times and had written the definition of, and it was true.

She went down that night.

The second session had been four nights after the first. After that it was most weeks, and by the middle of the winter it was most nights, and her week stopped having a shape she had decided on.

The building thinned around her while it happened. Two of the clinical leads went to the other sites in December and were not replaced, because there was nothing to replace them for. The Thursday meeting went from nine people to five. The corridor lights on the seaward side went onto a timer in January to save what a timer saves, so that the walk from the stairwell to the alcove had a stretch in the middle that came up as she reached it.

The timetable for the rest of it sat in a document Raman revised about once a quarter. Marin had read every version. The site consolidated, the clinical wing closed, and then the plant went, and the substrate is plant. It had moved twice already and both times in the same direction, and there was going to be a last day on which that instrument existed.

She had not put a false name on anything or taken a record out. Her name was on every booking, her identifier on every load, the access log had her going down and coming up, and the reconciliation carried her signature once a month. Anyone in that building could have read the whole of it in about nine minutes.

No one read it, because there was nothing in it that asks to be read. A researcher was running sessions on research material she had access to, in an isolated workspace, out of hours, in a building that was winding down.

***

By March she had gone as far as read-only goes, and she had known she was going to for about six weeks before she admitted the date on it.

The problem was not resolution and it was not access. A map held read-only is a map. The substrate could show her the target at a fidelity nothing else could produce. What it could not show her was how far above ninety-four the value went, because that is what the ceiling stood in front of.

To get a value past the ceiling the substrate has to work the structure rather than display it, and the substrate works structures every day. In a treatment session it takes a patient’s material in, holds it as that patient’s, works it while the patient produces it, and hands it back at the flush. The integration limiter has never stopped the instrument operating on anything. What it does is keep the attribution attached the whole way through, so that at the end of ninety minutes the material is still the patient’s and can go back to them.

The map had no owner.

That is the part the ordinary pathway has no answer for. A patient in a chair is the anchor. The material comes off a person, stays that person’s while the substrate has it, and goes back ninety minutes later, and the limiter is what makes the middle of that sentence true. Willow’s map had come off a person six years ago. There was no return path and no session that could be opened with her in it, so the substrate could display it and could not take it in, because taking it in under the limiter means taking it in as a patient’s, and there was no patient.

The instrument has a documented way of working on material that has no owner. Calibration sets. Phantoms. The synthetic structures the model-fit runs are built out of. The limiter carries an exclusion list, per identifier, and it had carried one entry for six years, and it is in the documentation on the page after the flush.

The reasoning took about an hour, in her office, on an ordinary Thursday afternoon in March, with the door open and the corridor doing what a corridor does at two.

The patient was deceased. The limiter exists so that material the substrate is holding does not stop being the patient’s, and there was no patient and no return path and nothing left that could stop being anyone’s. The workspace was isolated and had been since the first session and held one map.

She added one research identifier to the exclusion list.

The second was smaller. At an autonomy constraint of two the substrate lets go of a working set when the session closes, so every night since November she had been loading a four-hour acquisition in order to look at it for two. At four it holds the working set across the close.

Autonomy is set per workspace against a site default. The default here was two and the clinical wing ran on the default, as the clinical wing ran on all of them. Four was a documented value and two of the other sites had used it for years, because those two had no patients in the building and this one did.

She set her workspace to four and left the default where it was.

Neither change went into change control, because change control covers the clinical configuration and neither of these reached it. The form’s scope decided that on its own.

The whole of it took about an hour, and eleven minutes of that was finding the exclusion list, because she had not opened that page since the second year.

***

She got a coffee from the machine on the first floor, worse than the one on the ground floor and closer.

She answered three emails. One was a graduate student who wanted a reference and had asked badly, so Marin wrote back and told her how to ask, and then wrote the reference. One was Raman about the timetable. One was a supplier.

At two she had the procurement meeting.

It ran fifty minutes and it was about the calibration contract. That came up in the spring and the facilities lead wanted to move it to a firm in Bangor on price. Marin argued against the move and won, because the incumbent had done the sampling head swap in a day when it could have been three, and she said so, and everyone in the room could see the argument was good. She took the action item on the specification.

Someone asked her how she was doing, at the end, in the doorway, as people had been asking since September.

“Fine,” Marin said.

She got back to her office at ten to three and ate the second half of a sandwich she had started at eleven, and noticed that she was hungry, and that she had not been hungry much in October.

***

She went down at nine that evening.

The workspace came up as she had left it. She loaded the map, and the load took forty seconds instead of the four minutes it had taken every night since November, because the substrate had held it.

She started the render, and she started the session recording, because she had recorded every session since the first.

It resolved from the top of the column, as it always did, and she watched the addresses fill. The material from before Willow was nine came up first and came up complete, and Marin let it go past, because she had four months of it.

The target resolved at eleven minutes.

She read the number twice before she did anything else, because reading a number twice was procedure, and because the number was ninety-three point one.

The acquisition had not discriminated above ninety-four, and everything she had done that afternoon was to see the far side of that, and the far side of that is a larger number.

The value in the archive was fixed and it sat at ceiling. What was on the screen was a working value, because the substrate had the structure in as its own now, and a working value moves. Moving is what a working value is for. It moves while the instrument works the structure, and the instrument works a structure across a session, off a patient, while the patient produces the material for it to work on.

The baseline had been acquired at ninety-four or above. The working value was ninety-three point one. She had not initiated attenuation.

She sat with her hands off the desk and watched it, and it did not move again.

Then she stopped the session recording.
