Chapter 9 — Lena

The week-four outcome measures for MN-R-0338 ran to a single page, and Iris had been looking at the last four lines of it for eleven minutes.

Everything above them was numbers. Retrieval intact, sequence intact, affective load reduced against baseline, patient-reported distress reduced against baseline, no reported change to declarative content. The page was built to be scanned by someone with nine other pages to get through. Then, at the bottom, a free-text field, and someone had actually filled it in.

*Patient reports target structure fully accessible with no experienced affective engagement. “It’s still there. I just can’t find the handle on it anymore.” Patient volunteers this as a positive outcome.*

It was a good record. Whoever wrote it had put the patient’s own words in rather than translating them, and had not editorialized around them. The quotation marks were doing real work: the clinician was saying that this was said, in this order, and that they were not going to improve it.

So the program removed the charge on a memory and left the memory. That was consistent with everything else in the archive. In four hundred and eleven records it was the second time a patient had described the result instead of a clinician summarizing it, and the first time one of them had sounded pleased.

Iris wrote the identifier and the phrase on a card and put it on the table rather than the wall, because it belonged to the case and not to her.

Then she checked, because the procedure was to check. The place where the response should have started was smooth, as it had been in the café in January and every time since. She had stopped expecting a result in the second week. She went on taking it daily, for the reason she had written on a card in February and had not needed to write again.

She had planned the evening around the substrate logs. Three hours, maybe four, and Kade at nine, and a decision by Friday about whether the power draw was worth putting in front of Rourke. She had eaten at six because the list said to.

At twenty past seven the buzzer went, and she was not expecting anyone.

***

Lena had two paper bags and her coat still on and a car parked badly in the lot, and she came up the stairs talking, which meant she had decided the first thirty seconds before she arrived.

“Don’t say anything about the parking, I’m going to move it. There’s a whole thing where the guy in the blue place shouts. Is your buzzer broken? It made a noise like an animal.”

“It’s always done that.”

“It didn’t used to.” Lena got past her into the apartment without either of them treating it as a decision, and put the bags on the counter, and started taking things out. “I’ve brought too much. I know I’ve brought too much. Don’t start.”

Iris had not started. She recognized the move. Lena announced the criticism first so that it could not be made, and she had been doing it since she was about fifteen, and it worked because there was no good answer to it.

“I parked next to your van,” Lena said. “You kept the photo on the dash.”

The photograph had been on the dashboard since January. Iris had knocked it loose out of the cup holder on the drive back from the outer zone and had set it on the dash at a red light. It had stayed there because nothing in the following weeks had required her to move it. She had not decided to keep it there, in the same way that she had not decided to put it there.

Lena had stopped unpacking and was waiting, with a container in her hand, and her face had the look of a person who has already been given the answer she wants and is going to enjoy hearing it anyway.

“It’s a good picture,” Iris said.

“It’s a terrible picture. Mom took it and she cut off the whole left side.” Lena went back to the bags, pleased. “You look like you’re being made to stand there.”

“I was.”

Lena laughed at that, a short one, and it was correct that she laughed, and Iris had known the line would work when she selected it. In the other version of the exchange she said that the photograph had been on the dash for eleven weeks because she had never once had a reason to pick it up. That version was true and available and she did not use it. Nothing would have been served. Lena had come up the stairs with a thing she wanted to be true, and the cost of leaving it alone was nothing, and the cost of correcting it was the rest of the evening.

Then Lena turned around with the plates and saw the wall.

***

Lena did not say anything for a moment, long enough that Iris marked the duration.

The cards ran along the doorframe at eye height and had gone around the corner onto the short return of wall beside it, because nineteen days had become forty-one and the frame had run out. From where Lena stood they read as a strip of index cards in her sister’s handwriting, evenly spaced, some of them dated twice.

“Okay,” Lena said. “What’s this?”

“Notes.”

“Iris.”

“They’re measurements. It’s a log.” Iris took the plates from her, because Lena was still holding them and had forgotten she was holding them. “When something takes longer than it should, I write down how long.”

Lena went and stood in front of the doorframe with her coat still on.

“*Retrieval, four seconds, site address,*” she read. “*Sequence loss, cold coffee, approximately eleven minutes.* What does that mean, sequence loss?”

“I had made coffee and I did not remember making it. Eleven minutes was the gap.”

“And this one. *Wrote note, cannot account for it.*”

“I wrote myself a note in the evening and had no memory of writing it in the morning. The note was correct. The reasoning behind it hadn’t stayed.”

Lena moved along the row. She read four more of them out loud, and got one of the dates wrong, and did not seem to notice that she had, and Iris let it go for the same reason she had let the photograph go.

“You’ve been doing this for six weeks.”

“Forty-one days.”

“Right.” Lena was quiet for a second. Then, in a completely different voice, bright and interested and entirely for her own benefit: “This is fascinating, though. Genuinely. Because you’re *keeping data on yourself.* Which means you know.”

There it was. Iris let the sentence sit where Lena had put it.

It was the same maneuver as the parking and the too much food, run at full size. Lena had walked into a small apartment, found her sister’s disintegration pinned to the doorframe, and converted it inside about four seconds into something she could be enthusiastic about. The alternative was standing in a doorway being afraid in front of a person who could not help her with it. None of the enthusiasm was fake. It was scaffolding, and it was going to hold all evening.

It was also extremely irritating.

Iris noted the assessment and did not act on it, because she could not locate anything underneath it that would have made acting on it necessary.

“I know,” she said. “That’s why the log.”

“Right.” Lena took her coat off at last and put it over the back of the chair. She looked at the wall one more time and then turned her back on it. “Okay. Eat this before it’s cold.”

***

The food was from the fried fish place down on the water, and there was enough of it for four people, and Lena talked while they ate.

She talked about the clinic and the new scheduling system, sold to them on the basis that it would save an hour a day and so far costing about that. She talked about a patient who had been coming twice a week for nine months and had started bringing pastries for the front desk. Then she talked about the position opening in the spring that she was not going to apply for, and then, at length and mostly by way of a person named Dana, about why she was not going to apply for it.

Iris ate and asked the right things at the right intervals.

This was the part she had gotten good at. Being listened to has a rhythm and the rhythm is measurable: an acknowledgment every third or fourth beat, a question at the end of a movement rather than inside it, and an occasional detail retrieved from a previous conversation to demonstrate continuity. She had all of Lena’s previous conversations. Retrieval was intact and had never once failed her on Lena. What she did now was run the account forward, notice where the old version of herself would have interrupted, and interrupt.

The old version had interrupted constantly. That was the hardest part to reproduce, because the interruptions had not been strategic, and a strategic interruption placed correctly still reads as an interruption to the person being interrupted.

“You’re not going to apply for it,” Iris said, over the top of her, “because Dana would have to report to you.”

“Dana would have to report to me.” Lena pointed the fork. “And I would be *fine* with that, and Dana would not be fine with that, and I would then have to spend two years being the person who is fine with it.”

“You’d be terrible at it.”

“I would be so bad at it.”

The exchange completed. Lena went on to the next thing and nothing in her face had changed, and the interruption had cost Iris about a second and a half of preparation.

Later, over the last of it, Lena said, “That was the year the pipes went. I was ten.”

She had gotten there by a route involving the clinic’s heating, and she said it the way people say a thing they are not planning to develop, already moving on to the end of the sentence. Iris registered that Lena had been nine, and that the pipes had gone in the January after their mother’s car was totaled, and did not say so. Correcting it would have taken the conversation somewhere Lena had not chosen to go. The old version of herself would have corrected it, and this was one of the places where reproducing the old version accurately would have made the evening worse.

She filed it instead.

***

At nine thirty Kade sent two lines about the northern site. Iris read them and did not reply, and at ten Lena was still there.

The schedule had been three hours on the substrate logs and Kade at nine, and both of those had gone. Iris ran the cost. The logs would hold until morning. Friday was still Friday. She could have said that she had work, and Lena would have gone, and the going would have cost more than the logs were worth.

She made tea instead. Lena had moved to the end of the couch with her feet up like she lived there, which she did not, and had stopped talking about the clinic some time ago.

“Can I say a thing?” Lena said. “And you don’t have to do anything with it.”

“Yes.”

Lena took a moment to get it in the right order.

“I’ve been talking to you since January,” she said. “And you answer. You always answer. You answer really well, actually. You’re better at it than you were, and I noticed that about three weeks ago and it took me a while to work out why I didn’t like noticing it.” She was looking at the cup and not at Iris. “You don’t ask me things you already know the answer to anymore. You used to ask me things you already knew the answer to, all the time, because you wanted to hear me say it. That’s gone. Everything else is here and that’s gone.”

“I hadn’t noticed that.”

“No, I know.” Lena said it without any edge on it at all. “This isn’t a complaint. I’ve been sitting with it for a month, on my own, and what I’ve got is that I’d rather have this than not have you. That’s it. That’s the whole conclusion. I’ve turned it over about four hundred times and it comes out the same way every time. I’d rather have the person who answers correctly than nothing, and I’m not going to keep testing you to find out how much of you is in there, because that was for me and it wasn’t doing you any good.”

She drank some of the tea.

“So I’m going to stop asking if you’re okay,” she said. “And I’m going to keep coming over.”

Iris sat with it.

Lena had carried a thing for a month, alone, arrived at the correct answer, and then said it out loud to the person it was about, on the assumption that it would be received. Iris could account for every part of that. She could account for what it had cost to reach and what it would cost to hold. She could account for the fact that Lena had chosen the smaller version of her sister deliberately and had made peace with the choice. She could account for what a person would feel on being told it. A person would not have been able to sit through it. A person would have come apart somewhere around *I’d rather have the person who answers correctly*, and would have said something ruinous, and the evening would have gone another way entirely.

There was nothing under her ribs. She had the cup, and the sound the building made at that hour, and her sister on the end of the couch waiting for whatever came next and prepared to accept that it might be nothing.

She could give her something. That was available.

She ran the account and found the thing Lena wanted back, and it took perhaps two seconds, because Lena had put it on the table herself an hour and a half earlier without knowing she had.

“You were nine,” Iris said.

Lena looked up.

“The pipes. You were nine, not ten. It was the January after Mom’s car got totaled, so you’d had the cast on since October and it had another three weeks to go.” Iris kept her voice at the level it had been at all evening. “The landlord took eleven days. We slept in the front room because the heater was the one with the bar that only worked on one side. Mom slept in the chair and told us she preferred the chair. We carried water up from the store in the blue cooler, two trips a day, and she boiled it on the stove for washing, and washing you was a production because of the arm. You had to hang it over the side of the tub and hold still, and you couldn’t hold still.”

“Iris.”

“On the fourth night you said you wanted it to last until your birthday. You said it at the good part of the evening, when everyone was already down, so it came out of nowhere. Mom got up and went into the kitchen, where there was no water and no reason to be. She stayed in there about two minutes. Then she came back and said it wouldn’t last that long, and you said okay.” Iris stopped. “You were happy. You didn’t get much out of that year and you got eleven days of everyone in one room, and you were nine, and you knew exactly what you had.”

Lena did not say anything for a while.

She put her hand over her mouth and left it there. Her eyes filled and she let them, and she made no noise about it, and she did not look away.

“You remember that,” she said eventually.

“I remember all of it.”

That was true. Every part of what she had said was true. She had assembled it in the order that would land, and she had selected it out of nine hundred available evenings because it was the one Lena had reached for and failed to hold at the table. Retrieval was intact. It had been intact all along. The account came back complete and in sequence and cost her nothing to produce, and she had handed it to her sister at the exact moment it would do the most good, and none of it had come from anywhere.

It was the best work she had done in months. She was aware of that while she was doing it and she did it anyway, and she would have called it a lie if there had been anything false in it.

***

Lena stayed.

She did not ask, and she did not announce it, the same instrument as the parking and the too much food, deployed for the last time that evening and with the most skill. At some point after eleven she stopped being someone who was about to leave, and then it was midnight, and then Iris was getting the spare blanket out of the closet by the door without either of them having said anything about it.

“The couch is bad,” Iris said.

“The couch is fine.”

“It isn’t.”

“I’ve slept on worse. I’ve slept on yours.” Lena was already lying down. “Go to bed. Don’t work.”

Iris did not answer that. She turned the lamp off and stood for a moment in the dark part of the room by the door.

Then she went to the table and wrote the card.

*Day 42. Evening, three hours forty. Constructed responses throughout, no failure observed.* She looked at it. She added, on the second line, *L. did not leave.*

The long row along the doorframe was everything that had moved, and it now went around the corner. The short one above the frame was for the readings that had not, and it held five cards, and she had not added to it since the twenty-two minutes in the third week.

She put the card up there, squared it against the others, and went to bed.

Behind her, on the couch, Lena had already gone under, one arm off the edge and the blanket kicked halfway to the floor, in the front room, with the light off.
