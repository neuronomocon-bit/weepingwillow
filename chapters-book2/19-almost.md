Chapter 19 — Almost

Rourke forwarded the call on the Monday morning with four words on it. *This one is different.*

It had come into the number on the Saturday, two days after the meeting, from a woman who had given her name and her address and had answered every question the operator asked her and had then asked whether she could speak to someone about it properly. The summary ran to five lines. The last one said the caller had not reported anything she considered suspicious and had asked twice whether she was wasting their time.

Iris drove out at eleven.

***

The stop was on the outer edge past the yards, one of the ones with a shelter and a bench and a printed timetable in a frame, on a road that took the bus twice an hour and had been resurfaced on one side only.

The woman was about sixty and came out to meet her at the end of the path because she had said she would. She had a cardigan on over a work shirt and she was not frightened, and that was the first fact Iris established and the one that mattered most, and she established it by watching her walk the length of the path.

They sat on the bench.

“I want to say at the start that I don’t think anything happened,” the woman said. “I called because they said to call if you’d seen anything, and I have been going round and round with it for two days, and my daughter said I should just call and stop chewing it.”

“That is what the number is for.”

“Well.” She put her hands in her lap. “There was a woman at this stop on Wednesday.”

“What time.”

“Twenty past two. I know because the two twenty was late and we both stood here for it.”

“Tell me what you did.”

“We talked about the timetable.” The woman pointed at the frame on the post. “It’s wrong. It’s been wrong since they changed the route in the winter and nobody has come out to put a new one in, and I said so, and she agreed with me, and she said something about how they had done the same on the coast road.”

“Had they.”

“They had, actually.” She said it as though it were a point in the woman’s favor. “I checked afterward. That’s a real thing. It happened in February.”

***

Iris took the description properly, in the order she took descriptions, and got a complete one.

Height, taken against the frame of the shelter, because the woman could point at it. A dark coat, wool, below the knee, no pattern. Dark hair, not long. A build the woman called ordinary and then, when asked, said she meant that there was nothing to say about it. No bag. No phone in her hand, and the woman had noticed that and had thought it was nice.

“How old.”

“Forty. Maybe a bit less.” She considered it. “I would say forty and I would not argue with you if you said thirty-five.”

“How long were you talking.”

“Not long. The bus was seven minutes late and we didn’t talk the whole seven.” She worked it out. “Most of a minute. Maybe a minute.”

“And then.”

“And then the bus came and I got on it and she didn’t. Which is normal. People stand at a stop for all sorts of reasons.” The woman looked down the road at the resurfaced side. “I sat down and I looked out and she wasn’t on the sidewalk, and that isn’t anything either, because there’s a path down the side of the depot and everyone uses it.”

Iris wrote all of it and did not prompt her.

“There’s one more part and it’s the reason I’ve been chewing it,” the woman said. “And it’s going to sound like I’m being silly.”

“It will not sound like anything. I am writing it down as you say it.”

She took a moment.

“I’m not sure she was moving the whole time I was looking at her.”

Iris kept her pen where it was.

“Say more about that.”

“That’s the trouble, I can’t.” The woman turned her hands over. “She was talking. Her face was doing what a face does. She looked at me and she looked at the timetable when I pointed at it. And there is a bit in the middle where I have the picture of her and the picture doesn’t have anything moving in it, and I don’t know how long the bit is, and I have tried to time it in my head about forty times and I can’t.” She stopped. “It’s one of those you notice at the time and let go of.”

“Did you let go of it.”

“I did. Completely. I got on the bus and I thought about my shopping.” She looked at Iris. “It came back on Friday when I was doing nothing at all, and it has been there since, and I could not tell you why.”

***

Iris asked the last of it. That was whether the woman had felt anything, and she got the answer she expected.

She had not. She had felt nothing at all, at the time or since, except for the picture. She had thought the woman was pleasant. She had said so twice.

Then Iris ran the assessment she had run on Joel and on Lila and on Caleb, without saying that was what she was doing, because the questions do not sound like questions if you ask them in the right order. Sleep. Appetite. Memory. Whether anything in the last week had felt shorter than it was.

The woman answered all of it, at length, with the enthusiasm of a person who likes being asked. Every function Iris tested came back unreduced, and she had not scored a set like it since January.

So there was nothing to tell her.

Iris worked that out on the bench while the woman was still talking about her daughter. There was no warning to give a person who had not been touched, and no precaution she could name that would have helped, and no version of the sentence that would not have taken an ordinary Wednesday away from her and put something in its place that she could do nothing with. She had come out to the end of the path in a cardigan and would go back up it.

“You have been useful,” Iris said, and it was true, and she left it there.

***

She stayed on the bench after the woman had gone, and ran the check, because she was standing where it had been five days ago and the procedure said to.

It came up.

It did not climb, as it had on the road to Caleb, where she had been able to measure the whole ascent. This arrived already at a level and stayed there. Frontal, steady, and stronger than anything she had recorded except that Friday afternoon in March, and she sat with her hands on her knees and let it run and did not move.

It did not increase.

It held for a length of time she was not able to count afterward, because she had not started counting at the beginning of it. Then it came down, slowly, on its own schedule and not because of anything she had done.

She wrote down what she could defend. Location, frontal. Onset, not observed. Duration, not counted. Intensity, second highest recorded. Termination, gradual and unprompted.

Then she sat on the bench with the card in her hand and knew, with a completeness she had no instrument for, that she had been looked at and set aside.

***

She went back through it on the drive.

There had been a phrase in March that arrived in her hand already finished, and she had made herself test it, and it had survived every test she could build. She had a rule about that. A thing arriving without an account of itself gets tested rather than trusted.

She built the tests on the drive back and none of them would run.

She could not test proximity, because she had no second observer and no instrument but herself. She could not test direction, because the reading had no direction in it and never had. She could not test intent, because there is no measurement of intent anywhere in the world. She could not test presence, because the only corroboration a reading has ever had is a body afterward, and there was no body, and not having one five days later confirmed nothing.

Every route she built ended in the same place. The only evidence for the certainty was the certainty.

She was on the industrial road when she understood that this was the first time in two years she had been sure of something she could not support, and that certainty with nothing under it was not a category she was entitled to use, and that she was sure anyway.

She wrote *certain, untestable* on a card at the light and did not put a category on it, because there was no row on the wall for a card like that.

Then she did the operational part, because there was one and it took eleven seconds.

The four cards from Thursday were still above the doorframe and one of them said *Hide*, and under it, *not tested*, and under that, *the first visit found me at home.* It had now found her at a transit stop on the outer edge in the middle of a Monday, forty minutes from her own door, with a stranger sitting next to her. She had been in that seat under a shelter with her name on nothing.

She wrote the new line on the back of the card, because the front was full.

*Second occasion. Not at home. No contact.*

***

What the account established, and she wrote this out in full at the table because it was the part that mattered:

A woman held a conversation at a bus stop for most of a minute, in daylight, at twenty past two, with a stranger who did not disengage, and the stranger was accurate about a route change on the coast road in February. The witness produced a complete physical description on demand and was not frightened at the time and is not frightened now.

What it did not establish was anything about a face. The witness had not been asked to hold one and had not tried.

Iris held the two of them apart for a while and then wrote the third line under them.

Every account she had before this one came from a person who could not describe what they had seen. Three of them had sat on Rourke’s desk four days ago and had disagreed about everything but a coat. This woman had described her as she would have described a colleague, and had done it five days later, and had got the coat right as well.

Whatever it is, it is better at this than it was in January.

She looked at that sentence for a while. It was the whole finding and it was four words longer than it needed to be, and she left it, because trimming it would have been a way of making it smaller.

***

The quarterly reviews had been in the third batch and she had not needed them until now.

The substrate group ran one every quarter and they were four pages and they were dull, and she had opened perhaps a dozen of them looking for something else. The one she wanted was dated fourteen months after the last logged load, and it was six pages instead of four, and the extra two were an appendix.

The appendix had four findings in it.

The residue was no longer decaying. Not slowly. It had a flat line where the model had a curve, and the man who wrote it had put both traces on the same axis so that a reader could see it without being told.

There were integration events on the substrate log that did not correspond to any session. He listed them, with dates, and there were a great many of them.

The autonomy constraint was sitting at four. The configuration record said two and had said two since the instrument was commissioned, and there was no change entry against it anywhere in six years of change control.

And there were after-hours sessions under one operator identifier, going back further than his log retention would let him see, with no session records attached to any of them.

He did not draw a conclusion. He wrote that the four observations might be related and might not, that he had ruled out the three instrumentation causes he could rule out, and that he was raising it because the fourth item was outside his competence to assess. He named the identifier and did not name the person, and he did not need to, because that identifier was on every consumables order in the building.

The escalation went to the program director and it went the same day.

***

The answer was on the file underneath it, four days later, and it was a page and a half, and it took the findings in the order he had raised them.

The operator identifier belonged to the program lead. The access was hers by right, the research archive was hers or hers and about nine other people’s, and the material in the workspace was one deceased participant’s baseline map held under the research schedule. He had spoken to her. That is the whole of what the file says about that. *Discussed with the operator.* Four words, in a clause, at the end of a sentence about something else.

The workspace was isolated and had been since it was opened. He had asked for that to be verified rather than assumed, and the verification was attached as half a page with a technician’s initials on it. No clinical material in it. No patient session had ever touched it.

The clinical configuration had been checked the same day. The limiter was active against every clinical identifier and the site default for autonomy was still two. He attached that as well.

The integration events were accounted for by the exclusion list, which is a documented mechanism and had been in the instrument since it was commissioned.

Then the paragraph Iris read four times.

He wrote that the change control form did not cover parameters set at workspace scope, that this was a gap in the form rather than a breach of it, and that he was raising a revision request against it. In the meantime the state of that workspace was to be logged in full at every quarterly and reported to him directly if the residual moved, if the clinical configuration changed, or if anything left the workspace.

And the workspace and everything held in it were scheduled for termination when the wind-down completed in the spring.

Every sentence of it was true when it was written, and every one of them had been checked before it was written.

Underneath that, three days later, there was one more line on the ticket, and it was from the man who had raised it.

He closed it himself. The reason field said the operator was authorized, containment verified, clinical configuration unaffected, monitoring in place, termination dated. The field for further action was empty.

That was the whole record.

***

Iris read it twice and then sat back and worked out what the two of them had built between them.

Every assumption in that answer had been checked, and every one of them was true on the day it was written. The workspace was isolated. The clinical side was clean. No one in that building had more right to be in that archive than the operator. The residual was going to be watched at every quarterly. The whole of it was going to be destroyed in the spring.

Five true statements, and the fifth was carrying the other four, and the fifth was the one that stopped being true.

Then she went looking for the rest of it on purpose, and it took twenty minutes.

The revision request against the change control form was in the operational archive, raised that week, sitting in a list with a hundred and forty other items in it. Nine lines below it was a request to add a tenth entry to the controlled list of session types, raised the previous year by the same man.

Neither had been actioned. The records group had two people.

And the whole of what was said in the room where the program lead was finally asked what she was doing on the lower level is one clause in a director’s reply. *Discussed with the operator.* The answer is not in the record, because a conversation is not a document, and the only occasion Iris could find on which Meridian formally looked at any of it produced four words and a date.

***

She had spent eleven years reading systems that people had been ignored by, and they have a shape.

Someone files a report. No one answers. They file it again, and the second one is longer, and by the third there is a line at the bottom in a different tone that says so. Then the submissions stop, and there is no way to tell from the record whether the person left or gave up or was moved onto something else, and it does not matter, because the outcome is the same either way.

She had recovered a great deal of that. It has a rhythm you can see from across a room.

This was not that.

He asked once. He was answered inside a week by a competent person who checked every assumption before he wrote it down, attached the verification, named three conditions that would bring it straight back, and put a date on the end of all of it. Then he closed his own ticket, because that is what you do with a ticket that has been answered, and there was no second submission because there was nothing left to submit.

She went back and read the appendix again to see whether he had been wrong about any of it.

He had not been wrong about any of it.

***

The name was on the front sheet. Simon Virek.

She ran him as she ran anything, starting at the front and working slowly. He was on the staff list from the second year to the seal. He was on the consumables orders, on nineteen substrate logs, on a conference abstract in the third year with two other authors. He had a middle initial and a professional registration number and an address in Lowport that had been sold twice since.

After the seal there was nothing.

She checked the things she could check and wrote down each one as she cleared it, because a search is only worth what you can say about where you looked. No employer she could find. No registration renewal. No new address in the three states she had access to. No professional listing, no license, no publication in six years by anyone with that name and that initial.

He was not dead as far as anything she could reach was concerned, and she wrote that carefully, because she could not reach very much.

A man had walked out of that building six years ago and had not appeared anywhere she could reach since.

***

The last piece of work she did that night was put the answer next to the form.

Raman had written that the workspace and everything held in it were scheduled for termination when the wind-down completed in the spring, and it had been the truth on the day he wrote it, and it was the sentence the other four were standing on.

The form was the one she had found eighteen days ago and had gone back to on the Monday. One asset, carried under a code and no description, moved out of the category for a thing that goes on a pallet and into the category for part of a building. Three weeks before the seal.

The building was sealed months before the spring in that answer.

She put the two documents side by side and did not need to write anything under them, and then wrote it anyway, because the wall was the record and the record did not care what was obvious.

*Scheduled for termination. Reclassified as fixed. Not terminated.*

The signature field on the form was on the corroded slice and was in the condition it had been in the last two times she looked.

Then she checked the date on the closed ticket against the other date she had, because they were four pages apart in the same archive and she had not put them together until now.

The ticket closed about two months before a technician on the night rotation went into the substrate hall for a routine check and came out at twenty-seven minutes instead of eight.

***

She wrote the card last.

*Simon Virek. Substrate group, yr 2 to seal. Raised it once. Nothing after the shutdown. FIND HIM.*

She had never used capitals on a card before and she looked at that for a moment and did not change it.

The long row on the doorframe was measurements and the short row above it was measurements that had not moved, and neither of those was what this was. She put it on the wall by the light switch, on its own, above the list that said *Water. Coffee. Check device. Check the map. Eat.*

Then she stood in front of the wall and read the whole of it from January, because it had been three days since she had, and the reading was the only part of the practice that had never once failed her.

Everything on it was accurate.

There was one card in the middle of the long row, written that afternoon at a red light on the industrial road, that she could not have defended to anyone.
