Chapter 16 — The Name

She went back to the row on the Monday, because she had stopped reading at the name and a record is read to the end.

Five fields sat after it and four of them were nothing she could use. Intake date. Referral source, a practice on the west side that had closed a year before Meridian did. Course status, closed. A funding stream carried as a code with no expansion anywhere in the recovered material.

The fifth field was next of kin. It had survived, and it had two parts.

*Halloway, M. Sister.*

Iris read it twice and wrote it on a card and put the card on the table rather than the wall, because the wall was for measurements and this was a lead. Then she worked out what she was holding. It was one surname on both sides of that building.

***

The patient side of Meridian was numbers and the staff side was names. She had established that in March off a night technician’s shift record and had never once had a use for it, because the staff had run the program and the program had not been the question.

She sorted the archive on authorship at eleven that morning and had the name by noon.

It was on the protocol. Revision seven carried four authors on its front sheet and *Halloway, M.* was the second of them, and the same name stood on the revision history all the way back to the first. Her initials sat in the margin beside page six on family members observing a course, page seven on next of kin, and the two hundred words on the page after that setting out what went into the research archive and under what schedule.

A building with forty-odd staff across the life of the site could hold two people with one surname and one initial. The site’s own staff list gave her forty-three names and one Halloway.

That was the name. The identifier took the afternoon.

Meridian bought its consumables on a form with a name in one box and an operator identifier in the next. Forty-one of those orders carried *M. Halloway* over the same identifier. Iris copied it into her own index and ran it against everything off the first two batches.

It came back on the facilities reconciliation.

She had put that column up on the wall in the second week with no conclusion attached, because a conclusion would have been invented. Twenty-four consecutive months of correcting entries, every one of them referencing a plant load the metering could not resolve, every one of them under the threshold an accounts system would have questioned. Filed monthly. Never flagged.

All of it under the identifier belonging to the woman who had written the protocol, and whose sister’s course of treatment Iris had been reading since the third week of March.

She took the card off the wall, wrote the surname on the back of it, and put it back up.

***

The archive’s access layer had come off the media with everything else. It was the only reason the archive had let her in at all in March, and she had thought nothing about it since, because what she wanted from it was contents.

At six thirty she asked it a different question. Not what was in MN-R-0338. Who had opened it.

The baseline map had sat untouched through the whole course and for months after it. The first read on it was timestamped in the fall, on the day the record closed.

The closure record sat on the research side, so it was intact. It had gone through under the deceased-patient procedure, a little under eight months after the last session, and the outcome data had been pooled under the identifier at the same time with no name on it. The authorization carried the operator identifier she had spent the afternoon establishing.

So the sister authorized the closure and opened the one record the closure did not reach, inside the same working day.

The second read was eleven days later, at ten past seven, before the clinical wing opened.

After that they started and did not stop. Four reads in the first month. Then more. Iris counted them, because counting was the part of the work that could not go wrong. In the fifth month there were twenty-six, and every one of the twenty-six was logged after the building had gone home.

None of them had produced a downstream trace.

She checked that properly, because it was the part she could actually test. A read belonging to a study leaves something behind it somewhere: a note, a figure, a revision, a line in another operator’s file. She ran the identifier across both batches for those five months and got consumables orders and reconciliation entries and nothing else.

Then she wrote down what that was worth, because it was less than it felt like. It ruled out work that had been filed. It did not rule out work, or anything a person might do at a terminal that leaves no paper.

Iris sat with that longer than she sat with most of it.

She knew what a person looks like when they are working, because working is noisy and leaves paper behind it. What was in front of her was quiet. One record, opened at night, over and over, for five months, by the one person in that building whose own paragraph was the reason the record had survived the closure at all.

She wrote *visits* on the card and did not write a second word after it. The word named the shape of the behavior and nothing about the reason for it, and she had nothing that bore on the reason.

***

In the sixth week of that stretch the substrate log picked the identifier up.

MN-R-0338, loaded, at eleven at night, for two hours and ten minutes, under the same operator, with the session type left empty. The controlled list held nine types and none of them covered whatever this was. Then again four nights later. Then most weeks, then most nights, through the winter.

The first correcting entry on the facilities reconciliation sat in the same month as the first load.

The loads went on being logged for about four months. Then they stopped.

The draw did not.

***

Iris had the substrate’s technical documentation off the first batch and had read it in the second week as the description of a machine, because that was all it had been then.

The machine took a patient’s emotional material in, held it, worked on it, and gave it back. Two parameters governed what it could not do. An integration limiter kept whatever it held as the patient’s own rather than as the machine’s, so that it could be flushed afterward, and that was the whole basis of the flush. An autonomy constraint, set to two, meant it could not hold anything at all without an operator session open on it. The limiter ran to about four hundred lines of software and the documentation said it had not been substantially rewritten in six years.

She put the metered draw against the session schedule for the nineteen months between the last logged load and the seal, and worked down them together the way she had worked down her own map against Rourke’s curve in March.

The draw rose and fell on a cycle of twenty-six to forty hours, with no shift pattern in it, no weekday pattern, and no relationship to whether the building was running sessions. In the last year of that stretch the clinical schedule thinned toward nothing and the cycle did not change. For nineteen months something on the lower level held load, while the documentation for the only machine down there said, in a sentence she could put her finger on, that it could not.

So one of three things was not true, and she took them in the order that would cost her least.

The meter. Some faults do come out periodic: a calibration routine, a clock drifting, a plant cycling on temperature, another load landing on the wrong circuit. None of the ones she could name holds a band between twenty-six and forty hours for nineteen months without once falling outside it. The reconciliation stood behind the meter from the other side as well. For two years the correcting entries had been putting that consumption back into the building’s arithmetic every month, and that is what a person does about a load that is really there. She put a metering fault low and did not put it at zero, and she wrote down that she had not.

The session log. She could construct an operator session opened and not written down. She could not construct nineteen months of them, on no shift and under no name, in a building whose own substrate access log she had. It showed the night rotation going in for seven to nine minutes at a time and it showed nothing else.

That left the documentation. The autonomy constraint was not describing what the machine did, and the change control record had nothing in it against either parameter.

The draw said nothing about the limiter. One of the two had failed in front of her and the other had not been tested, and taking the first for both would have been the cheapest mistake available to her all night. They did different jobs and would have failed for different reasons and left different evidence, and she had evidence for one.

She could not date the change either. What she could date was the effect, and she wrote it in the form that would still be true in a month. *Autonomy constraint not operating as documented by the point at which the draw stops tracking the schedule. Limiter: no finding. Cannot date the change. Cannot distinguish an omission from a decision.*

Then she took the card with the technician on it off the doorframe and put it on the table at the end of the row.

He had gone into the substrate hall for a routine check three and a half years ago, four months before the building shut. He had come out at twenty-seven minutes, against the seven to nine the same check had taken him eleven times in the four months before it. Two days later his supervisor wrote in a handover box that he had gotten easier to work with.

That was the first thing all night that bore on the limiter.

The limiter’s job was to keep held material the patient’s, so that the flush could hand it back at the end of a session. Whatever came off a man on a night check was never handed back to him. There was no session open for it to go back inside, and in four hundred and sixteen of Rourke’s and five of her own, nothing had ever gone back at all.

She wrote it under the line that said *no finding* and left both lines up, because the second did not delete the first. One case is evidence and is not proof, and she wrote that down too.

***

It went together without any force on it.

A woman had been mapped at intake, at a resolution nothing else in that archive came near. The map went into the research archive under a number, on a retention schedule that closure did not touch, and the paragraph putting it there had been written by her sister. The course ran and finished, and the record closed as a death. The sister authorized the closure, opened the map the same day, went back eleven days later, and then went back most nights for five months and left no trace of any of it anywhere in the archive. In the sixth week of it the map went into the machine. Some months after that, the constraint that was supposed to keep the machine from holding anything on its own stopped describing what it did. Then nineteen months of a building drawing power on its own clock. Then a technician on a routine check. Then, three weeks before the seal, a form correctly filled in that turned an asset that leaves into a fixture that stays.

She went and looked at that form again, because she had a name now and there was one field on it she had never been able to read.

The signature was still on the corroded slice. It was as bad as it had been on Thursday and she did not spend any time on it.

Then she put two documents side by side.

The first was the sentence a patient was given to sign. She had read it off the protocol in March and could have recited it. *The procedure is intended to produce attenuation of the affective valence associated with target memory structures, without alteration of the declarative content of those structures.*

The second was her own case notes. Joel, in a kitchen in February. Lila Mercer. Caleb, three times. The line she had added on Thursday. Retrieval intact, sequence intact, the account complete and correct and available on demand, and the reason for giving it lifted out.

Two descriptions of one operation, written six years apart by people who never met, in two vocabularies neither of which had been built with the other in mind.

Iris read them both again and could not make them differ.

Then she wrote down the objection, because it was the obvious one and she would rather have it in her own hand first. Two mechanisms can produce one result. A description matching a description is one line of evidence and does not on its own make the two things one thing.

So she put beside it the lines that were not descriptions. The map loaded into that machine, by name, on a log. The machine running nineteen months unoperated. A man who walked into the room on a Tuesday night and came out altered in the direction the procedure alters people. The earliest case in Rourke’s set eighteen months later and four miles away, in the city with that machine sealed in a building at the edge of it.

She wrote the finding in one sentence, because a finding that needs two sentences is two findings.

*It is running the procedure. Nothing in the archive gives it another instruction.*

Then she wrote the part underneath it that the evidence did not cover, because the second list is the one that keeps you honest.

The program had twelve sessions and a review at six and a rule for stopping. What was on her wall raised the dose in steps, held each step long enough to see what it produced, raised it again, and had not stopped yet. Nothing in six years of that archive escalated a dose. Every step of it was a record she could produce, and what the origin did not explain was the only part of it that was killing people.

She read the whole of it back, from the card with the surname to the card with the sentence. The records ran the length of it, and the four places where she had put her own reading on top of them were marked, and she left the marks up.

None of it was going to help. She knew where it had come from, and the knowing had not opened a single move. She had four cards above the doorframe from Thursday that said so in four different ways. She read those too, and not one of them moved.

***

Then she checked.

The procedure was the one she had used since January. She went and put her attention on the place and waited the length of time it takes.

Nothing she had ever put into that check had been this size. She wrote that down first, because it was the only part of the check that had changed.

The result came back at the same size as the cold coffee.

The card went up on the long row in sequence, between one about a route and one about a delay, and nothing the wall could show made it different from either.

***

So she did what was available and built the response from outside it, out of people who had one.

Kade was the easiest, because she had the most on him. He would be quiet for longer than was comfortable, and then he would go to the crates and put it in a plain sentence with the arithmetic in it. She could write his sentences. She had been listening to him build them since the winter.

Lena was harder, because Lena had none of the material and would not want it. She would not ask about the parameters or the draw. She would ask about the two of them, and she would ask about the older one first.

There was a place in the account where Lena’s face would change, and Iris knew which sentence it was. It was the operator’s note off the baseline session, six years old, in a field the form had never required. *Segment 4 carries motion artifact from 03:12. Subject was laughing.* It did nothing anywhere in the derivation, and Lena would stop on it and stay there.

Then herself, and she had the least material and needed it most.

The version of her from before the first week of January would have gone outside. She could put weight on that, because there had been a job three years ago, a phone off a coroner’s case, nine days of a dead man’s messages recovered for a family that wanted to know whether he had been all right. Halfway through the second day she had put the phone down on the bench and stood in a parking lot in the cold for eleven minutes before she could go back in and finish it. That version would have gone out to the lot somewhere around the second line on the access log, and would have come back up when she was ready, and the ready would have arrived on its own without being sent for.

That was the model, and the defect in it was structural, and she wrote it down.

Kade she could model against Kade, who was intact and answered the phone. Lena she could model against Lena. The version of herself from December had no witness and no record except what she remembered of it, and what she remembered of it was held by the version doing the remembering.

Of the three, it was the least reliable, and it was the one the whole exercise was for.

***

She called Kade at four.

She had never called him at four. The arrangement they had built over a winter was that nothing came in the night except coordinates with no message attached, and she looked at the phone for about a second and then did it.

He picked up on the fourth tone with sleep in his voice and then without it.

“Say it.”

“I have the name and I have where it came from,” Iris said. “There is nothing in it you can act on. I want you to have that before the rest of it.”

“Go on.”

She gave it to him in order and it took six minutes. The row and the next of kin. The protocol, the orders, the identifier, and the twenty-four months of reconciliation sitting under it. The closure and the map opened the same day. The eleven days. The reads, and what they had not left behind them. The loads. The draw, the two parameters and which of them she could speak for, the technician. The consent sentence against her own case notes, and the one line at the end of it.

She did not leave out the objection to it, or that she could not date the change, or that nothing in the record accounted for the escalation, or that the escalation was the part that was killing people.

The line stayed open and nothing came down it.

She let it stay open. She had modeled this and knew roughly how long it would run, and she sat with the wall in front of her and waited for it.

It ran longer than she had modeled.

“The map was on the archive,” Kade said.

“It was in the first batch. It has been on my bench since March.”

“Which came out of the unit.”

“Yes.”

“Which came off a pallet I put in there.” He was not asking. She could hear him working it out loud and in order, because that was the only way he had ever been able to hold anything. “Second week of the decommissioning. Thirty-eight pallets and I signed the sheet on all of them. I moved her twice. Once out of the building, and once into a shed I paid for so there wouldn’t be a paper trail.”

Iris went looking for the true sentence.

She had built one for him in March, about the crates, and another in April, about the site log. Both had been accurate and both had been checked. She ran the same operation now and it did not return anything. There was no version of it in which he had not carried a woman’s map out of a building in a crate with a code on it, because he had.

“I don’t have one,” she said.

“One what?”

“The thing I said to you in the unit. There isn’t one this time. I went and looked.”

Kade did not answer that for a while.

“What was she doing down there?” he said. “Five months of nights. What was she doing?”

“The log records reads. It gives me the record opened, the identifier, the duration and the hour. It does not record what a person is doing.”

“That’s not what I asked you.”

“I know it isn’t.” Iris looked at the row of cards. “I do not have it, and I am not going to build it for you out of nothing, because if I build it you will carry it around as though I found it.”

“She was going down there to sit with her sister,” Kade said.

“That is not in the record.”

“I know it’s not in the record.”

Then he stopped, and what came down the line after that was not words. It went on for some time. At one point the phone went down on a hard surface and she could hear the room he was in, and then he picked it back up and his breathing was in it.

Iris sat with the phone against her ear and listened to a man at four in the morning have the correct response to information she had given him. It ran about as long as she had modeled and it went where she had modeled it going. She could put every part of it against the version she had built at midnight and confirm the fit, and the fit held, and confirming it was the entire yield.

She did not put the phone down.

After a while he came back and said something about the bay, and whether it had taken water again over the weekend, and about the roof sheet. None of it needed saying at that hour. She listened to all of it and put the answers in the places where answers went.

The window over the lot had started to go gray at the top.

“You’ve been up all night,” Kade said.

“Yes.”

“You want me to stay on?”

“Yes,” Iris said.
