Chapter 18 — Escalation

The batches had been coming out of Rourke’s records room since the first week of April, and by the Friday Iris had about a third of the cohort on the table in front of her.

It was slower than it sounded. The room was unindexed and eleven months behind and had been filled from the door end backward, so the order the files came out in was the order they had gone in, and that was no order at all. She was building the index herself, one line per patient, name and date and presentation, out of four rows of shelving a woman had been meaning to sort out since the year before last. A hundred and thirty-one names by the Friday. It would be all four hundred and sixteen in about three weeks and she had told no one that, because there was nothing to tell.

She was doing it because it was the only work available to her.

***

The first one that week was a man on Cotter Street, found on the Tuesday, and she got the name off a woman two doors down who had been outside with a bin when the crew came.

The second was on the Wednesday, out past the rail crossing.

Neither of them had an event in it. She had stopped asking whether there had been an event by the third week of March, because there had never once been one, and asking made people think she was implying something about them.

She went to Cotter Street on the Wednesday afternoon anyway and stood on the sidewalk opposite for eleven minutes with her attention loose at the front of her skull, and got nothing.

That was worth what a null is worth. She had no measurement of how long a reading lasted after the fact, so a clean street thirty hours later meant that nothing had been there in thirty hours, or it meant that thirty hours was long enough. She wrote it down in the form that said both. There had been a stretch in January when she would have driven to every address in the city on the chance of it, and she could remember deciding to, and she could not any longer produce the thing that had made her turn the key.

By the Thursday there were four in the paper, in two paragraphs at the bottom of a page, under a heading about a rise in sudden deaths. The paragraphs were accurate. The paper had counted correctly and had gone to the county for a comment and had printed the comment, which said that no connection had been established and that anyone with concerns should contact their own doctor.

Iris read it twice and could not fault a line of it.

***

Kade sent her the names on the Friday evening in one message with nothing else in it, because that was the arrangement, and there were six of them.

He had them because the yard talks. Two of the six had worked the port at some point and one had a brother on the crews, and a man in that business who has been asking careful questions since the winter has a great deal of the city available to him.

He had also written, at the bottom, under the sixth name, *I can get more of these. Say if you want me to.*

She looked at that for longer than she looked at the list.

He had been on this since January and had gone through his own manifests eleven times and had told her in March not to get better at it, and what he had built for himself since was a way of being useful that did not require an explanation from her. It was good work. He had produced six names in a day out of a city that had produced four in a newspaper.

She read down the list.

The fourth name on it was Lila Mercer.

She checked it against the address she had, because checking was the procedure and because she had a rule about not accepting a name that arrived on its own. The address matched. The building was the one with the gravel strip and the shrubs cut in even lines, and the second floor, and the hallway light that was on all the way along.

She replied *Yes. Names and dates only. Nothing about how you got them.* Then she put the list on the table and squared it against the edge and went to get the card from Thursday of the week before.

***

The card said *405 to 409 living partials. Return status unknown.*

She had written it on Day 51 and had been able to defend every part of it. Four hundred and four of Rourke’s still alive, and herself, and a margin of four she could not resolve in either direction, because Joel and Lila and the two out of the outer zone were summaries with the identifying material stripped out and she had no way of telling whether they sat inside Rourke’s four hundred and sixteen or beside it.

She had the index now, or a third of it. She ran the six names against what she had and got five of them, and she checked those five twice by different routes because she was going to have to state it as a fact.

Five of the six were Rourke’s. That took four hundred and four to three hundred and ninety-nine.

The sixth was Lila.

She sat with that a moment, because the arithmetic was not clean. Lila had been one of the four she could not place, and she was placed now. A person who has died has stopped being a living partial whether or not the column was ever worked out, so the margin came down from four to three, and it came down for the worst available reason.

Three of the nine had no names on them at all.

She wrote *400 to 403 living partials* and then sat and looked at it, because it did not hold.

Three people were dead and untested. Any of the three could have been in Rourke’s four hundred and sixteen, and any of the three could have been one of the margin she still could not place, and there was no one to ask. A count of the living with three dead people standing outside it is not a count.

The top could not move, because an unnamed death cannot raise it. The bottom had to come down by three, because the floor of a range is the number you get when everything you cannot check goes the worst way it could go.

She wrote it again.

*397 to 403 living partials. Deduplication unresolved. Nine deaths in eleven days, six named, five confirmed in R’s set, three unnamed and untested against the above.*

She read that back and it held. The range was wider than the one she had written on Thursday and every number in it was smaller, and both of those had happened for the same reason.

She was still inside it. She had put herself in the column in April and had not taken herself out, and the range she had just written down included her, and she had written it without the inclusion registering as anything at all.

Then she wrote a second card, and it took about four seconds, and she did not have to construct it.

*Warned one. No change to outcome.*

She put all of it above the doorframe and went back to the index.

***

The advisory came out on the Tuesday.

It was one page and it was well written. A number of sudden deaths had occurred in the Lowport area over a period of eleven days, in adults among whom no shared clinical history had been identified, and the county was treating a possible environmental factor as the leading line of inquiry. Residents in the affected districts were advised of three ordinary precautions. A number had been set up and would be staffed from eight until eight. Anyone with information about a sudden death in the previous three weeks was asked to call it.

The last paragraph asked people not to speculate about causes ahead of the findings, because speculation without findings tends to reach the people least able to act on it.

Iris read the whole page standing at the table with her coat still on.

Every sentence in it was true, and one of them was true in a way that would have taken a paragraph to explain. No shared clinical history had been identified. There was one. It sat in four hundred and sixteen files in a records room on the other side of the city, under a category a single clinician had invented and could not define, and there was no route by which a county officer looking for a common exposure would have arrived at it in eleven days or in eleven months. An environmental factor was the leading line of inquiry because it was the only line of inquiry that could be run by the people who had been given the job.

She read the last paragraph a second time.

There was one person in that city whose speculation would be better than speculation, and the instruction was addressed more exactly to her than to the district it was written for, and she agreed with it. She had a mechanism, a date, an origin and a name, and no route by which any of it could be said to a county officer without producing a woman who had cut a fence and could not prove where the drives came from. She had been carrying that since the second week of April and it had not changed shape once.

It was the best available explanation for a set of people who could not be told the real one, and it had a process attached to it now, and a process is a thing that keeps going.

***

The meeting was on the Thursday at seven, in the hall behind the library, and about ninety people came.

It was well run. A woman from the county chaired it and started on time and said at the beginning how long it would last and did that. There was a table with four people at it and a screen behind them that worked. The panel did not raise their voices and neither did the room, and the questions were good questions.

A man near the front asked what the three precautions were for if the cause had not been identified. He was told, accurately, that the precautions were the ones that would help against the largest class of candidates, that they cost nothing, and that recommending them was not the same as claiming to know. He said that was fair and sat down.

A woman asked whether the water had been tested. It had, twice, and the results were on the county site, and she was told where.

Rourke read the health section.

She was fourth at the table and she spoke for about six minutes, and Iris had heard her speak twice and had never heard this register. She did not use a contraction in the whole of it. She gave the number of deaths and the period and the age range. She said the deaths did not present a common cause on the certificates and that this was not unusual in a small number over a short period. She said a cluster in time is not by itself evidence of a common origin, and that the work of the next several weeks was to determine whether the cluster was real or was the ordinary behavior of a small number.

She did not overclaim once. She said *we do not know* three times, and each time she said what would be done about not knowing.

Then she said the department had no evidence of transmission between people and no evidence of a contaminant in the supply, and that people should continue with their lives and call the number if they had information.

She was completely persuasive.

Iris sat eleven rows back and watched a woman say, well and carefully and without a false word in it, a set of things she had stopped believing on a Monday afternoon in the first week of April, in her own office, with a folder on the desk she had not opened.

The room believed her. Iris watched about ninety people put something down.

Afterward the chair thanked everyone and said the next meeting would be in three weeks unless there was reason to bring it forward, and people got their coats, and two of them stayed to ask the panel small practical questions about their own streets, and the panel answered them.

***

Rourke’s office at half past nine had the blinds up because the afternoon was over, and the second desk had six stacks on it.

“You were there,” she said.

“Eleven rows back.”

“I saw you.” Rourke did not sit down. She took her coat off and put it over the chair and stood with her hand on the back of it. “I want to give you something and I want to tell you what it is first, because if I hand it over without saying it you’ll work it out in about a minute and I’d rather say it myself.”

“All right.”

“The cluster is real. The exposure model isn’t.”

She turned the middle screen around, as she had done in March with two lines on it, and there were more lines on it now.

“Nine in eleven days against four years of base rate is a real excess and I can defend the arithmetic to anyone who wants it. What I can’t defend is the county’s picture of what made it.” She put a finger on the screen and took it off again. “I ran it four ways. Geography first. If it’s environmental it has a source, and a source has a shape, and these don’t have one. They’re across the whole city and they’re not near anything. Water zones, the two industrial parcels, the fuel line, the old landfill on the east side. Nothing a first-year would let me publish.”

“And the timing.”

“No exposure window fits it. An acute exposure gives you a front. A chronic one gives you a slope. I’ve got a scatter.” She sat down at last, on the edge of the second desk. “Then I ran the only variable I actually have.”

“Prior flattening.”

“Prior flattening.” Rourke said it flatly. “Every death I can put a name to was already in my caseload. Five of the six. The sixth matches the presentation and I can’t confirm her either way, and I’m not counting a resemblance as a hit. Three I can’t name at all. Nothing else I tested comes within a mile of it.”

“And four hundred of them are alive.”

“And four hundred of them are alive, which is the whole of it.” She let that sit. “It isn’t a predictor. Every death I can place was already flattened, and almost none of the flattened are dying, this month, that I can see. What I have got is a finding about where to look. It says nothing whatsoever about who.”

She stopped.

“Do you know what I asked you for in March?”

“A name off your own cohort,” Iris said. “A function that would stop, and roughly when. You would go and see them and not tell me what you did.”

“That’s it. That was the bar, and I set it in about forty seconds in this room, and I have spent six hours on a Sunday finding out why I set it there.” Rourke looked at the screen and not at her. “I have got a better finding than the one I asked you for. I have got an association I would have called impossible in March and I would put my name on it tomorrow. And I have not got a name, a function or a date. That was the entire material the test was made out of.”

“You found the population.”

“I found the population. I can’t find the person.” She said it without any drama on it at all. “I can’t publish it, because the moment I do the next question is the mechanism and I don’t have one and there’s no version of me that gets one. I can’t warn them, because the warning is that they’re in a category I invented and can’t define and there is nothing on the end of it for them to do. There is not one operational instruction that comes out the other side of six hours of work. Nineteen years, and I have never had a finding that was that good and that useless in the same afternoon.”

***

She took three sheets off the second desk and put them where Iris could reach them.

“Call log,” she said. “The number’s been open six days. Most of it is people who are worried, which is what a number is for. Three of them are describing the same thing near three of the addresses and I don’t know what to do with them.”

Iris read all three.

The first was a man who had seen a woman walking on Cotter Street on the Tuesday morning at about the time the crew was called. He put her at forty, tall, dark hair, dark coat. He said she was walking normally and that he had noticed her because she had not looked at the ambulance, and everyone looks at an ambulance.

The second was a woman near the rail crossing on the Wednesday. She put her at mid-twenties. Fair. Not tall. She said the woman had stood outside the building a while and then gone in, and that there had been nothing about her worth calling about at the time, and that she was calling now because of the number.

The third had seen someone on the Saturday out past the yards and could not give an age at all. The summary recorded that the caller had been asked three times and had said each time that she could not, that the face had been perfectly ordinary, and that she had not been able to describe it afterward.

“Three people,” Rourke said. “Three descriptions. They agree about the coat.”

“And nothing else.”

“And nothing else. Which is what you get. I’ve taken hundreds of witness accounts and people are terrible at this and always have been.” She squared the sheets and left them where they were. “That’s what I’d say if anyone asked me. It’s also true.”

“Yes.”

“I notice I said it out loud just now to a room with two people in it.”

***

She got her coat off the back of the chair and did not put it on.

“I’m going to go on saying the other thing,” she said. “In public. For as long as they keep asking me to. I’ll read the statement, I’ll say we don’t know, I’ll say the work is ongoing, and I’ll do it well, because I’m good at it and the alternative is a city that’s been told there’s something it can’t see and can’t avoid and can’t be tested for, and nothing to do about any of it. Panic with no instruction attached to it is just harm with better ventilation.”

She put one arm in the coat.

“Do you think that’s defensible.”

Iris had the answer before the question finished, and she checked it for accuracy the way she checked everything now, and it held.

“Yes,” she said.

Rourke stood with the other arm out.

“You don’t want to think about it.”

“I did think about it. You have not got a warning with an instruction attached to it. I delivered the only version of one that exists, once, two weeks ago, and she took all of it and did nothing differently, and that is one case and I am not building a rule on it.” Iris kept her voice where it had been all evening. “The other side of it does not need a rule. The harm from saying more is immediate and it is certain and it lands on two hundred thousand people who cannot act on it either. You have weighed a certain harm against an unproven benefit and you have got it the right way round.”

“That’s the same answer.”

“It’s the accurate one.”

Rourke got the second arm in, slowly, and took her keys off the desk, and stood there holding them.

Iris ran it again from the beginning, because being asked a question twice is information about the answer. She looked for the part she had got wrong and there was not one. The reasoning was sound and it was hers and she had checked it, and she would have given the same answer to a review board.

Something else had been wanted. She could see the shape of the place it should have gone, and she could not identify what it was, and she had no procedure for finding out, and the woman on the other side of the desk was not going to tell her.

“Go home,” Rourke said. “I’ll send the rest of the room out in batches.”
