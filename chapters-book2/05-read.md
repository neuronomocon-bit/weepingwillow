Chapter 5 — Read

The first batch came home in four crates, and Iris took the table apart to make room for it.

The screens went up on the wall above the window in a row. She cabled them along the frame and taped the run down so she would not catch it with a foot, and the case map moved up there with them. That left her the whole table, four feet by six. She wiped it down and set the crates underneath rather than on top of it. The floor was for material that was not currently being worked on. The table was for one thing at a time, and she had learned that at twenty-six from a man who lost a client’s only copy by putting it down next to a coffee.

Ninety-four cartridges. She had counted them at the unit, counted them again into the van, and then counted them a third time off the inventory in her own doorway. The third count was not caution. It was the new practice: anything she would later have to state as a fact got counted twice by different methods on separate occasions, because her memory of having counted was no longer evidence of the same quality as the count.

She wrote the number down and started the bench.

The rules went up first, on a card above the table where she would see them from the chair. *Image before read. Verify every close. Nothing runs off an original.* None of it was new. It was the standard she had worked to since she was twenty-four. Putting it on the wall changed nothing about the standard and she put it on the wall.

The reader she owned took the format. The second one came off a man in Gorse Point who had four of them in a lock-up and no clear idea why anyone still wanted one. He wrapped it in a towel for the drive back and told her the format had been good and had lost to a cheaper one, and that this was the story of everything. He charged her sixty and threw in a spare belt. Two readers meant two images running while she read a third off the array, and the arithmetic on that was the difference between three weeks and five.

She set the first cartridge at two thirty and it came up in under a minute.

Then the archive wanted to know who she was.

That took the rest of the afternoon. None of it was difficult and all of it was slow. The access layer had been configured by an institution that expected to exist. It wanted a department. The department had been folded into another department twice since the last time it had been signed into, so the first name was refused and the second name was refused, and the third pass went through on a credential Kade had not used for anything in three years and had never had revoked. She watched the handshake complete with her hands off the keys and let the tree finish resolving on its own.

It came up clean. Labels that had gone without an update for the last two years the building was open. Session data nested under a heading for a group that had been renamed out from under it. Underneath that, in the order a system writes things rather than the order a person would, six years of work.

This was the part she liked.

She had never said so out loud, because saying it invited a conversation about why, and every answer she had was strange. A dead system waking up and answering. Three years of no one asking, and then someone, and the machine had no opinion at all about the gap. It did not care that the company was gone. It had been left in the middle of a sentence and it picked the sentence up.

She started the first image and let it run, and the array at her elbow ticked as it wrote.

***

On the second morning a cartridge threw a soft error at ninety-one minutes, and she lost the day to it.

The image had been running clean until then. The error came up in the middle of a session block, the reader retried it four times on its own, and the fourth retry took. A read that succeeds on the fourth attempt is a read that succeeded. It is also a read that failed three times.

She could have taken it. That was the shape of the temptation and she looked straight at it: accept the close, flag the block, carry on, and be four hours further along by the evening. There were three more batches behind this one, and a corroding index behind those, on a clock she could not see.

She re-imaged it instead.

The second pass threw the error at ninety-one minutes. So did the third. An error at a consistent offset is not damage, because damage does not keep an appointment, and she had been telling clients some version of that sentence for eleven years. She moved the cartridge to the second reader, ran it a fourth time, and it came off end to end without a retry.

Then she opened the first reader and found the belt.

It had glazed on one face and slipped under sustained load, a fault that stays invisible until a machine has been working for an hour and a half. She fitted the spare from Gorse Point, ran a known-good cartridge through, and lost the rest of the afternoon to re-imaging everything the first reader had closed since Tuesday, because the fault had been developing and she could not put a date on when it started.

Eleven cartridges. All eleven came back identical to the first pass, and the day had bought her nothing except the knowledge that it had bought her nothing.

That was the job. Hurrying the write is how a block goes bad at hour four and the whole set restarts at hour five. She had done exactly that once, at twenty-four, on a family’s only copy of something, and she had been better at everything then except patience.

***

By the third day she had the material in a shape she could work, and by the fourth she had it in order.

The order was the work. Everything in the research archive carried three things it could be sorted on: a research identifier, a date, and a session type off a controlled list of nine. Nothing carried a name. She had said as much to Kade in the unit, and it was different to see it four hundred and eleven times in a column.

She built her own index as the images closed, because the archive’s had been built for people who already knew what they were looking at. Identifier, date, type, duration, integrity. One line per record. She sorted it on the first two and read down.

Most identifiers carried a short run. Nine or ten records across three or four months: a baseline at the front, a review two thirds of the way along, an outcome measure at the end. The program had been regular and the regularity showed. She could see a course of treatment repeated three hundred times, and she could see where it broke. People who stopped after two. People who came back a year later. One identifier with a single baseline and nothing after it at all.

None of that told her anything. It was a program doing what a program does. There were four hundred and eleven records of it in this batch alone, and three more batches waiting in the unit.

So she went at it from the other end.

Structured fields are uniform by design and they hide everything. The place a person shows up in a record is the free text, because free text is where someone has decided the form is not sufficient. She ran a scan for non-empty operator notes across the whole batch. It took nine minutes and came back with eleven.

Eleven records out of four hundred and eleven where a human being had typed something the schema did not require.

Two were a technician logging a hardware fault in a field meant for something else, the standard move when the field you want does not exist. Nine were on one identifier.

She wrote it on a card and pinned it to the doorframe with the measurements.

*MN-R-0338. Nine operator notes. No other identifier carries more than one.*

***

The card above the light switch said *Caleb. Three weeks.* The three weeks were not up. She had checked the date twice that morning.

She read the nine notes in order on the fifth day, which meant reading the records they hung off, which meant taking MN-R-0338 from the front.

The front was a baseline mapping session. Four hours and ten minutes of acquisition in six segments, three breaks, one operator, one supervising clinician, and a research identifier where a name should have been. The date put it a little over six years back. The file was intact from head to tail.

Iris read the session log first. A log records what happened. Everything else in a file records a conclusion, and the two are not the same document. Segment one had started eleven minutes late. There was a break after two and a longer one after four. The acquisition had run eighteen minutes past its slot to recover the fourth segment, and the extension had been authorized in the same minute it was requested.

Then she read the measures.

The program scored what it called affective valence on the target structure, zero to one hundred, on a scale designed by people who expected to use most of it. The baseline for MN-R-0338 came in at one hundred, with a flag on it. The flag resolved to a line in the instrument documentation: the acquisition did not discriminate above ninety-four, and any value returned at ceiling was to be recorded as at least, never as equal to.

At least ninety-four. The field for years since the index event was three characters wide and had a nine in it.

The rest of the record was a map, and Iris could not read a map. She could read what the file said about one. The file said it was complete, that all six segments had been retained, that the target was well defined and stable across the acquisition, and that the subject had tolerated the session without incident.

Underneath that, in a free-text field the schema did not require, the operator had written four sentences.

*Segment 4 carries motion artifact from 03:12. Subject was laughing. Retained rather than re-acquired: the segment is usable, and she has been in the chair for three hours. Flag for review at six if the artifact affects the comparison, not before.*

Iris read it twice.

Everything in it was correct. The judgment was defensible and the reasoning was stated. The decision fell conservative in the direction that cost the researcher rather than the subject. And it had been written by a person with no procedural reason to write anything at all. Someone had stood at a terminal at the end of a four-hour acquisition and taken the trouble to record why a flawed segment was being kept, in a field that was never going to be read, about a woman who had been laughing three hours into it.

The rest of it assembled without effort. Nine years of something the file did not name and did not need to name, because the number in the three-character field was the whole of it. A woman who had carried it long enough that the instrument could not measure the top of her. An operator who wrote *she* instead of *the subject*, once, and never went back to correct it.

The reconstruction was complete. It was accurate. She was confident of every part of it.

She went and looked at the place where something should have been, because the checking was procedure now, and procedure does not stop being useful when the answer stops changing.

It was smooth.

There had been a job three years ago, a phone off a coroner’s case, a man’s last nine days recovered a message at a time for a family who wanted to know whether he had been all right. He had been. That was the finding and it was a good one. Halfway through the second day she had put the phone down on the bench, gone out to the parking lot, and stood there in the cold with her hands in her pockets for eleven minutes before she could go back in and finish it. She had not timed it. She had simply been outside, and then she had been ready, and the ready had arrived on its own without being sent for.

She worked forward through the rest of the run.

The notes got shorter. The second was two lines about a scheduling change and why it had been made. The third recorded that the subject had asked a question the operator could not answer, and that the answer had been found and sent on to her afterward. By the sixth they had gone almost entirely procedural. The ninth was four words long and dated at the review at six. After that the operator stopped, and the records ran on past her with nothing in that field at all.

Iris read MN-R-0338 for six hours and stopped because the batch ran out.

Before she closed the terminal she checked the clock against her own benchmark for cataloguing a set this size. It stood at twenty-nine hours. She had set it at thirty-one and had never beaten it by more than a morning.

She was eleven hours inside it.

The number went onto a card and up above the doorframe, on the short row, with the four that had not moved.

***

On the sixth day she took the empties back to the unit and brought the second batch out. The bay smelled as it had the first time. A fresh inch of water stood in the builder’s bucket in the corner.

She spent an hour at the back stack before she loaded anything, with her light and a magnifier and both hands off the media.

The rig for the caddies was on her bench at home in pieces. Nine days, she had told Kade, and it would be nine days, because what she was building had to give her one attempt per drive and be right the first time. The platters were corroded at the edges and around the mount. A head that touches a corroded surface takes the surface with it, and there is no second pass at a place you have already scraped. So the enclosure had to hold humidity, the donor heads had to match a caddy model that had been discontinued before the shutdown, and the whole assembly had to be proven on drives that did not matter before a drive that did went anywhere near it.

She had four dead caddies off a scrap dealer for exactly that, and she was going to burn all four.

Nothing in the back stack had improved while she worked on the archive. Nothing in it would. She wrote down the condition of two more caddies, put her light away, and left the stack in the order she had found it, because the order it was in was the last information about how it had been stored.

The second batch was not sessions. It was the facilities side.

Six years of consumption logs off the building, metered by circuit, aggregated monthly, with a reconciliation attached to every month. Iris read three of them before she understood what she had, and then she pulled all seventy-two and put them in a column.

The first forty-eight months were ordinary. Discrepancies in both directions, small, self-canceling across a quarter, the noise any building makes against its own meters.

In the forty-ninth month, a correcting entry. And in every month after it, to the end.

Twenty-four consecutive months, all filed under the same operator identifier, all referencing a plant load the metering did not resolve, all small. None of them flagged. They had been accepted by an accounts system that would accept anything under a threshold, and the threshold had never once been reached, because every entry sat under it by a comfortable margin and none of them ever crept.

She put the raw draw up on the wall screen and looked at it.

It rose and it fell. There was no shift pattern in it and no weekday pattern. The intervals from one rise to the next were not regular. They were also not random, and she sat with that a while, because those two together did not describe anything she had a category for. A thing that runs on a schedule looks like a schedule. A thing that runs on demand looks like noise. This was neither, and it had been neither for two years, and someone had quietly kept the arithmetic tidy around it the entire time.

She wrote it down with no conclusion attached, because a conclusion attached to that would have been invented, and put the card on the doorframe.

Kade called that evening while she was still eating.

“Where are you.”

“Batch two. The archive is coming off exactly the way I said it would.”

“How much of it.”

“Four hundred and eleven session records, and about nine hundred pages of facilities paperwork I have not finished with.” She moved the plate off the inventory. “Every session is in order by date. The dates survived. The dates were the one thing that building was careful about.”

She heard him work out what to ask next.

“Have you got a name on any of it.”

“No. The names are in the back stack. That is nine days out and I am not shortening it.”

“So what have you got.”

Iris looked at the column on the screen. Four hundred and eleven lines, sorted, complete, and anonymous from the top of it to the bottom.

“Someone’s whole course of treatment,” she said. “From the first day. Filed under a number.”

Kade was quiet for a second.

“All right,” he said. “Get the number.”
