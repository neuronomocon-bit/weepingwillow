Chapter 5 — Kade
Kade’s office was on the second floor of a building that had been something else before. The exterior still carried the old signage—faded lettering half-removed, adhesive outlines left behind. Inside, it had been stripped down to function.
No reception. No front desk.
A door with a keypad that worked.
Iris entered without knocking.
The room was longer than it was wide. One wall held a series of pinned maps—Lowport and the surrounding coastline. Not decorative. Some printed, some hand-marked. Colored tags. Thin lines drawn between locations. Notes in tight handwriting.
The opposite wall was bare except for shelving. Boxes. Labeled, but not neatly. Dates. Site codes. Some crossed out.
A single desk sat near the center, angled slightly away from the maps. Not positioned for conversation. Positioned to see everything else.
Kade was standing when she came in. He didn’t look up immediately. He was finishing something—writing, not typing. Pen moving steadily across a page. He capped it, set it down, then looked at her.
“You’re early.”
“Traffic was clear.”
He nodded once. Accepted.
There was a chair across from the desk. She didn’t sit until he did.
He reached to the side, pulled a folder from a stack, and set it between them without opening it.
“MN-44-7,” he said.
She watched him.
“That’s the Meridian site,” he added. “Your job.”
“I know.”
He held her gaze for a moment longer than necessary. Not challenging. Measuring.
“You pulled the data,” he said.
“Yes.”
“It was intact.”
“Yes.”
He nodded again. Then:
“Anything else?”
The question sat there.
Iris considered it.
“There were gaps,” she said.
“In the data?”
“No.”
He didn’t react immediately. The pause was small. Measured.
“In what, then?”
“My timeline,” she said. “Entry to exit.”
He leaned back slightly in his chair. Not surprised. Not dismissive. Filing it.
“How long?”
“Thirty-four minutes.”
“You were inside for three hours.”
“I know.”
He picked up the folder, opened it, flipped once, then closed it again.
“You didn’t log an incident.”
“No.”
“Did you experience one?”
She looked at the maps behind him instead of answering.
There were more markers than she expected.
Clusters.
Not random.
“Yes,” she said.
“What kind?”
She tried to reconstruct it.
There was a shape where the memory should have been. Not the content. Just the outline.
“I don’t know.”
“That’s not an answer.”
“It’s accurate.”
He watched her again. Longer this time.
“You don’t remember.”
“No.”
“And you’re comfortable with that.”
She thought about that. The word.
Comfortable.
“I recognize it,” she said.
“That’s not what I asked.”
“No.”
The silence held for a few seconds. Not tense. Not easy.
Kade shifted his attention back to the desk. He moved the folder slightly, aligning it with the edge.
“Meridian didn’t shut down clean,” he said.
“I assumed that.”
“Don’t assume. It creates problems.”
She nodded once.
“They had multiple sites,” he continued. “Primary facility on the coast. Secondary storage. Offsite data mirrors. Some of it flagged for retrieval before closure.”
“How many.”
He hesitated.
“Enough.”
“That’s not a number.”
“It’s not one.”
She let that sit.
He reached behind him, pulled one of the maps free from the wall, and set it flat on the desk between them.
Lowport. The coastline. Industrial zones marked in heavier lines.
He tapped one location. Then another. Then a third.
“These were all tied to Meridian,” he said. “Logistics, storage, support.”
“Operational.”
“Not directly.”
“Then what.”
“Overflow,” he said. “Redundancy. Things they didn’t want centralized.”
She followed the spacing between the points. Not evenly distributed. Not clustered in a way that suggested efficiency.
Spread.
“Why separate them like this,” she asked.
“Risk management.”
“From what.”
He didn’t answer that immediately.
“From themselves,” he said.
The words landed flat. Not emphasized.
Iris looked back at the map.
“How many are still active,” she asked.
“None.”
“You’re sure.”
“Yes.”
“You checked them.”
“Yes.”
“When.”
“After shutdown.”
“That was years ago.”
“Yes.”
She waited.
He didn’t continue.
She looked at the markers again.
“Then why are we here,” she said, “if everything is inactive.”
He didn’t respond right away.
Instead, he stood, walked back to the wall, and pulled down a second map. This one had fewer markings. Newer paper.
He placed it over the first.
Different points.
Some overlapping. Some not.
“These are recent,” he said.
“From what.”
“Reports.”
“Of what.”
He met her eyes again.
“Behavioral anomalies.”
The phrase was clean. Clinical.
“Medical,” she asked.
“Mostly.”
“Diagnosed.”
“Attempted.”
She thought about Joel. The way he spoke. The way he didn’t.
“These are near the Meridian sites,” she said.
“Some of them.”
“Not all.”
“No.”
“Then it’s not a direct correlation.”
“No.”
She traced one point with her finger. A location she recognized.
“That one,” she said. “North side.”
He nodded.
“K-01,” he said.
“Joel.”
“You’ve seen him.”
“Yes.”
“And?”
She considered how to answer.
“He’s functional,” she said.
“That’s not what I asked.”
“It’s what matters.”
He waited.
“There’s less,” she added.
“Less what.”
She didn’t answer immediately.
She was looking at the space between the markers now. Not the points themselves.
“Something has been removed,” she said.
Kade didn’t react.
Not visibly.
But he didn’t interrupt either.
“From him,” she continued. “From Lila Mercer.”
“You saw her.”
“Yes.”
“And you’re making that conclusion from two cases.”
“I’m making that conclusion from what’s left.”
He leaned forward slightly, elbows on the desk.
“That’s not evidence.”
“No.”
“Then what is it?”
“Pattern recognition.”
“That’s still not evidence.”
“No.”
He sat back again.
“You’re drawing a line between Meridian and a set of unrelated behavioral cases,” he said.
“They’re not unrelated.”
“Based on what?”
“Proximity. Timing. Consistency.”
He glanced at the map again.
“Consistency how?”
“The way they answer,” she said. “Not what they say. How it stops.”
“Examples.”
“I know what is necessary,” she said. “It was appropriate.”
He didn’t write it down.
“I don’t have that information,” she added. “I respond when required.”
Kade was still.
“Those aren’t medical responses,” she said.
“No,” he agreed.
“They’re… constrained.”
He considered that word.
“Constrained how?”
She tried to define it.
“They’re correct,” she said. “But they don’t extend.”
“Meaning.”
“They answer the question. Nothing beyond it.”
“That’s not unusual.”
“No.”
“But it’s consistent.”
He looked at her.
“And you think Meridian caused that.”
“I think it’s connected.”
“That’s not the same thing.”
“No.”
The room settled again.
Kade stood, walked back to the wall, and replaced the maps carefully.
When he turned back, his attention stayed on her longer than before.
Not on what she was saying.
On her.
“You’re different,” he said.
She didn’t respond.
“Before Meridian,” he continued, “you would have pushed harder by now.”
“On what.”
“Everything.”
She thought about that.
It felt correct.
“I have the information I need,” she said.
“That’s not how you used to work.”
“No.”
“Then how do you work now?”
She considered the question.
“More efficiently.”
He held her gaze.
“That’s not what I meant.”
“I know.”
He moved back to the desk, picked up the original folder, and opened it fully this time. Inside were multiple tabs. Site codes. Coordinates. Notes.
He slid it toward her.
“There are six locations,” he said. “Confirmed Meridian-adjacent. Not the primary facility.”
She looked down at the pages.
“Data retrieval,” she asked.
“Some.”
“What else.”
“Verification,” he said. “Status. Anything left behind.”
“Define left behind.”
He hesitated again.
“Anything that shouldn’t be there,” he said.
“That’s not specific.”
“It’s what I have.”
She scanned the first page.
Coordinates. Access notes. Structural condition.
“You’ve been to these,” she said.
“Yes.”
“And they were empty.”
“Yes.”
“When.”
He didn’t answer immediately.
“Then,” he said.
She looked up.
“Then isn’t now.”
“No.”
He watched her for a second.
“You’re still going to do it,” he said.
“Yes.”
“You don’t know what you’re looking for.”
“No.”
“You don’t know what happened at Meridian.”
“No.”
“You don’t remember your own incident.”
“No.”
He nodded once.
“Good,” he said.
She didn’t ask why.
He stepped back from the desk, giving her space to review the file.
“Same terms as before,” he said. “Access, retrieval, no questions that get written down.”
She closed the folder.
“I have a question,” she said.
He waited.
“Why me.”
“You were already inside,” he said.
“That’s not a reason.”
“It is.”
“It’s not sufficient.”
He considered that.
“You came back out,” he said.
She held his gaze.
“So did the data.”
“That’s not what I meant.”
He didn’t expand.
She stood.
The chair made a small sound against the floor. It felt louder than it was.
She picked up the folder.
“I’ll start with the closest site,” she said.
“Send me a log when you’re done.”
“I will.”
She turned toward the door.
“K-01,” he said.
She stopped.
“Keep an eye on him,” he added. “If anything changes.”
“It will,” she said.
“How do you know?”
She thought about Joel’s voice. The way it ended exactly where it needed to.
“It’s moving,” she said.
He didn’t ask what she meant.
“Send the log,” he repeated.
She nodded once and opened the door.
The hallway outside was empty. Same as before.
She stepped out, then paused.
Not because of anything she felt.
Because she recognized the moment where something should be.
Concern. Hesitation. Something directed back into the room she was leaving.
There was nothing there.
She noted it.
Then she left.

***

Kade remained standing for a few seconds after the door closed.
He didn’t move immediately.
Then he reached for the map again. The newer one.
He looked at the points she had traced.
Then at the others.
His hand hovered over one of the markers, not touching it.
He pulled it free.
Set it down on the desk.
Adjusted it slightly.
Then stopped.
He looked toward the door.
Like he was trying to remember something about her.
Something specific.
It didn’t come.
He turned back to the map and pinned the marker back in place.
Aligned with the others.
Then he moved on.
