The story, without the ending

Iris Vale recovers data from places nobody else will go. Her client, a former logistics coordinator named Daniel Kade, sends her into the abandoned Meridian Neurological lab on the Lowport coast. The job is routine, and she comes out with the data. She does not come out with thirty-four minutes of her afternoon. On the drive home she finds a photo of her younger sister, Lena, and feels nothing at all.

Something in that building took one thing from her: not her memory of Lena, just the love. Then Iris starts to see the same absence in other people across Lowport, calm and polite and hollow, filed by the city’s doctors as a treatable condition. When she maps the cases over time they are not scattered. They move in a line, along the old Meridian infrastructure Kade once helped build, and the closer Iris gets to whatever is making it, the more of herself she loses.

Who connects to whom

The cast

Iris Vale

Protagonist · data recovery

A data-recovery tech who takes jobs inside sealed buildings nobody else will touch. After Meridian, the one thing taken from her is her love for her younger sister, Lena. She still remembers Lena and knows she should care. She doesn't.

The damage keeps spreading: late reactions, bad calls, false certainties. Because her Taking was interrupted, she can sense Willow nearby, which makes her the only one who can track it. Every step closer costs her more.

Willow

The entity

The thing Iris meets inside Meridian. Almost human, with a face that looks learned rather than born. It takes by touch, and the feeling drains out of a person's memories. Nothing it does looks cruel, and it never speaks.

Lena Vale

Iris's younger sister

Warm, direct and persistent. She won't accept that her sister has changed and keeps fighting for the relationship. She's the emotional core of the book because she's exactly what's missing.

Caleb Ward

Field tech · old friend

A tech Iris has worked with for years: sharp, funny, a little reckless, and one of the few who knew her before. He notices she's changed and says so. At the midpoint he is Taken, in the one chapter told from his point of view.

Daniel Kade

Client · ally

A former Meridian logistics coordinator who hires Iris and keeps feeding her sites. Practical and controlled. He moved Meridian's crates and data without ever asking what was in them.

Dr. Elena Rourke

The skeptic

Lowport's medical authority on the cases, with hundreds of patients and no recoveries. She's right about the symptoms and wrong about the cause, and she shares her files with Iris anyway.

Lila Mercer

The first Taken person Iris meets

Calm, polite and completely hollow. She sets the tone for the whole book: no drama, only absence.

Joel & Leah

The first case

Joel disappears for two days and comes back fine, with nothing behind his answers. His wife Leah is frightened, a smaller version of what Lena is living with.

The world

Lowport, Maine, around 2050

A fictional coastal city past its peak: shipyards, fog, salt, and old tunnels under everything. The technology is near-future, but the street would still feel like ours.

Meridian Neurological

A sealed research building on the coast, built more like a bunker than a clinic. The book never says what was done inside it, and it did not shut down cleanly.

Glossary

Willow
What the book calls the entity.
The Taking / the Taken
Willow’s touch, and the people it has touched. The Taken stay calm and functional, and something is gone.
Expected / Observed
Iris’s self-logging, the way a technician logs a system: what she expected to feel, and what she observed.
Proximity sense
Pressure in Iris’s head when Willow is near. Because her Taking was interrupted, she is the only one who can track it.
Question → Answer → Stop
How every conversation with a Taken person goes: a question, an answer, and then nothing more.
Periods on questions
Iris and the Taken end spoken questions with a period, not a question mark. Flat affect, built into the punctuation.

Themes

  • Horror by subtraction. Nothing explodes. Willow doesn't add anything or distort anything, it removes, and what's left is functional, calm and incomplete. The horror is in the gap.
  • Loss isn't always visible. People keep walking, talking and working, and most of the world can't see what's gone. The city absorbs the damage.
  • Identity is fragile. A person isn't just their memories or just their emotions but the connection between the two, and that connection can be interrupted.
  • Pain has purpose. Grief anchors us. Take away the pain and the meaning goes with it. Is suffering part of being human, or just damage?
  • You can survive and still be gone. Iris lives, stays aware and keeps working, and becomes progressively less herself.

Influences

Roughly in the order they arrived: picture books and childhood, then fantasy, then cyberpunk and science fiction about memory, machines and what makes a person real.

Mercer Mayer

Picture books

The Little Critter books and There's a Nightmare in My Closet.

Judy Blume

Middle grade

Tales of a Fourth Grade Nothing, Superfudge, Are You There God? It's Me, Margaret. Books that took kids' inner lives seriously.

Bill Basso · The Top of the Pizzas

Picture book · 1977

Ogre, a pizzeria cook kept hidden in the kitchen because of how he looks, finds his place on a rooftop full of gargoyles.

J.R.R. Tolkien · The Hobbit

The read-along of the 1977 animated film. An ordinary person pulled out of a quiet life into something much bigger.

Middle · Fantasy

Drizzt Do'Urden, a dark elf who walks away from everything his people are.

A band of friends in a world the gods have left, with Raistlin at the center: a brilliant mage who trades pieces of his humanity for power.

A burned-out hacker takes one last job for an artificial intelligence trying to slip its limits. The book that defined cyberpunk.

Philip K. Dick

Blade Runner · Total Recall

The writer behind both films' source stories, which ask whether your memories are what make you real.

The Wachowskis · The Matrix

A world that feels complete and isn't, and people who don't notice what's been taken from them.

On using AI

Weeping Willow is my idea: the world, the characters, the story, and every decision about what happens and why. I’m a new author, and I come at a book the way I come at a song. It starts with an idea, and then I build it out. AI is a tool in that process. It helps me keep the outline and storylines straight across books, check continuity and facts, and get drafts of chapters onto the page from my direction, which I then review and edit. I use it the same way at work, to keep projects organized and moving. It’s like being a songwriter who also produces the record: the vision, the arrangement and the final call on every take are mine. In my opinion, AI can help creative people bring their ideas into reality with less friction. Less friction, not less work. It doesn't decide what the story is. That part is mine.Kristopher Michael
